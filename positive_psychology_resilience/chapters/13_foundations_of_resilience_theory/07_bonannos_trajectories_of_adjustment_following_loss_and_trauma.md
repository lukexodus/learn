## Bonanno's Trajectories of Adjustment Following Loss and Trauma


### Overview

George Bonanno's trajectory research represents one of the most influential empirical and methodological contributions to contemporary resilience science, particularly within the study of bereavement and potentially traumatic events (PTEs). Rather than assuming a uniform population response to loss or trauma (e.g., an assumption that most people require extended time and/or intervention to "work through" grief or trauma), Bonanno's research programmatically identified multiple distinct, empirically derived trajectories of post-event adjustment, with resilience — defined narrowly as stable, healthy functioning with minimal disruption — emerging as the single most common trajectory across numerous studied populations and event types. This finding substantially reshaped clinical and research assumptions about "normal" responses to loss and trauma.

### Methodological Innovation: Trajectory-Based Analysis

**Departure from Prior Assumptions**

Prior to Bonanno's work, much bereavement and trauma research implicitly or explicitly assumed a relatively uniform population trajectory — that most individuals exposed to loss or potentially traumatic events would experience significant initial distress, requiring time (and sometimes formal intervention) to gradually process and recover. Bonanno's key methodological contribution was to apply prospective, repeated-measures longitudinal designs combined with person-centered statistical techniques (notably latent growth mixture modeling) to empirically test whether the population actually showed this single uniform pattern, or whether multiple distinct subgroup trajectories existed.

**Person-Centered vs. Variable-Centered Analysis**

This approach exemplifies a "person-centered" analytic strategy (identifying qualitatively distinct subgroups of individuals following different patterns) as opposed to a "variable-centered" strategy (assuming a single population-average trajectory and studying variables that predict individual deviation from that average) — a methodological distinction with substantial implications for how research findings are interpreted and applied.

### The Four (or More) Canonical Trajectories

Bonanno's research, across studies of bereavement, disaster exposure, combat exposure, spinal cord injury, and other potentially traumatic events, has repeatedly identified a recurring set of trajectory patterns:

| Trajectory | Pattern Description | Approximate Relative Prevalence (Illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resilience | Stable, healthy functioning maintained throughout, with minimal or transient disruption following the event | Frequently the most common trajectory across studied samples |
| Recovery | Significant initial disruption to functioning, followed by gradual return to baseline over the following months to roughly a year or two | A substantial minority in most samples |
| Chronic dysfunction/distress | Significant, sustained disruption to functioning that does not substantially improve over the observed follow-up period | A smaller but clinically significant minority |
| Delayed dysfunction/reactivation | Functioning initially appears stable, but deteriorates significantly at a later point following the event | The least common trajectory in most studies, and empirically the most contested/debated in terms of prevalence and even existence as a distinct pattern |

**[Unverified]** Exact prevalence percentages for each trajectory vary considerably across specific studies, populations, event types, and follow-up durations; Bonanno's own publications report differing specific percentages across different studies, so this table intentionally presents relative/qualitative prevalence patterns (resilience trajectory generally most common) rather than citing a single universal percentage figure that would misleadingly imply a fixed number applicable across all contexts.

### Key Empirical Findings and Their Significance

**Resilience as the Modal (Most Common) Response**

Perhaps Bonanno's most influential and, at the time, most counterintuitive finding is that the resilience trajectory — stable, healthy functioning with minimal disruption — is typically the single most common pattern observed following bereavement and many types of potentially traumatic events, rather than being a rare exceptional outcome as prior clinical assumptions and popular understanding often implied. This finding parallels and reinforces Masten's "ordinary magic" thesis (covered previously) in emphasizing that positive adaptation following adversity is common rather than rare, though Bonanno's trajectory-based demonstration approaches this claim through a distinct methodological route (prospective trajectory modeling around discrete traumatic/loss events) compared to Masten's broader developmental systems framework.

**Distinguishing Resilience from Recovery (Terminological Note, Recap)**

As introduced in the earlier "Related Constructs" topic, Bonanno's framework treats "resilience" and "recovery" as two empirically and conceptually distinct trajectories — resilience denotes stable functioning with minimal disruption, while recovery denotes a significant dip followed by gradual return to baseline. This is an important terminological distinction from the broader trait/process/outcome resilience framework (from the "Defining Resilience" topic), which would potentially classify both trajectory patterns as instances of positive long-run adaptation.

**Absence of "Necessary" Grief Work**

Bonanno's findings challenged the historically influential assumption (associated with certain psychodynamic and stage-based grief theories) that active "grief work" — extensive emotional processing and working-through of loss — is a necessary precondition for healthy long-term adjustment. The empirical prevalence of the resilience trajectory (minimal disruption, without extensive visible grief processing) suggested that healthy adjustment does not require a uniform grief-work process for all individuals, a finding with significant implications for clinical practice and expectations around "normal" grief.

### Predictors of Trajectory Membership

Bonanno's and related research programs have examined predictors that distinguish which trajectory a given individual is more likely to follow, including:

| Predictor Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-event characteristics | Prior mental health history, personality factors (including some overlap with hardiness-related appraisal styles), prior trauma exposure |
| Event characteristics | Severity, suddenness, controllability, and type of the loss or traumatic event |
| Coping and appraisal style | Flexible coping ("coping flexibility"), positive emotion regulation capacity, meaning-making capacity |
| Social and contextual resources | Social support availability, financial/material resources, community resources |

**Coping Flexibility**

A specific concept associated with Bonanno's broader research program is coping flexibility — the capacity to adaptively select and, when needed, shift between different coping strategies depending on situational demands, as opposed to rigidly applying a single coping style regardless of context. This concept connects Bonanno's trajectory research back to the broader coping literature (Lazarus and Folkman) covered in the "Related Constructs" topic, while extending it with a specific emphasis on flexibility/adaptability as a predictor of resilient trajectory membership.

### Clinical and Applied Implications

1. **Recalibrating clinical expectations**: Recognizing that resilience is often the modal response to loss and trauma has implications for grief counseling and trauma-informed care practice — it supports a stepped-care or watchful-waiting approach for many individuals (rather than assuming universal need for intensive intervention), while still ensuring appropriate support is available and accessible for the substantial minority following chronic-dysfunction or delayed-dysfunction trajectories. **[Inference]**
2. **Avoiding pathologizing normal resilient responses**: Bonanno's work has been cited as a corrective against inadvertently pathologizing individuals who do not display extensive visible distress following loss or trauma, since such minimal-disruption responses are common and not evidence of denial, suppression, or delayed/impending problems (though the delayed-dysfunction trajectory's existence means this reassurance is not absolute or universally guaranteed for every individual). **[Inference]**
3. **Targeting resources toward genuinely at-risk trajectories**: Trajectory-based thinking supports more efficient allocation of limited clinical and support resources toward individuals showing early signs consistent with chronic-dysfunction or delayed-dysfunction patterns, rather than universal intensive intervention for all individuals exposed to a given type of loss or trauma.
4. **Caution regarding early trajectory prediction**: While predictors of trajectory membership exist, **[Inference]** confidently classifying a specific individual's future trajectory very early after an event remains an inexact endeavor given the probabilistic (not deterministic) nature of the predictor research; premature trajectory labeling risks both under-supporting someone who will need more help and over-pathologizing someone on a resilient course.

### Relationship to Other Chapter Concepts

**[Inference]** Bonanno's trajectory research complements and partially operationalizes several previously covered concepts: it provides empirical, quantitative trajectory evidence supporting the general dynamic-process view of resilience covered in the prior topic (demonstrating multiple distinct real trajectory shapes rather than a single average pattern); it offers a more discrete-event-focused, clinically applied counterpart to Masten's broader, more developmentally continuous ordinary magic framework; and its coping-flexibility construct extends the general coping literature covered in the "Related Constructs" topic with a specific resilience-relevant refinement.

### Common Points of Confusion

- **Trajectory "resilience" vs. broader trait/process/outcome resilience**: As emphasized throughout, Bonanno's specific technical use of "resilience" (denoting one specific trajectory shape: minimal disruption) is narrower than, and should not be casually equated with, the broader resilience framework covered in the "Defining Resilience" topic, which could potentially encompass both the resilience and recovery trajectories as instances of positive long-term adaptation.
- **The delayed-dysfunction trajectory's contested status**: Unlike the other three trajectories, which are relatively consistently identified across Bonanno's and related studies, the delayed-dysfunction/reactivation trajectory has been subject to more methodological debate regarding its true prevalence and even its existence as a genuinely distinct pattern versus a statistical artifact of measurement timing or sample characteristics in some studies. **[Unverified — contested]**

**Key Points**

- Bonanno's trajectory research used prospective, person-centered longitudinal methods to empirically identify multiple distinct patterns of post-loss/trauma adjustment, challenging prior assumptions of a uniform population response requiring extended grief work.
- The resilience trajectory (minimal disruption, stable functioning) is typically the most common pattern across studied populations and event types — a finding that reinforces, via a distinct methodological route, Masten's broader "ordinary magic" thesis that positive adaptation is common rather than rare.
- Bonanno's specific, technical use of "resilience" as one trajectory among several (distinct from "recovery") is narrower than the broader trait/process/outcome resilience framework and should be carefully distinguished in academic writing and research design.
- Coping flexibility — adaptively shifting coping strategies to match situational demands — is a key predictor construct associated with Bonanno's research program, connecting trajectory research back to the broader coping literature.
