## Multisystemic and Ecological Models of Resilience


### Overview

Multisystemic and ecological models of resilience formalize the insight, foundational to Masten's "ordinary magic" theory, that resilience cannot be adequately understood at the level of the individual alone. These models explicitly conceptualize resilience as emerging from dynamic interactions across nested and interacting systems — biological, psychological, family, community, institutional, and cultural — drawing heavily on general ecological systems theory in developmental psychology, most notably Urie Bronfenbrenner's bioecological model. This topic examines the structure of these models, their theoretical lineage, and their methodological and applied implications.

### Theoretical Lineage: Bronfenbrenner's Bioecological Model

**Core Structure**

Urie Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory (originally proposed in the late 1970s and refined into the "bioecological model" through the 1990s) organizes an individual's developmental environment into nested, mutually influencing system levels:

| System Level | Description | Resilience-Relevant Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microsystem | Immediate settings with direct contact (family, school, peer group) | Parent-child relationship quality, classroom climate |
| Mesosystem | Interactions *between* microsystems | Communication between home and school (e.g., parent-teacher relationship) |
| Exosystem | Settings that indirectly affect the individual without their direct participation | A parent's workplace stress affecting home environment |
| Macrosystem | Broad cultural, ideological, and institutional context | Cultural attitudes toward child-rearing, national social welfare policy |
| Chronosystem | The dimension of time — how environmental events and transitions unfold across a person's life course | Timing of a divorce, a war, or an economic recession relative to developmental stage |

**Application to Resilience**

Resilience researchers adapted this general ecological framework to specifically model how protective and risk factors at each system level interact to shape developmental outcomes following adversity, rather than searching only for individual-level trait explanations. This adaptation is central to what distinguishes Masten's mature theoretical model (and similar multisystemic resilience frameworks) from earlier, more individually focused resilience research.

### Multisystemic Resilience Model Structure

**Core Organizing Principle**

Multisystemic resilience models organize protective processes across levels that roughly parallel Bronfenbrenner's structure, though resilience-specific formulations (e.g., Masten's) often use somewhat different level labels oriented specifically around adaptive systems rather than generic ecological settings:

| Level | Example Protective Processes |
| --- | --- |
| Biological/neurobiological | Genetic factors moderating stress reactivity; HPA axis regulation; neuroplasticity supporting recovery of function |
| Individual/psychological | Cognitive appraisal style, self-regulation capacity, problem-solving skills, sense of self-efficacy |
| Relational/family | Secure attachment, authoritative parenting, family cohesion and communication |
| Peer/social network | Positive peer relationships, prosocial peer group affiliation |
| Institutional (school, workplace, healthcare) | Effective schools, supportive workplace policies, accessible healthcare systems |
| Community | Community cohesion, informal social control, availability of community resources and organizations |
| Cultural/societal | Shared meaning-making systems, cultural identity resources, societal-level social policy and economic conditions |

**Cross-Level Interaction, Not Simple Addition**

A defining feature of genuinely multisystemic models (as opposed to simply listing protective factors at different levels) is the emphasis on *interaction* across levels — for example, a strong family-level protective factor (secure attachment) may partially buffer against the effects of a community-level risk factor (neighborhood violence exposure), while a strong community-level resource (a well-functioning school with extended support services) may partially compensate for family-level risk (parental unavailability due to multiple jobs). **[Inference]** The precise mathematical/statistical form of these cross-level interactions (additive, multiplicative, threshold-based) is an active area of methodological development in resilience research and is not fully standardized across studies; different researchers use different statistical interaction models depending on their specific theoretical assumptions and available data structure.

### Developmental Cascade Models

**Definition**

Developmental cascade models, closely associated with Masten's later theoretical work, describe how functioning (positive or negative) in one domain at one developmental period can propagate — or "cascade" — to affect functioning in other domains at later developmental periods, creating compounding or amplifying effects over time.

**Illustrative Cascade Example**

A commonly cited cascade pattern (illustrative, drawing on general patterns described in the developmental cascade literature) proceeds roughly as follows: early self-regulation difficulties can lead to early academic struggles, which can contribute to peer rejection or social difficulties, which can in turn increase risk for later externalizing or internalizing problems, which can further impair academic and occupational functioning in adolescence and early adulthood — with each domain's difficulties amplifying risk in subsequent domains, rather than each risk factor operating independently and additively.

**Implications for Multisystemic Models**

Cascade models add a crucial temporal, dynamic dimension to multisystemic resilience frameworks: protective or risk processes operating at a given system level at one point in development do not have static, isolated effects — they can propagate forward in time and across domains, meaning early intervention at any given level can have effects that compound favorably across multiple later domains (or, conversely, early risk in one domain can compound unfavorably across later domains if not addressed).

### Methodological Approaches to Studying Multisystemic Resilience

| Approach | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Multilevel/hierarchical modeling | Statistical modeling of individuals nested within families, schools, or communities, allowing simultaneous estimation of effects at multiple levels |
| Longitudinal/growth curve modeling | Tracking trajectories of functioning over time to identify cascade patterns and turning points |
| Person-centered approaches (e.g., latent class/profile analysis) | Identifying subgroups of individuals with distinct patterns of risk and protective factor combinations, rather than assuming uniform linear effects across a population |
| Mixed-methods/community-based participatory research | Particularly common in community-level and cultural resilience research, combining quantitative multilevel data with qualitative understanding of local context and meaning-making |

**[Inference]** No single methodological approach fully captures the theoretical complexity of multisystemic models; most published multisystemic resilience research necessarily focuses on a subset of levels and interactions (e.g., family and individual levels, with community-level factors treated as covariates) rather than comprehensively modeling every level Bronfenbrenner's original framework specifies, reflecting practical data-collection and modeling constraints rather than a theoretical limitation of the models themselves.

### Extension to Community and Population-Level Resilience

Multisystemic thinking extends naturally beyond individual development to community-level and population-level resilience research, particularly in disaster, conflict, and public health contexts:

- **Community resilience frameworks** examine how community-level systems (social cohesion, institutional capacity, economic diversity, infrastructure robustness) collectively shape a community's capacity to withstand and recover from collective adversities (natural disasters, economic shocks, pandemics).
- **Cross-level linkage**: Individual-level resilience and community-level resilience are theorized to be mutually reinforcing — individuals draw on community resources as protective factors, while resilient individuals (with functioning self-regulation, social skills, and community engagement) contribute to overall community functioning and cohesion, creating a bidirectional, multisystemic relationship rather than a simple one-directional individual-to-community or community-to-individual causal arrow. **[Inference]**

### Practical/Applied Implications

1. **Intervention point flexibility**: Because multisystemic models identify protective processes across many levels, interventions need not target every level simultaneously to be effective — strengthening protective factors at any accessible level (e.g., a school-based program, a family-strengthening program, or a community infrastructure investment) can plausibly shift outcomes, though effects may be larger when multiple levels are addressed in coordination.
2. **Avoiding single-level reductionism**: Multisystemic models caution against interventions or policies that address only the individual level (e.g., individual skill-building programs) while ignoring family, community, or structural-level risk factors that may be driving much of the observed adversity exposure in the first place — a critique that connects to the broader critical/community psychology concerns raised regarding individual-focused positive psychology interventions (see the limitations topic).
3. **Policy relevance**: The inclusion of macrosystem and exosystem levels explicitly connects resilience science to social policy domains (economic policy, healthcare access, housing policy) that are traditionally outside the scope of individual-focused psychological intervention, providing a theoretical bridge between psychological resilience research and public health/social policy fields.

### Comparison: Individual-Level vs. Multisystemic Resilience Models

| Dimension | Individual-Level (Earlier) Models | Multisystemic/Ecological Models |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit of analysis | The individual and their traits/processes | Individual nested within family, community, culture |
| Typical intervention target | Individual skill-building, cognitive/behavioral change | Multiple levels: individual, family, institutional, community, policy |
| Explanation for variable outcomes | Individual differences in traits or coping | Interaction of protective/risk factors across levels, plus cascade effects over time |
| Relationship to policy | Limited direct connection | Explicit connection via macrosystem/exosystem levels |

**Key Points**

- Multisystemic resilience models extend Masten's ordinary magic thesis by formally organizing protective processes across nested ecological levels, drawing directly on Bronfenbrenner's bioecological model.
- Developmental cascade models add a crucial temporal dimension, showing how functioning in one domain can propagate to affect other domains over time, rather than treating risk and protective factors as static, isolated, additive influences.
- Genuine multisystemic models emphasize cross-level interaction (buffering and compounding effects across levels), not merely a list of protective factors organized by level.
- These models extend naturally to community and population-level resilience research (disasters, conflict, pandemics), and directly support policy-level as well as individual-level intervention approaches.
