## World Bank Environmental and Social Framework's ESS10 on stakeholder engagement

### Overview and Regulatory Context

Environmental and Social Standard 10 (ESS10), *Stakeholder Engagement and Information Disclosure*, is one of the ten Environmental and Social Standards within the World Bank's 2016 Environmental and Social Framework (ESF), which applies to World Bank Investment Project Financing (IPF). ESS10 applies to all projects supported by the Bank through Investment Project Financing, and the Borrower will engage with stakeholders as an integral part of the project's environmental and social assessment and management throughout the project life-cycle.

ESS10 recognizes the importance of open and transparent engagement between the Borrower and project stakeholders as an essential element of good international practice. Effective stakeholder engagement can improve the environmental and social sustainability of projects, enhance project acceptance, and make a significant contribution to successful project design and implementation.

ESS10 functions as the World Bank framework's structural counterpart to IFC's PS1 community engagement pillar: both institutions converge on the same underlying principle even though they use different governance architectures. The International Finance Corporation makes a similar point in its Performance Standard 1, stating that stakeholder engagement is the basis for building strong, constructive, and responsive relationships that are essential for the successful management of a project's environmental and social impacts.

### Objectives

ESS10's Guidance Note frames the standard's objectives around four core functions:

1. To establish a systematic approach to stakeholder engagement that will help Borrowers identify stakeholders and build and maintain a constructive relationship with them, in particular project-affected parties.
2. To assess the level of stakeholder interest and support for the project and to enable stakeholders' views to be taken into account in project design and environmental and social performance.
3. To ensure that appropriate project information on environmental and social risks and impacts is disclosed to stakeholders in a timely, understandable, accessible, and appropriate manner and format.
4. To provide project-affected parties with accessible and inclusive means to raise issues and grievances, and allow Borrowers to respond to and manage such grievances.

### Proportionality Principle

ESS10 embeds a risk-proportional engagement intensity requirement, aligning engagement scale to project risk classification. The level and frequency of stakeholder engagement conducted is proportionate to the project's risks and impacts.

- Projects with high and substantial environmental and social risk should include several formal points of engagement, as well as ongoing information dissemination throughout the project cycle.
- Projects with moderate and low risk should include several points of engagement before the project is approved by the Bank, and ideally more than one point of engagement during project implementation.

### Stakeholder Categorization

The ESS10 template distinguishes stakeholder categories that shape engagement design, methods, and prioritization:

**Affected parties** — individuals, groups, and local communities who are directly or indirectly affected by the project, positively or negatively, and who need to be identified and engaged with as part of the environmental and social assessment process.

**Other interested parties** — individuals, groups, or organizations with an interest in the project that may or may not be directly affected by it. Depending on the nature and scope of the project and its potential risks and impacts, examples of other potential stakeholders may include government authorities, local organizations, NGOs, and companies, and nearby communities. Stakeholders may also include politicians, labor unions, academics, religious groups, national social and environmental public-sector agencies, and the media.

**Disadvantaged/vulnerable individuals or groups** — those who may be disproportionately affected by the project or further disadvantaged in the process, requiring differentiated engagement approaches (special measures such as separate consultation formats, alternative communication channels, or targeted outreach).

### Information Disclosure Requirements

ESS10 sets specific disclosure timing and content obligations that precede consultation. The Borrower will disclose project information to allow stakeholders to understand the risks and impacts of the project, and potential opportunities. The Borrower will provide stakeholders with access to relevant information as early as possible before the Bank proceeds to project appraisal, and in a time frame that enables meaningful consultations with stakeholders on project design.

Disclosure obligations continue beyond project approval and into implementation. Engagement after the Bank approves a project should include disclosure of information on how stakeholder feedback was incorporated into project design and environmental and social assessment documentation, alongside ongoing stakeholder consultations on implementation and supervision issues.

Mandatory disclosure of relevant project documentation is addressed within ESS10's core provisions, and this disclosure requirement interacts with the Bank's broader Access to Information Policy.

### The Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP)

The SEP is ESS10's principal implementation instrument. The Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP) should be clear and concise and focus on describing the project and identifying its stakeholders — it is key to identify what information will be in the public domain, in what languages, and where it will be made available.

**Structural flexibility:** For lower-risk projects, elements of an SEP may be included as part of the Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP), and preparation of a stand-alone SEP may not be necessary — reflecting ESS10's proportionality principle applied to documentation itself, not only to engagement intensity.

**Pre-appraisal timing challenge:** In cases where specific project details are not yet known at the time the SEP is prepared, the framework sets out the principles to be followed for stakeholder engagement in a manner that is consistent with ESS10 and indicates how people are notified as key information becomes known; the SEP is updated as and when specific details become known — for example, the specific locations, stakeholders, and schedule of engagement activities.

**Supplemental SEPs for retrofitted engagement:** In cases where the Bank's involvement starts after the Borrower has already initiated a project, the Bank will work with the Borrower to review the stakeholder engagement provisions in place and, if necessary, to develop and implement a supplemental Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP) to ensure adequate stakeholder engagement going forward.

### Grievance Redress Mechanism (GRM)

ESS10 requires that project-affected parties be provided with accessible and inclusive means to raise issues and grievances, and that Borrowers respond to and manage such grievances. This is documented alongside the SEP, and supporting Bank resources include a dedicated Grievance Redress Mechanism Checklist among the templates and tools associated with ESF implementation.

### Documentation of Engagement Activities

ESS10 imposes structured record-keeping requirements to create an auditable trail of consultation activity. Documentation of stakeholder engagement includes, as appropriate:

- Date and location of each meeting, with a copy of the notification to stakeholders.
- The purpose of the engagement (for example, to inform stakeholders of an intended project or to gather their views on potential environmental and social impacts).
- The form of engagement and consultation (for example, face-to-face meetings such as town halls or workshops, focus groups, written consultations, online consultations).
- Number of participants and categories of participants.
- Summary of main points and concerns raised by stakeholders.

### Interaction with Other Environmental and Social Standards

ESS10 operates as the general engagement standard, but several other ESSs impose heightened, topic-specific disclosure and consultation requirements that operate in addition to ESS10's baseline. Projects must also apply the special disclosure and consultation requirements set out in ESS5, ESS7, and ESS8 — meaning practitioners should treat ESS10 as a floor rather than a ceiling for engagement rigor when a project also triggers land acquisition/resettlement (ESS5), Indigenous Peoples/Sub-Saharan African Historically Underserved Traditional Local Communities (ESS7), or cultural heritage (ESS8) considerations.

- **ESS5** (Land Acquisition, Restrictions on Land Use and Involuntary Resettlement) — parallels IFC PS5's compensation and consultation logic.
- **ESS7** (Indigenous Peoples/Sub-Saharan African Historically Underserved Traditional Local Communities) — Environmental and Social Standard 7 covering Indigenous Peoples strengthened screening, participation, and free, prior, and informed consent requirements relative to the World Bank's prior safeguard policies.
- **ESS8** (Cultural Heritage) — parallels IFC PS8's consultation requirements for heritage-affecting projects.
- **Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP)** — the SEP's commitments (or SEP elements, for lower-risk projects) are typically reflected within the ESCP, which forms part of the project's legal agreement.

### Implementation Performance and Independent Evaluation Findings

Independent evaluation of ESF implementation has identified both strengths and persistent gaps in ESS10 application. ESS10, covering stakeholder engagement and information disclosure, strengthened inclusion and stakeholder engagement relative to the Bank's prior safeguard policies, but Stakeholder Engagement Plans are underused as tools for feedback and adaptive management according to an independent evaluation of ESF implementation.

More broadly, despite unambiguous policy guidance across development finance institutions, the issue of inadequate stakeholder engagement — also known as consultation and disclosure of information — is a recurring one in complaints to independent accountability mechanisms (IAMs) at international financial institutions. ### Practical Example

**Example:** A World Bank-financed urban water supply expansion project is classified as "Substantial Risk," triggering multiple formal engagement points across the project cycle.

- **Stakeholder identification:** The Borrower's SEP maps affected parties (households in service-expansion zones, informal settlement residents with unclear tenure, small business owners along pipeline routes), other interested parties (municipal utility staff, local NGOs, academic water-policy researchers), and disadvantaged groups (elderly residents without digital access to project updates, non-literate households).
- **Pre-appraisal disclosure:** Before Bank appraisal, the SEP and draft environmental and social assessment documents are disclosed in local languages at accessible community locations and online, with a defined comment period preceding consultation meetings.
- **Consultation:** Multiple town-hall-style meetings are held across affected neighborhoods, supplemented by smaller focus groups for informal settlement residents whose land tenure situation might make them reluctant to speak in larger public forums.
- **Feedback incorporation disclosure:** Following Bank approval, the Borrower discloses a summary showing how consultation input shaped pipeline routing and compensation approaches for temporary business disruption.
- **Grievance mechanism:** A multi-channel GRM (phone hotline, in-person intake at ward offices, written submission box) is established, with documented response-time commitments.
- **Documentation:** Each engagement event's date, location, form, attendance breakdown, and summary of concerns raised are logged and made part of the project's environmental and social documentation, supporting later independent review if needed.
- **Implementation-phase engagement:** During construction, periodic follow-up consultations track unresolved concerns (e.g., disruption to small businesses) and confirm whether mitigation commitments are being met, rather than treating consultation as concluded once financing was approved.

[Inference: the urban water project scenario is illustrative rather than drawn from a documented case; the sequencing (identification → pre-appraisal disclosure → consultation → post-approval feedback disclosure → GRM → documentation → implementation-phase follow-up) reflects ESS10's documented structure and associated Guidance Note and SEP template guidance.]

### Key Points

- ESS10 applies to all World Bank Investment Project Financing and functions as the general stakeholder engagement and disclosure standard within the 2016 Environmental and Social Framework.
- Engagement intensity is proportional to project risk classification — high/substantial-risk projects require multiple formal engagement points plus ongoing dissemination; moderate/low-risk projects require fewer.
- The Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP) is the primary implementation instrument but can be folded into the Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP) for lower-risk projects rather than existing as a standalone document.
- ESS10 distinguishes affected parties, other interested parties, and disadvantaged/vulnerable individuals or groups, each warranting differentiated engagement approaches.
- Disclosure obligations run both before Bank appraisal (to enable meaningful pre-approval consultation) and after approval (to show how feedback shaped project design).
- A defined, structured documentation protocol (date, location, purpose, form, attendance, concerns raised) creates an auditable engagement record.
- ESS10 is a floor, not a ceiling: ESS5, ESS7, and ESS8 impose additional, topic-specific disclosure and consultation requirements on top of ESS10's baseline.
- Independent evaluation has found ESS10 strengthened engagement and inclusion relative to prior policy but identified underuse of SEPs as adaptive-management tools as a persistent implementation gap.
