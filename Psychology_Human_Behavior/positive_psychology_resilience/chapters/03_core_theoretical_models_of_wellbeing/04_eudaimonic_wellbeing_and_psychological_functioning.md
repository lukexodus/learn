## Eudaimonic Well-Being and Psychological Functioning


### Overview

Eudaimonic well-being represents a philosophically and empirically distinct alternative to hedonic well-being, locating flourishing not in pleasant subjective experience but in psychological functioning — the realization of human potential, authentic self-expression, and virtuous activity. Rooted in Aristotelian philosophy, the eudaimonic tradition has produced two dominant empirical research programs: Carol Ryff's model of Psychological Well-Being and Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory.

### Philosophical Foundations

#### Eudaimonia vs. Hedonic Pleasure

Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* explicitly rejects pleasure as the highest good, arguing instead for *eudaimonia* — activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, sustained across a complete life. The term is frequently mistranslated simply as "happiness," obscuring its actual meaning closer to "flourishing" or "living well," which does not reduce to feeling good.

**Key Points**

- Aristotle's *self-realization* criterion is central: eudaimonic well-being involves the fulfillment or realization of one's true nature (*daimon*), not the satisfaction of any desire whatsoever — a distinction later formalized by Waterman as the difference between "personal expressiveness" (activity that expresses and fulfills one's true self) and hedonic enjoyment (which may or may not reflect one's true nature or values).
- This creates a testable conceptual claim: an activity can generate high momentary pleasure while feeling personally inauthentic or disconnected from one's values (hedonic without eudaimonic content), and conversely, an activity can be experienced as meaningful and self-expressive while being effortful or even unpleasant in the moment (eudaimonic without strong hedonic content).

#### The Self-Realization Criterion in Empirical Research

[Inference] Operationalizing "realization of one's true nature" for empirical measurement purposes required substantial translation work by well-being researchers, since Aristotle's original formulation is metaphysically loaded (referencing a "true self" or "daimon") in ways that are difficult to measure directly; the two dominant empirical programs below represent different, non-identical solutions to this operationalization problem.

### Ryff's Model of Psychological Well-Being (PWB)

#### Six-Dimensional Structure

Carol Ryff (1989) constructed her model by synthesizing multiple strands of humanistic and developmental theory (including Maslow's self-actualization, Rogers' fully-functioning person, Jung's individuation, Erikson's stages of psychosocial development, and Aristotelian eudaimonia) into six empirically distinguishable dimensions of psychological well-being:

| Dimension | Core Definition | Low-Scoring Profile |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Autonomy | Self-determination, resistance to social pressure, internal locus of evaluation | Overly concerned with others' expectations, conforms under pressure |
| Environmental Mastery | Sense of competence in managing life and surrounding context | Feels difficulty managing everyday affairs, feels unable to change/improve surroundings |
| Personal Growth | Sense of continued development, openness to new experience | Feels stagnant, lacks sense of improvement over time |
| Positive Relations with Others | Warm, satisfying, trusting relationships; capacity for empathy and intimacy | Few close relationships, isolated, unwilling to compromise |
| Purpose in Life | Goals, sense of directedness, belief life is meaningful | Lacks sense of meaning, few goals, aimless |
| Self-Acceptance | Positive attitude toward self, acknowledges and accepts multiple aspects of self, including past | Dissatisfied with self, wishes to be different than one is |

**Key Points**

- Ryff's model was explicitly constructed as a theory-driven alternative to Diener's tripartite SWB model, which Ryff and colleagues argued was atheoretical (built primarily from empirical clustering of survey items rather than derived from an explicit philosophical account of well-being).
- Factor-analytic studies generally support the six dimensions as related but empirically distinguishable, though the degree of independence among certain dimension pairs (e.g., Self-Acceptance and Environmental Mastery) has been debated in the psychometric literature. The Ryff Scales exist in multiple lengths (from very brief 3-item-per-dimension versions to longer 20-item-per-dimension versions), allowing researchers to trade off measurement precision against survey burden.

### Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

#### Basic Psychological Needs as the Mechanism of Eudaimonic Functioning

Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory takes a different empirical route to eudaimonic well-being, proposing that psychological functioning and well-being emerge from the satisfaction of three basic, purportedly universal psychological needs:

- **Autonomy** — the experience of volition and self-endorsement in one's actions (acting from genuine interest or values, rather than external or internal pressure)
- **Competence** — the experience of effectiveness and mastery in interacting with one's environment
- **Relatedness** — the experience of connection, care, and belonging with others

**Key Points**

- Unlike Ryff's model, which describes *outcomes* of good psychological functioning (six dimensions of being well), SDT proposes a *causal mechanism*: needs satisfaction produces well-being, and needs frustration produces ill-being, across contexts and (SDT claims) across cultures.
- SDT explicitly distinguishes autonomy from independence: autonomous action can be socially embedded or even involve reliance on others, as long as the action is self-endorsed rather than coerced — an important clarification against the common misreading of "autonomy" as simple self-sufficiency.

#### The Organismic Integration Continuum

SDT further refines the concept of autonomous motivation through a continuum describing how thoroughly an externally originating behavior has been internalized:

**Key Points**

- Behaviors regulated by Identified, Integrated, or Intrinsic motivation are considered autonomous (self-endorsed) even though only Intrinsic motivation involves inherent enjoyment of the activity itself — meaning SDT's autonomy criterion is broader than "doing what feels good," encompassing behaviors a person values and endorses even without pleasure (e.g., disciplined study toward a personally-chosen career goal).
- This distinction is central to why SDT counts as an explicitly eudaimonic (not hedonic) framework: well-being tracks need satisfaction and value-congruent action, not moment-to-moment pleasure.

#### Cross-Domain and Cross-Cultural Applications

SDT has been applied extensively across domains including education, healthcare, sport, and organizational psychology, generally finding that autonomy-supportive environments (as opposed to controlling ones) predict better engagement, persistence, and well-being outcomes across these different applied settings.

[Unverified] The claimed universality of the three basic needs across all cultures has been a subject of ongoing empirical investigation and some debate — particularly regarding whether autonomy functions identically as a need in more collectivist cultural contexts — and should be treated as a claim under continued empirical scrutiny rather than definitively settled.

### Comparing Ryff's PWB and SDT

| Dimension | Ryff's PWB | Self-Determination Theory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Theoretical structure | Six descriptive dimensions of good functioning | Three basic needs functioning as a causal mechanism |
| Primary theoretical sources | Humanistic/developmental synthesis (Maslow, Rogers, Erikson, Jung) + Aristotle | Aristotle + organismic/motivational psychology |
| Explanatory ambition | Describes what psychological well-being looks like | Explains why need satisfaction produces well-being |
| Measurement | Ryff Scales of Psychological Well-Being | Basic Psychological Needs Satisfaction Scale, various domain-specific SDT measures |
| Cultural universality claim | Less strongly asserted as universal | Explicitly claimed as cross-culturally universal (subject to ongoing debate) |

[Inference] The two frameworks are best understood as complementary rather than competing: Ryff's dimensions can be read as partial *outcomes* of the needs-satisfaction process SDT describes (e.g., Autonomy and Environmental Mastery in Ryff's model plausibly result from sustained autonomy and competence need satisfaction in SDT's terms), though this integrative reading is an interpretive synthesis rather than a claim either research program formally makes about the other.

### Empirical Relationship to Hedonic Well-Being

**Key Points**

- Eudaimonic and hedonic well-being measures are consistently positively correlated across studies, but factor-analytically distinguishable — supporting the claim that these represent related but non-identical constructs rather than two names for the same underlying phenomenon.
- Waterman's empirical work on "personal expressiveness" found that eudaimonic and hedonic enjoyment, while correlated, could be dissociated for specific activities — some activities were rated high in personal expressiveness/meaning but only moderately hedonically enjoyable, and vice versa.
- [Inference] This dissociation is one of the stronger empirical arguments for treating eudaimonic well-being as a genuinely separate construct rather than as a subtype or relabeling of hedonic well-being, though the practical magnitude of dissociation varies considerably depending on the specific activities and populations studied.

### Applications and Interventions

**Example**

Autonomy-supportive educational interventions grounded in SDT typically involve: providing meaningful rationale for required tasks (supporting identified/integrated regulation), offering structured choice within constraints, acknowledging student perspective and negative feelings about difficult tasks, and minimizing controlling language (e.g., avoiding "must," "should," in favor of informational framing) — consistently associated in the SDT literature with improved engagement and persistence relative to controlling instructional styles. [Inference: specific effect magnitudes are context- and study-dependent.]

**Example**

Ryff-model-informed interventions often target specific underperforming dimensions directly — e.g., a Personal Growth-focused intervention might involve structured novel-skill acquisition with reflective journaling, while a Purpose in Life-focused intervention might involve values clarification exercises and goal-setting frameworks.

### Critiques and Open Debates

**Key Points**

- **Operationalization gap from Aristotle**: Both Ryff's PWB and SDT represent substantial interpretive translations of Aristotelian eudaimonia into measurable psychological constructs; neither can claim to be a direct, uncontested empirical instantiation of Aristotle's original metaphysically-rich concept of a "true self" or "daimon."
- **Overlap with hedonic well-being**: Given the consistent positive correlations between eudaimonic and hedonic measures, some researchers question whether the distinction, while philosophically coherent, adds substantial incremental predictive or explanatory value beyond a well-measured hedonic SWB score in many applied contexts — a genuinely contested empirical question rather than a settled critique.
- **Individualism embedded in "autonomy"**: As with PERMA and VIA, the centrality of autonomy in both Ryff's and SDT's frameworks has drawn cross-cultural critique, particularly relative to more relationally-embedded conceptions of flourishing found in Confucian and other collectivist philosophical traditions.

### Conclusion

Eudaimonic well-being research operationalizes Aristotle's claim that flourishing consists in virtuous, self-realizing activity rather than pleasant experience alone, through two major empirical programs: Ryff's six-dimensional model of psychological well-being, which describes the structural features of good functioning, and Self-Determination Theory, which proposes basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) as the causal mechanism generating that functioning. Both frameworks are empirically distinguishable from, yet consistently correlated with, hedonic subjective well-being, supporting the broader positive psychology consensus that flourishing is a multidimensional phenomenon not reducible to positive affect and life satisfaction alone.
