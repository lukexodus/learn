## Case Study Analysis Methods


### Overview

Case study analysis methods provide a structured approach for examining real or realistic organizational scenarios to develop diagnostic reasoning, apply theoretical frameworks, and practice consulting judgment in a bounded, reflective context. Within organizational psychology education and consulting practice, case studies serve both pedagogical purposes (skill development) and applied purposes (structured analysis of actual organizational situations), and are grounded methodologically in case study research traditions (notably Yin's case study methodology) as well as business/management case-teaching traditions (e.g., the Harvard Business School case method).

### Purposes of Case Study Analysis

| Purpose | Context |
| --- | --- |
| Pedagogical skill-building | Developing diagnostic and analytical reasoning in training/capstone contexts |
| Applied organizational diagnosis | Structured analysis of an actual client situation as a bounded case |
| Theory testing/illustration | Demonstrating how theoretical frameworks apply to real organizational complexity |
| Comparative learning | Examining multiple cases to identify patterns across contexts |

### Types of Case Studies

#### By Research Design (Yin's Typology)

- **Exploratory case study** – used when the phenomenon and research questions are not yet well-defined, generating hypotheses for further investigation
- **Descriptive case study** – provides a detailed, rich description of a phenomenon within its real-world context
- **Explanatory case study** – used to explain presumed causal links in real-world interventions too complex for survey or experimental methods, often relying on pattern-matching and explanation-building analytic techniques

#### By Structure

- **Single case study** – in-depth analysis of one organizational situation, appropriate for unique, critical, or revelatory cases
- **Multiple/comparative case study** – analysis of several cases to identify replication logic (similar findings) or contrasting logic (different findings explained by theoretically predictable differences)

#### By Pedagogical Format

- **Illustrative case** – demonstrates application of a specific theory or framework in a relatively clear-cut scenario
- **Decision-forcing case** – presents an unresolved dilemma requiring the analyst to recommend a course of action, common in business school case teaching
- **Critical incident case** – focuses on a specific bounded event or decision point rather than an extended organizational history

### Structured Case Analysis Framework

#### Step 1: Comprehensive Reading and Fact Gathering

Initial analysis separates objective facts (what is stated in the case) from inferences and assumptions, resisting the common tendency to jump immediately to solutions before fully understanding the situation as presented.

#### Step 2: Problem Identification and Framing

Distinguishing symptoms from root causes, analogous to the presenting-problem-versus-underlying-problem distinction in live consulting entry work. Case analysts should explicitly articulate the core problem(s) rather than assuming the most visible or emotionally salient issue is necessarily the primary one.

#### Step 3: Stakeholder and Context Analysis

Mapping relevant stakeholders, their interests, sources of power, and the broader organizational and environmental context (industry conditions, competitive pressures, regulatory environment) shaping the situation.

#### Step 4: Theoretical Framework Application

Applying relevant organizational psychology theory (e.g., motivation theory, leadership models, organizational diagnosis frameworks, group dynamics theory) to explain observed patterns and generate insight beyond surface-level description. Strong case analysis explicitly connects specific case evidence to specific theoretical constructs rather than asserting theory abstractly.

#### Step 5: Option Generation and Evaluation

Generating multiple plausible courses of action (rather than anchoring prematurely on a single solution) and evaluating each against explicit criteria (feasibility, alignment with organizational constraints, stakeholder acceptability, timeline, resource requirements).

#### Step 6: Recommendation and Justification

Articulating a specific, actionable recommendation with clear justification tracing back to the theoretical analysis and evaluation criteria, along with acknowledgment of risks, trade-offs, and implementation considerations.

### Analytical Techniques Within Case Analysis

#### Pattern Matching

Comparing observed patterns in the case against theoretically predicted patterns (e.g., does the case exhibit the specific dysfunction pattern predicted by a given team dynamics theory?), a core analytic technique in explanatory case study methodology.

#### Explanation Building

Iteratively developing and refining a causal explanation for observed organizational outcomes by systematically testing and revising an initial explanatory narrative against case evidence.

#### Root Cause Analysis Techniques

- **Five Whys** – iteratively asking "why" to trace a symptom back through successive causal layers to a root cause
- **Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagrams** – categorizing potential causes across dimensions (e.g., people, process, systems, environment) to ensure comprehensive causal consideration
- **Force field analysis** – identifying driving and restraining forces relevant to a specific case dilemma

#### SWOT and Environmental Scanning

Structured analysis of Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats, often used in cases involving strategic or competitive dimensions alongside organizational psychology considerations.

### Rigor Considerations in Case-Based Analysis

#### Avoiding Common Analytical Errors

- **Solution-jumping** – proposing solutions before adequate problem definition
- **Single-cause attribution** – attributing complex organizational outcomes to a single cause when multiple contributing factors are typically present
- **Unsupported inference** – treating personal assumptions as case facts without explicit acknowledgment
- **Theory misapplication** – applying a theoretical framework superficially (name-dropping) rather than demonstrating genuine analytical connection to case evidence

#### Balancing Structure and Judgment

**[Inference]** While structured frameworks (the step sequence above, root-cause techniques) provide valuable discipline, effective case analysis also requires contextual judgment about which frameworks are most relevant to a given case's specific dynamics; mechanically applying every available framework to every case tends to produce diffuse rather than incisive analysis.

**Key Points**

- Strong case analysis explicitly separates facts, inferences, and theoretical interpretation rather than blending them together undifferentiated.
- Effective analysis resists premature solution-jumping, ensuring adequate problem definition and stakeholder/context mapping precede recommendation development.
- Theoretical frameworks should be applied with explicit evidentiary connection to case details, not invoked as abstract validation for an already-favored conclusion.

### Case Study Analysis in Capstone and Applied Contexts

In capstone or applied consulting contexts, case analysis methods extend beyond fixed written cases to live or semi-live organizational situations, requiring additional considerations:

- **Incomplete information management** – unlike polished teaching cases, live situations require the analyst to identify what critical information is missing and how to obtain it
- **Stakeholder access and validation** – opportunity (and responsibility) to validate assumptions directly with organizational members rather than relying solely on case-document facts
- **Actionability and follow-through** – recommendations must account for genuine implementation feasibility within the specific organization, not only theoretical soundness in the abstract

### Practical Example

A graduate-level capstone team analyzes a case describing a manufacturing company experiencing high turnover among newly promoted first-line supervisors.

1. **Fact gathering:** The team separates documented facts (turnover rate, tenure data, exit interview themes) from inferences embedded in the case narrative (e.g., an executive's stated but unverified opinion about "generational differences" driving turnover).
2. **Problem framing:** Initial symptom framing ("supervisors quitting") is reframed, through stakeholder-context analysis, around an underlying issue: inadequate leadership transition support for individual contributors promoted into supervisory roles.
3. **Theoretical application:** The team applies role transition theory and self-efficacy theory to explain the pattern, connecting specific case evidence (newly promoted supervisors reporting feeling unprepared for people-management responsibilities) to these frameworks.
4. **Root cause technique:** A Five Whys analysis traces the turnover symptom back through insufficient onboarding, to an absent formal leadership development pipeline, to an organizational assumption that strong individual performance alone qualifies someone for management.
5. **Recommendation:** The team proposes a structured supervisor transition program (grounded in the self-efficacy and role transition analysis) with phased responsibility increases and structured mentoring, evaluated against feasibility and resource criteria explicit in the case.

### Common Pitfalls

- Jumping directly to recommendations without adequately establishing problem definition and stakeholder context
- Treating case-embedded character opinions or assumptions as objective facts without critical evaluation
- Superficially citing theory names without demonstrating genuine evidentiary connection to specific case details
- Attributing complex organizational outcomes to a single simplified cause rather than acknowledging multi-causal complexity
- In applied/capstone contexts, proposing theoretically elegant recommendations that ignore genuine organizational implementation constraints
