## Preparing an Approval Memorandum for a PPP Project

### Purpose and Function of the Approval Memorandum

**Key Points**

- The approval memorandum (often called a Cabinet Memo, Board Paper, PPP Committee Submission, or Investment Committee Memo depending on jurisdiction) is the formal decision document submitted to the body authorized to approve a PPP project at a given gate
- It exists to translate technical, financial, and legal analysis into a decision-ready package for non-specialist decision-makers (ministers, board members, committee members)
- Distinct approval memoranda are typically required at multiple gates across the project lifecycle — approving the project for procurement is a different memo from approving Financial Close
- A well-constructed memo anticipates and pre-empts the questions a scrutinizing body will ask, rather than merely describing the project

### Typical Approval Gates Requiring a Memorandum

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[Concept/Screening Approval] --> B[Feasibility Study Approval]
    B --> C[PPP Suitability/VfM Approval]
    C --> D[Procurement Launch Approval]
    D --> E[Preferred Bidder Approval]
    E --> F[Financial Close Approval]
    F --> G[Post-Award Contract Amendment Approval]
```

Each gate has a distinct memo with a distinct decision being requested — a common structuring error is producing one generic memo template reused without adjusting the actual "ask."

### Standard Memorandum Structure

| Section | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Executive Summary | One-page synthesis: what is being asked, why, and the recommendation |
| Background and Context | Sector need, policy alignment, project history to date |
| Project Description | Scope, technical parameters, PPP structure proposed |
| Rationale for PPP / Value for Money | Why PPP versus traditional procurement, VfM analysis summary |
| Fiscal and Risk Implications | Budgetary impact, contingent liabilities, fiscal risk matrix |
| Legal and Regulatory Compliance | Confirmation of alignment with PPP law, procurement law, sector regulation |
| Risk Allocation Summary | High-level allocation of key risks between public and private parties |
| Stakeholder Consultation Summary | Findings from community, environmental, or affected-party consultation |
| Procurement Strategy / Status | Modality chosen or procurement outcome (for later-gate memos) |
| Recommendation | The specific decision/approval being sought |
| Annexes | Supporting studies, financial model summary, legal opinions |

### Step 1 — Executive Summary Drafting

**Key Points**

- Should be readable and decision-complete on its own — many approving bodies read only this section in detail
- State clearly: (1) what decision is being requested, (2) the project in one paragraph, (3) the headline fiscal/risk numbers, (4) the recommendation and why
- Avoid burying the "ask" — decision-makers should not have to search for what they are actually approving

**Example**

"This memorandum seeks Cabinet approval to proceed to competitive procurement for the Riverside Water Treatment PPP, structured as a 25-year DBFOT concession with an estimated capital cost of $180M. Value-for-money analysis indicates a Public Sector Comparator premium of 14% in favor of the PPP option. The project's maximum contingent fiscal exposure under the proposed risk allocation is estimated at $45M (net present value), remaining within the Ministry of Finance's contingent liability ceiling for the sector. Approval is recommended."

### Step 2 — Rationale for PPP / Value-for-Money Summary

**Key Points**

- Should summarize (not reproduce in full) the Value-for-Money (VfM) analysis, typically comparing the PPP option's risk-adjusted cost against a Public Sector Comparator (PSC)
- Present the comparison transparently, including qualitative factors (innovation, whole-of-life asset management, risk transfer) that a purely quantitative PSC comparison may understate

**Typical VfM summary presentation:**

$$VfM_{\%} = \frac{PSC_{risk\text{-}adjusted} - PPP_{bid\ NPV}}{PSC_{risk\text{-}adjusted}} \times 100$$

**Example**

$$VfM_{\%} = \frac{210M - 180M}{210M} \times 100 = 14.3\%$$

[Inference: methodological conventions for PSC construction (discount rate selection, risk quantification approach, optimism bias adjustment) vary substantially by jurisdiction and should be disclosed in the memo's methodology annex rather than presented as a single unqualified figure.]

### Step 3 — Fiscal and Contingent Liability Disclosure

This is frequently the section receiving the most scrutiny from finance ministries and is often where a memo is sent back for revision.

**Required disclosures typically include:**

- Direct fiscal commitments (availability payments, subsidies, VGF) — annual and NPV terms
- Contingent liabilities (guarantees, termination payment exposure, minimum revenue guarantees) — expected value and stress-tested worst-case exposure
- Off-balance-sheet treatment analysis and statistical classification implications (e.g., whether the project is classified on- or off-government-balance-sheet under applicable public accounting standards)
- Alignment with any legislated PPP fiscal ceiling or contingent liability cap

**Example fiscal risk matrix summary:**

| Risk Event | Probability | Estimated Fiscal Exposure (NPV) | Expected Value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Authority-default termination | Low | $60M | $3M |
| MRG floor triggered (demand shortfall) | Medium | $25M/yr max | $8M |
| Force majeure compensation event | Low | $15M | $1.5M |

### Step 4 — Risk Allocation Summary

**Key Points**

- Present risk allocation in a clear, scannable matrix rather than narrative prose — decision-makers should be able to see at a glance which party bears which risk
- Flag any risk allocation that departs from the standard/precedent position for the sector, with explicit justification

**Example matrix (abbreviated):**

| Risk Category | Public Authority | Private Party | Shared |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Design and construction | — | ✓ | — |
| Demand/volume | — | — | ✓ (MRG floor) |
| Change in law (discriminatory) | ✓ | — | — |
| Change in law (general) | — | ✓ | — |
| Force majeure | — | — | ✓ |
| Land acquisition delay | ✓ | — | — |

### Step 5 — Legal and Regulatory Compliance Confirmation

**Key Points**

- Confirm the project has been structured and (for later-gate memos) procured in compliance with applicable PPP legislation, public procurement law, and sector-specific regulatory requirements
- Reference specific legal opinions obtained, and flag any outstanding legal risks or unresolved regulatory approvals (e.g., environmental permits pending)
- For memos requesting Financial Close approval, confirm all Conditions Precedent have been satisfied or waived, and by whom

### Step 6 — Recommendation and Decision Options

**Key Points**

- Present the recommendation clearly, but where genuinely relevant, offer the decision-making body real options rather than a single take-it-or-leave-it path — this is both good governance practice and reduces the likelihood of the memo being rejected outright for lack of alternatives considered

**Example decision options framing:**

**Option A (Recommended):** Approve proceeding to competitive procurement under the proposed DBFOT structure.

**Option B:** Approve proceeding but with a reduced MRG floor (60% vs. 70% of base case traffic), increasing private risk transfer but likely raising the required toll rate.

**Option C:** Defer approval pending further traffic demand study refinement.

### Step 7 — Annexes and Supporting Documentation

Typical annexes referenced (not reproduced in full) in the memo body:

- Full Value-for-Money / PSC comparison report
- Financial model summary and key assumptions
- Legal opinion(s) on structure and compliance
- Environmental and social impact assessment summary
- Stakeholder/public consultation report
- Draft risk matrix (detailed version)
- Procurement timeline

### Common Pitfalls in Memo Drafting

**Key Points**

- Overloading the executive summary with technical detail that belongs in annexes, obscuring the actual decision being requested
- Presenting VfM analysis as more precise or certain than its underlying assumptions justify — decision-makers should understand the sensitivity of the conclusion to key assumptions (discount rate, risk quantification)
- Failing to disclose contingent liabilities in comparable terms to direct fiscal commitments, which can understate the project's true fiscal footprint
- Omitting a clear statement of what happens if the requested approval is **not** granted (timeline impact, sunk cost, alternative options) — approving bodies frequently ask this and an unprepared memo stalls the decision
- Treating the memo as a one-way information transfer rather than anticipating and pre-addressing likely questions or objections [Inference: the specific concerns a given approving body raises will depend heavily on that jurisdiction's fiscal governance culture and recent PPP track record]
