## Solar Photovoltaic System Architecture


### Overview

Solar photovoltaic (PV) system architecture defines how DC power generated by photovoltaic cells is aggregated, converted, and interfaced to the AC grid. Unlike rotating-machine generation (synchronous or induction generators), PV systems are fundamentally static, semiconductor-based DC sources, meaning the entire system architecture — from cell to grid connection point — is an exercise in power electronics design, DC circuit topology, and inverter control rather than electromechanical machine design.

### PV Cell, Module, and Array Hierarchy

- **Cell**: the fundamental semiconductor p-n junction device generating DC voltage (typically 0.5-0.7 V per cell) under illumination via the photovoltaic effect
- **Module (panel)**: cells connected in series (and sometimes parallel) within a single frame, typically producing 30-50 V open-circuit and rated power commonly in the 300-600 W range for modern modules
- **String**: multiple modules connected in series to reach a higher DC voltage suitable for inverter input (commonly several hundred to over 1000 V for utility-scale systems)
- **Array**: multiple strings connected in parallel to reach the desired total DC power capacity feeding one or more inverters

$$V_{string} = N_{modules} \times V_{module}, \quad I_{array} = N_{strings} \times I_{string}$$

### The Photovoltaic I-V Characteristic

PV cells exhibit a nonlinear current-voltage relationship fundamentally different from a conventional voltage or current source, characterized by a **maximum power point (MPP)** that shifts with irradiance and temperature.

$$I = I_{ph} - I_0\left(e^{\frac{V + IR_s}{nV_T}} - 1\right) - \frac{V + IR_s}{R_{sh}}$$

where $I_{ph}$ is photo-generated current (proportional to irradiance), $I_0$ is diode saturation current, $n$ is diode ideality factor, $V_T$ is thermal voltage, and $R_s$, $R_{sh}$ are series and shunt resistances.

- **Key Points**
  - Short-circuit current ($I_{sc}$) scales approximately linearly with irradiance
  - Open-circuit voltage ($V_{oc}$) decreases with increasing cell temperature — a significant design consideration in hot climates, since string voltage sizing must account for both cold-condition maximum voltage (for inverter/equipment voltage rating compliance) and hot-condition minimum voltage (for maintaining inverter MPPT operating range)
  - Partial shading of even a small portion of a series string can disproportionately reduce string output, since shaded cells act as a current-limiting bottleneck for the entire series string — addressed by bypass diodes within modules that allow current to route around a shaded sub-string section

### Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT)

Because the I-V curve's power output peak shifts continuously with irradiance and temperature, inverters (or dedicated DC optimizers) implement MPPT algorithms to continuously adjust operating voltage/current to track the instantaneous maximum power point.

**Common MPPT algorithms:**

- **Perturb and Observe (P&O)**: periodically perturbs operating voltage slightly and observes resulting power change, adjusting direction accordingly — simple to implement but can oscillate around the MPP and struggle under rapidly changing irradiance
- **Incremental Conductance**: compares instantaneous conductance to incremental conductance to determine MPP direction more precisely, generally offering better tracking accuracy under changing conditions than basic P&O
- **Multi-peak/global MPPT algorithms**: needed when partial shading creates multiple local power peaks in the array's aggregate P-V curve, since simple hill-climbing algorithms (P&O, incremental conductance) can converge on a local rather than global maximum under such conditions
- [Inference] Specific MPPT algorithm implementation and tuning are manufacturer-proprietary and often use hybrid or enhanced variants of these base algorithms; the categories described represent the standard conceptual approaches taught and referenced in PV engineering literature rather than a complete survey of every commercial implementation.

### Inverter Architecture Types

#### Central Inverters

A single large inverter (commonly hundreds of kW to several MW) serves an entire array of strings combined at a DC combiner. Dominant in utility-scale ground-mount solar farms.

- **Key Points**
  - Lower cost per watt at large scale due to economies of scale in power electronics
  - Single point of failure risk for a large capacity block, though modern designs often use multiple parallel power modules within one central inverter cabinet for redundancy
  - Typically limited MPPT granularity (one or a few MPPT channels for a very large array), making them more susceptible to mismatch losses from partial shading or module-to-module variation across a large field

#### String Inverters

Smaller inverters (commonly single-digit to tens of kW, though utility-scale variants now reach hundreds of kW) each serving one or a few strings directly, with multiple string inverters aggregated at the AC side rather than DC combining.

- **Key Points**
  - Finer-grained MPPT (each inverter, or each MPPT channel within an inverter, tracks a smaller, more homogeneous group of strings), reducing mismatch losses relative to central inverters
  - Distributed failure risk — loss of one string inverter affects only its associated string capacity, not the entire array
  - Has become the dominant architecture for utility-scale solar in many markets over roughly the past decade, as string inverter costs have declined and their MPPT/mismatch advantages have been increasingly valued

#### Module-Level Power Electronics (MLPE)

- **Microinverters**: perform DC-to-AC conversion at the individual module level, with each module's power independently converted and injected onto an AC branch circuit
- **DC Power Optimizers**: perform module-level MPPT and voltage/current conditioning in DC, but still feed a centralized string or central inverter for final DC-to-AC conversion
- **Key Points**
  - Maximizes energy harvest under partial shading or module mismatch conditions, since each module (or optimizer-managed module) is individually tracked
  - Provides module-level monitoring/diagnostics, valuable for maintenance and performance verification
  - Higher cost per watt than string or central inverter architectures, generally favoring residential/commercial rooftop applications (where shading and roof orientation complexity are common) over utility-scale ground-mount systems where uniform, unshaded conditions are more typical

### Grid-Following vs Grid-Forming Inverter Control

#### Grid-Following Inverters

The historically dominant control mode: the inverter measures grid voltage and frequency (via a phase-locked loop, PLL) and synchronizes its current injection to match, behaving as a controlled current source that "follows" a grid voltage established by other sources (conventional synchronous generation).

- Requires a stable grid voltage/frequency reference to synchronize against — cannot establish grid voltage independently
- Standard, mature technology across virtually all currently deployed utility-scale PV inverters

#### Grid-Forming Inverters

An emerging control paradigm where the inverter actively establishes its own voltage magnitude and frequency reference, behaving more like a voltage source (analogous to a synchronous generator) rather than a current source following an external reference.

- **Key Points**
  - Enables inverter-based resources to support grid stability in scenarios with declining synchronous generation (high renewable penetration systems), including black-start capability and operation in weak-grid or islanded conditions where grid-following control (dependent on a pre-existing stable reference) can struggle
  - Requires more sophisticated control algorithms (often based on virtual synchronous machine or droop-control concepts) to emulate stabilizing behaviors — such as inertia-like response and voltage regulation — that synchronous generators provide inherently through their physical rotating mass and excitation systems
  - Active area of ongoing standards development and utility pilot deployment; grid codes in several jurisdictions have begun introducing or considering grid-forming capability requirements for large-scale inverter-based resources
- [Speculation] The extent and timeline of grid-forming inverter requirement adoption varies significantly across grid operators and jurisdictions, and remains an actively evolving area of grid code development rather than a settled, universally standardized requirement as of this writing; utilities and standards bodies continue to refine specific technical requirements and testing procedures for grid-forming functionality.

### DC-Side Balance of System Components

- **Combiner boxes**: aggregate multiple string outputs in parallel, typically incorporating string-level fuses to protect against reverse-current fault conditions between parallel strings
- **DC disconnects**: required for isolation during maintenance, positioned per applicable electrical codes at defined points in the DC circuit (e.g., near the array, near the inverter)
- **Surge protection devices (SPDs)**: protect against lightning-induced transients on the extensive outdoor DC wiring characteristic of ground-mount solar arrays
- **Ground-fault protection**: monitors for unintended current paths to ground within the DC system, since PV arrays (unlike many AC systems) can continue generating current into a ground fault as long as illuminated, requiring dedicated DC ground-fault detection and interruption schemes

### AC-Side Interconnection Considerations

- **Step-up transformers**: aggregate inverter AC outputs to medium-voltage collection circuits (in utility-scale plants), then to a plant substation for transmission-level interconnection
- **Reactive power/voltage support**: modern grid-following PV inverters are commonly required to provide reactive power support (voltage or power factor regulation) at the point of interconnection, similar to wind turbine full-converter systems, since the inverter's control system can command reactive current independent of active power output (within its total current rating)
- **Ramp rate control and curtailment**: plant-level controllers can command inverters to limit output ramp rates or curtail output below available irradiance-limited capacity, supporting grid stability or complying with interconnection agreement limits

### Practical Example: String Sizing for a Utility-Scale Array

Scenario: Sizing a PV string for a string inverter with a DC input voltage range of 200-1000 V (MPPT operating range) and modules rated at $V_{oc} = 45$ V (at standard test conditions), with a site minimum expected temperature of -10°C and maximum expected module temperature of 70°C under operation.

1. Determine module $V_{oc}$ temperature coefficient (commonly around -0.3%/°C for typical crystalline silicon modules) and calculate maximum possible $V_{oc}$ at the coldest expected temperature, since open-circuit voltage rises as temperature falls
2. Calculate maximum string length (number of series modules) such that cold-condition $V_{oc} \times N_{modules}$ does not exceed the inverter's maximum DC input voltage rating (a safety-critical limit, not merely an operating-range limit)
3. Calculate minimum string voltage under hot-condition maximum power point voltage ($V_{mp}$, which also decreases with rising temperature) to confirm the string remains within the inverter's minimum MPPT operating voltage even at the highest expected operating temperature
4. Select final string length within the range satisfying both constraints, balancing against DC wiring/combiner design and desired string current for conductor sizing
5. Verify total array configuration (number of parallel strings) matches the inverter's rated DC input current capacity, accounting for any oversizing (DC/AC ratio) design choice made to improve energy harvest during non-peak irradiance conditions

**Conclusion**

Solar PV system architecture is fundamentally a power electronics and DC circuit design problem, distinguishing it structurally from rotating-machine renewable technologies like wind. The evolution from central to string to module-level inverter architectures reflects an ongoing industry trade-off between cost-per-watt (favoring larger, centralized conversion) and energy harvest optimization under real-world mismatch and shading conditions (favoring finer-grained, distributed conversion). The emerging shift from grid-following to grid-forming inverter control represents a potentially significant architectural evolution, addressing the stability challenges of grids with increasing shares of inverter-based, non-synchronous generation — though this transition remains in active development rather than settled universal practice.
