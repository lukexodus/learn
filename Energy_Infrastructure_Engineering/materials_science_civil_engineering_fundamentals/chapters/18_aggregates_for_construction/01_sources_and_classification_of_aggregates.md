## Sources and Classification of Aggregates


### Overview and Significance

Aggregates constitute the largest volumetric component of concrete and asphalt mixtures, typically comprising approximately 60–75% of concrete volume and even higher proportions in asphalt paving mixtures. Understanding aggregate sources and classification systems is foundational to material selection, since aggregate mineralogy, particle characteristics, and geological origin directly govern strength, durability, workability, and long-term performance of the composite materials in which they are used.

### Classification by Geological Origin

**Igneous Rocks**

Formed from the cooling and solidification of molten magma or lava, characterized generally by interlocking crystalline structure and, when unweathered, typically high strength and durability.

- **Intrusive (plutonic) igneous rocks** — cooled slowly beneath the earth's surface, producing coarse-grained crystalline texture (e.g., granite, gabbro, diorite); generally excellent aggregate sources due to high strength, low absorption, and durability
- **Extrusive (volcanic) igneous rocks** — cooled rapidly at or near the surface, producing fine-grained or glassy texture (e.g., basalt, rhyolite, volcanic glass/obsidian); basalt is a widely used, generally excellent aggregate source, though volcanic glass phases in some extrusive rocks can present alkali-silica reactivity concerns

**Sedimentary Rocks**

Formed through deposition, compaction, and cementation of mineral and rock fragments (clastic sedimentary rocks) or through chemical/biological precipitation (e.g., limestone).

- **Limestone** — composed primarily of calcium carbonate, among the most widely used aggregate sources globally due to widespread availability and generally favorable engineering properties, though dolomitic limestone varieties require screening for alkali-carbonate reactivity potential
- **Sandstone** — cemented sand-sized quartz or other mineral grains; engineering properties vary substantially depending on the cementing material and degree of cementation
- **Shale** — fine-grained, laminated sedimentary rock; generally considered a poor and often unacceptable aggregate source due to low strength, high absorption, and susceptibility to breakdown under wetting-drying or freeze-thaw cycling
- **Chert** — a microcrystalline/cryptocrystalline silica-rich sedimentary rock; a specifically flagged concern in aggregate durability screening due to high potential for alkali-silica reactivity

**Metamorphic Rocks**

Formed through transformation of pre-existing igneous, sedimentary, or other metamorphic rocks under heat and pressure, typically without complete melting.

- **Quartzite** — metamorphosed sandstone with quartz grains fused into an extremely hard, durable interlocking structure; generally an excellent aggregate source
- **Gneiss and schist** — metamorphosed rocks exhibiting foliated (layered/banded) mineral structure; engineering properties depend significantly on the degree and orientation of foliation, since strongly foliated rocks can exhibit directional weakness planes
- **Marble** — metamorphosed limestone; generally softer and less durable as a construction aggregate compared to quartzite or well-cemented sedimentary sources, though regionally used where locally available

**Key Points**

- Geological origin alone does not fully determine aggregate suitability; within any single rock type category, specific quarry sources can vary substantially in quality due to local mineralogical variation, weathering history, and degree of alteration
- Petrographic examination (per ASTM C295, previously detailed under microscopy methods) remains the definitive method for identifying specific mineralogy and potential reactivity concerns within a given geological source, since visual rock-type classification alone is insufficient for full durability screening

### Classification by Source and Production Method

**Natural Aggregates**

- **Pit-run or bank-run aggregates** — excavated directly from natural deposits (sand and gravel pits) with minimal processing beyond basic screening; gradation and quality can be more variable than processed aggregates
- **Crushed stone** — produced by mechanically crushing quarried rock, providing greater control over gradation and particle shape compared to naturally occurring gravel deposits, though typically exhibiting more angular particle shape
- **Natural sand and gravel** — produced by natural weathering, erosion, and fluvial (river) or glacial transport processes, typically exhibiting more rounded particle shape compared to crushed stone due to natural abrasion during transport

**Manufactured (Synthetic) Aggregates**

- **Blast furnace slag** — a byproduct of iron production, processed (air-cooled or granulated) into aggregate-suitable material; air-cooled blast furnace slag is used as a conventional aggregate, while granulated blast furnace slag is more commonly processed into a supplementary cementitious material rather than used as coarse aggregate
- **Lightweight aggregates (expanded shale, clay, or slate)** — produced by heating suitable raw materials to expansion temperature in a rotary kiln, creating a cellular internal structure that significantly reduces unit weight while maintaining useful structural properties, used in lightweight structural concrete applications
- **Recycled concrete aggregate (RCA)** — produced by crushing and processing demolished concrete for reuse as aggregate, an increasingly significant category driven by sustainability considerations, though requiring specific quality screening (residual mortar content, contamination, variable absorption characteristics) distinct from virgin aggregate quality control

**Key Points**

- Manufactured and recycled aggregates typically require additional or modified quality testing protocols compared to conventional natural aggregates, since their production processes introduce distinct property variations (e.g., higher and more variable absorption in RCA due to attached residual mortar) not present in natural aggregate sources
- [Inference] The suitability and required quality control protocols for RCA and other manufactured aggregates continue to evolve as standards bodies develop increasingly specific guidance; project specifications should be checked for current allowable replacement percentages and required supplementary testing rather than assuming blanket equivalence to virgin aggregate

### Classification by Particle Size

**Coarse Aggregate**

Per ASTM C33, material predominantly retained on the 4.75 mm (No. 4) sieve, typically ranging up to 37.5 mm or larger for mass concrete or specific applications, with specific gradation size numbers (e.g., ASTM C33 Size Number 57, 67) defining standard nominal maximum size ranges for different applications.

**Fine Aggregate**

Per ASTM C33, material predominantly passing the 4.75 mm (No. 4) sieve and predominantly retained on the 75 μm (No. 200) sieve, commonly natural or manufactured sand, with gradation requirements defined by a specified sieve analysis envelope and a **fineness modulus** calculated from cumulative percentages retained on a standard sieve series.

**Key Points**

- The nominal maximum aggregate size significantly affects concrete workability, required cement paste volume, and in structural applications, minimum required cover and spacing relative to reinforcement congestion
- Well-graded aggregate (a continuous, well-distributed particle size range) generally produces denser packing, reduced void content, and correspondingly reduced cement paste demand for a given workability, compared to gap-graded or uniformly-sized (single-size) aggregate

### Classification by Unit Weight

| Category | Approximate Unit Weight Range | Typical Application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lightweight aggregate | Less than approximately 1120 kg/m³ (70 lb/ft³) for the aggregate itself | Lightweight structural or insulating concrete |
| Normal-weight aggregate | Approximately 1200–1750 kg/m³ (75–110 lb/ft³) bulk density | Conventional structural concrete |
| Heavyweight aggregate | Greater than approximately 2100 kg/m³ (130 lb/ft³), using dense materials such as magnetite, barite, or steel/iron shot | Radiation shielding concrete, counterweight applications |

[Inference] Specific unit weight thresholds distinguishing these categories are defined within governing standards (such as ASTM C330 for lightweight aggregate) and can vary somewhat by specific standard or regional classification convention; the table values represent commonly cited approximate ranges rather than a single universally fixed boundary.

### Classification by Particle Shape and Surface Texture

**Shape Categories**

- **Rounded** — natural river or glacial aggregate with smoothly worn, rounded particle shape, generally producing higher workability at a given water content but potentially lower mechanical interlock/bond compared to angular material
- **Angular** — crushed stone with sharp edges and flat/elongated faces, generally producing higher mechanical interlock and potentially higher strength through better aggregate interlock, but requiring more cement paste/water for equivalent workability compared to rounded aggregate
- **Flaky and elongated particles** — specifically flagged as undesirable in most specifications since they tend to orient preferentially during placement/compaction, creating planes of weakness and reducing workability; standard tests (e.g., ASTM D4791 for coarse aggregate flat and elongated particles) quantify and limit this characteristic

**Surface Texture Categories**

- **Smooth** — typical of natural, water-worn aggregates
- **Rough/textured** — typical of crushed stone, generally promoting better mechanical bond with cement paste or asphalt binder

**Key Points**

- Shape and texture significantly affect both fresh-state workability (angular, rough-textured aggregate generally reduces workability at constant water content) and hardened-state bond strength (angular, rough-textured aggregate generally improves paste-aggregate bond and can improve strength, particularly in higher-strength concrete mixes where aggregate-paste bond becomes a more limiting factor)
- Asphalt mixture design places particular emphasis on aggregate angularity (both coarse and fine aggregate angularity are specifically tested and specified, e.g., per AASHTO/ASTM standardized angularity test methods) since angular, well-interlocked aggregate structure is fundamental to asphalt mixture rutting resistance

### Regional and Geological Source Considerations

**Key Points**

- Aggregate sourcing is inherently regional/local due to the high cost of transporting heavy, low-unit-value bulk material over long distances, meaning available local geology substantially constrains practical aggregate options for a given project location
- This regional dependency underscores the importance of local geological knowledge and aggregate source-specific testing/qualification programs (many transportation agencies maintain approved aggregate source lists based on periodic testing of specific regional quarries and pits) rather than relying solely on generic rock-type classification
- [Inference] Locally established approved aggregate source programs, where they exist, generally represent the most efficient and reliable qualification pathway for a specific project, since they incorporate accumulated regional performance history and periodic re-testing beyond what a one-time generic classification could provide

### Common Misconceptions

- Geological rock-type classification alone does **not** determine aggregate suitability; specific quarry source quality, weathering condition, and mineralogical variation within a rock type can produce substantially different engineering performance, requiring source-specific testing rather than reliance on general rock-type reputation.
- Crushed (angular) aggregate is **not** universally superior to natural (rounded) aggregate; each shape category offers distinct workability and bond trade-offs, and selection depends on the specific application's governing performance requirements (e.g., high-strength concrete versus high-workability pumped concrete).
- Recycled concrete aggregate is **not** automatically equivalent in performance to virgin aggregate; its distinct properties (typically higher absorption, variable quality depending on source demolition material) require specific quality screening and often adjusted mix design procedures rather than direct substitution.
- A locally available aggregate source being widely used historically does **not** guarantee it remains free of durability concerns (such as alkali-silica reactivity); periodic re-testing and petrographic screening remain warranted even for long-established regional sources, particularly as testing methods and understanding of reactivity mechanisms continue to develop.
