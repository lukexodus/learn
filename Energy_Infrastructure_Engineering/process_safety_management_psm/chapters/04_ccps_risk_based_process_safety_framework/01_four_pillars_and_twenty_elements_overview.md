## Four Pillars and Twenty Elements Overview


### Origin and Purpose

The Risk Based Process Safety (RBPS) framework was developed by the Center for Chemical Process Safety (CCPS), an industry technology alliance of the American Institute of Chemical Engineers (AIChE), and published in the guideline *Guidelines for Risk Based Process Safety* (2007). RBPS was designed as a successor to CCPS's original 1989 "Twelve Elements" management system framework, restructuring and expanding process safety management into a risk-based architecture organized around four foundational pillars, each containing multiple elements, for a total of twenty elements.

Unlike prescriptive regulatory frameworks (e.g., OSHA PSM's fourteen elements or EPA RMP's Part 68 program levels), RBPS is a voluntary, risk-tiered management system framework intended to help organizations allocate resources proportionally to risk, rather than applying uniform rigor across all processes regardless of hazard magnitude. RBPS is widely used as a gap-analysis and management-system-design reference, and many company-level Process Safety Management systems in the chemical, refining, and oil & gas sectors are explicitly structured around its four-pillar architecture.

### The Four Pillars

**Key Points**

- The four pillars represent a logical progression: understanding organizational context, understanding the hazards themselves, actively managing the risk day-to-day, and learning from experience to improve over time
- Each pillar groups elements that share a common management focus, though in practice many elements interact across pillar boundaries (e.g., Management of Change touches both Pillar II and Pillar III concerns)

1. **Commit to Process Safety** — Establishing the organizational foundation, culture, competency, and workforce involvement necessary to support process safety
2. **Understand Hazards and Risk** — Building and maintaining the technical knowledge base needed to identify and assess hazards and risks
3. **Manage Risk** — Implementing the operational and engineering controls that keep identified risks within acceptable limits on a day-to-day basis
4. **Learn from Experience** — Establishing feedback loops (metrics, audits, incident investigation, management review) that drive continuous improvement of the system

### The Twenty Elements by Pillar

**Pillar I: Commit to Process Safety**

| # | Element | Core Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Process Safety Culture | Shared organizational values and norms that prioritize process safety in decision-making at all levels |
| 2 | Compliance with Standards | Systematic tracking of and conformance to applicable codes, regulations, and internal/industry standards |
| 3 | Process Safety Competency | Ensuring individuals and the organization collectively possess the knowledge and skill needed to manage process risk |
| 4 | Workforce Involvement | Structured participation of the workforce (not just management) in process safety activities and decisions |
| 5 | Stakeholder Outreach | Engagement with external parties (community, contractors, regulators, suppliers) on process safety matters |

**Pillar II: Understand Hazards and Risk**

| # | Element | Core Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6 | Process Knowledge Management | Maintaining accurate, accessible process safety information (equivalent to OSHA PSM's PSI element) |
| 7 | Hazard Identification and Risk Analysis (HIRA) | Systematic identification of hazards and analysis/ranking of associated risks (encompasses PHA methodologies) |

**Pillar III: Manage Risk**

| # | Element | Core Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 8 | Operating Procedures | Documented, current procedures for safe operation across all operating modes |
| 9 | Safe Work Practices | Permit systems and practices governing non-routine work (hot work, confined space, LOTO, line-breaking) |
| 10 | Asset Integrity and Reliability | Ensuring equipment is designed, fabricated, installed, and maintained to remain fit for service (equivalent to Mechanical Integrity) |
| 11 | Contractor Management | Selection, oversight, and safety performance management of contractors working on or near covered processes |
| 12 | Training and Performance Assurance | Ensuring personnel are trained and periodically verified as competent to perform their process safety-related duties |
| 13 | Management of Change (MOC) | Systematic review and authorization of changes to process technology, equipment, procedures, or organization |
| 14 | Operational Readiness | Verification that a process is safe to start up or resume operation following a shutdown or change (encompasses PSSR) |
| 15 | Conduct of Operations | Deliberate, disciplined performance of tasks according to established standards, regardless of who performs them |
| 16 | Emergency Management | Planning, preparedness, and response capability for process safety emergencies |

**Pillar IV: Learn from Experience**

| # | Element | Core Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 17 | Incident Investigation | Systematic investigation of incidents and near-misses to identify root causes and prevent recurrence |
| 18 | Measurement and Metrics | Leading and lagging indicators used to monitor the health of the process safety management system |
| 19 | Auditing | Periodic, systematic evaluation of whether the management system is functioning as designed |
| 20 | Management Review and Continuous Improvement | Senior leadership review of system performance and deliberate action to close gaps and drive improvement |

### Comparison with OSHA PSM's Fourteen Elements

Since many facilities must simultaneously satisfy OSHA PSM (29 CFR 1910.119) and voluntarily implement RBPS for management-system maturity, understanding the mapping between frameworks is a common practical need:

| RBPS Element | Approximate OSHA PSM Equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| Process Knowledge Management | Process Safety Information |
| Hazard Identification and Risk Analysis | Process Hazard Analysis |
| Operating Procedures | Operating Procedures |
| Safe Work Practices | Hot Work Permit (subset) |
| Asset Integrity and Reliability | Mechanical Integrity |
| Contractor Management | Contractors |
| Training and Performance Assurance | Training |
| Management of Change | Management of Change |
| Operational Readiness | Pre-Startup Safety Review |
| Emergency Management | Emergency Planning and Response |
| Incident Investigation | Incident Investigation |
| Auditing | Compliance Audits |
| Workforce Involvement | Employee Participation |
| — (no direct OSHA equivalent) | Process Safety Culture, Compliance with Standards, Stakeholder Outreach, Conduct of Operations, Measurement and Metrics, Management Review and Continuous Improvement |

**Key Points**

- RBPS contains six elements with no direct one-to-one OSHA PSM counterpart, reflecting RBPS's broader management-system and culture orientation relative to OSHA's more prescriptive fourteen-element compliance structure
- [Inference] Organizations often use this mapping as a starting point for integrated management system design, layering RBPS's culture/metrics/review elements on top of an existing PSM-compliant program rather than building two parallel systems

### Risk-Based Resource Allocation Principle

The defining conceptual feature distinguishing RBPS from a simple checklist framework is proportionality: the intensity of activity within each element should scale with the magnitude of risk posed by the process, not be applied uniformly. A four-tier activity model is commonly associated with RBPS implementation guidance:

$$\text{Element Rigor} \propto \text{Process Risk} \times \text{Consequence Severity}$$

This means, for example, that the Hazard Identification and Risk Analysis element might be satisfied for a low-hazard utility process with a simple checklist-based review, while a high-hazard Program 3-equivalent process would warrant a full HAZOP or Layer of Protection Analysis (LOPA) study. [Inference] This proportionality principle is what allows RBPS to be applied efficiently across large, diverse corporate portfolios spanning many hazard levels, though the specific tiering thresholds are left to organizational judgment rather than prescribed numerically by CCPS.

### Implementation Considerations

**Example**

A mid-size specialty chemical manufacturer conducting an RBPS gap assessment would typically: (1) map each of its existing management system procedures against the twenty elements to identify coverage gaps, (2) score each element's current maturity level (e.g., using a 1–5 maturity scale), (3) prioritize gap-closure activities by combining maturity deficit with the risk ranking of the processes affected, and (4) assign element ownership to specific organizational roles (e.g., Asset Integrity to a reliability engineering manager, Process Safety Culture to site leadership collectively) since RBPS deliberately avoids concentrating ownership solely within a single "PSM department."
