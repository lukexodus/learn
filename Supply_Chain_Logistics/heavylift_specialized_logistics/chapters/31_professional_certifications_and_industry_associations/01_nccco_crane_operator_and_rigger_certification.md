## NCCCO Crane Operator and Rigger Certification


### Overview

The National Commission for the Certification of Crane Operators (NCCCO/CCO) is the leading independent, non-profit personnel certification body for crane operators, riggers, signalpersons, and related load-handling personnel in North America. CCO is a non-profit organization with a mission to develop effective performance standards, provide fair, valid, and reliable assessments, act as an industry resource, and be the leader in providing certifications for those who work in and around load handling equipment. NCCCO certification is directly relevant to heavy-lift and specialized logistics operations, since virtually all crane-assisted heavy-lift work in the United States — and increasingly in jurisdictions with reciprocal recognition — requires personnel holding current NCCCO or equivalent accredited certification.

---

### Organizational Background

**Key Points**

- The National Commission for the Certification of Crane Operators has been the leader in providing fair, valid, and legally defensible personnel certification programs since it was founded by the construction industry in 1995, originally formed as a non-profit organization in January 1995 by the Specialized Carriers & Rigging Association, with cooperation from organizations including the American Subcontractors Association, Associated General Contractors of America, Association of Union Contractors, Association of Equipment Manufacturers, International Union of Operating Engineers, Crane Manufacturers Association of America, and Steel Erectors Association of America.
- CCO is accredited to international standards by ANAB (the ANSI National Accreditation Board) and is recognized by OSHA — federal OSHA officially recognizes NCCCO as meeting both OSHA and ANSI/ASME requirements for crane operator certification.
- The organization is governed by a 9-member Board of Directors with a President and Vice-President, and a 30-member Commission representing ten distinct industry groups; the Commissioners oversee ten Exam Management Committees responsible for monitoring written and practical certification examinations across the organization's certification categories, alongside a separate Ethics and Discipline Committee that establishes ethical standards and standards of conduct.
- Scale of the program: NCCCO has administered more than 1.75 million examinations and issued more than 880,000 certifications over its history — figures reported in NCCCO's own 28-year program summary, up from over a million examinations and 336,000 certifications reported at the organization's 20-year mark, indicating substantial and sustained program growth.
- Regulatory context: the construction industry changed materially when OSHA began requiring accredited crane operator certification in 2018, shifting an industry that previously had minimal formal oversight toward rigorous, standardized personnel qualification requirements.

### Certification Categories

NCCCO certification is available across ten broad categories, spanning Mobile, Tower, Overhead, and Articulating Crane Operators, plus Digger Derricks, Dedicated Pile Drivers, Drill Rigs, Concrete Pumps, and Telehandlers, with additional certifications available for Signalpersons, Riggers, Crane Inspectors, and Lift Directors.

**Key Points**

- **Mobile crane operator certification** covers four main crane-type specialty categories: lattice boom cranes, telescopic cranes of less than 17.5 tons capacity (fixed cab), telescopic cranes above 17.5 tons capacity (swing cab), and hammerhead or self-erecting tower crane; candidates must be at least 18 years old with at least 1,000 hours of crane-related experience, and the certification requires written core examinations plus the option of one or more of the four mobile crane written specialty examinations, alongside practical examinations.
- **Practical examination structure**: for mobile crane certification, the four main practical tasks tested are hoisting, booming, swinging, and following hand signals and multi-function operations; the tower crane practical examination involves trolley travel, hoisting, swinging, and multi-function operations. Crane operation for all exam types is tested both with and without a load, and candidates can lose points through operational errors or time penalties.
- **Crane Inspector Program**: through separate specialty exams, candidates may be certified to inspect mobile (MCI), tower (TCI), and/or overhead (OCI) cranes to ensure proper maintenance and safety; the practical exam uses high-resolution photographs to test candidates' inspection knowledge, and candidates must attest to and provide documentation of a minimum of five years' crane-related experience.
- **Lift Director Program**: for those with responsibilities for lift planning and supervision, exams cover all disciplines required for safe planning and execution of simple and complex single- and multi-crane lifts, with separate certifications available for mobile crane lift directors (MLD) and tower crane lift directors (TLD).
- **Signalperson certification**: in addition to being able to give accurate visible and audible signals, CCO-certified signalpersons (SGP) are required to have basic knowledge of crane operations and limitations, understand specific considerations concerning the construction site, and know applicable safety standards and regulations; testing consists of a multiple-choice written exam and a computer-based practical exam, and signals for both mobile and tower cranes are tested.
- **Washington State Crane Certifier**: a state-specific program developed by NCCCO under agreement with Washington's Department of Labor, requiring candidates to demonstrate at least five years of crane-related experience, of which two years must be actual crane inspection activities; accreditation is available across Mobile, Tower, Articulating, and/or Overhead Crane categories — illustrating that NCCCO also administers jurisdiction-specific programs beyond its national certification catalog.

### Rigger Certification — Level I and Level II

The Rigger certification, administered by NCCCO, targets individuals engaged in load handling and movement, underscoring the importance of specialized training and assessment across rigging operations. The program is structured in two tiers, accommodating varying degrees of expertise and responsibility in the rigging domain.

**Key Points**

- **Rigger Level I** focuses on basic rigging practices, requiring proficiency in selecting rigging components and basic load control techniques; industry training guidance frames Level I as covering basic single-point lifts.
- **Rigger Level II** caters to more advanced rigging challenges, including the execution of complex lifts and the formulation of load-handling strategies; industry training guidance frames Level II as covering complex multi-point and below-the-hook lifts.
- Rigger training content spans sling selection, hitch types, load weight estimation, center of gravity, working load limits, and inspection practices consistent with ASME B30.9 sling-inspection requirements, alongside understanding of load dynamics, rigging hardware selection, and communication protocols essential for safe lifting operations.
- Each level demands a thorough understanding of safety protocols, rigging equipment, and the physics underlying load manipulation, ensuring riggers are well-prepared to maintain safety and efficiency on the job site.

### Recertification Requirements (Rigger Level I / Level II)

The following recertification detail is sourced directly from NCCCO's own program documentation and reflects current, authoritative program rules for the Rigger credential specifically.

**Key Points**

- CCO certification is valid for five years; candidates must complete all recertification requirements during the 12 months prior to their certification's expiration date.
- The Rigger written exams used for recertification are identical to the written exams used for initial certification, and there is no requirement for a Practical Examination as part of recertification.
- There is no grace period after certification has expired: candidates whose certification has lapsed must take the full Written and Practical Examinations to become certified again, rather than following the reduced recertification pathway.
- Rigger Level II certificants need only take and pass the Rigger Level II written exam to maintain both their Rigger Level I and Rigger Level II certifications — reflecting that Level II is treated as encompassing and superseding Level I for recertification purposes, even though both were originally separate credentials.
- [Inference: the above recertification detail is confirmed specifically for the Rigger Level I/II credential from NCCCO's own site; the five-year validity period is broadly consistent with references elsewhere to tower crane certification also being valid for five years, suggesting the five-year cycle is likely standard across NCCCO's certification categories generally — but category-specific recertification mechanics (e.g., whether a practical exam is required) should be verified per-category directly against NCCCO's site rather than assumed to be identical to the Rigger program.]
- Historical program detail illustrates the practical value of aligning expiration dates: NCCCO has noted that operators recertifying in one category (e.g., mobile cranes) can concurrently take the recertification written exam for another category they hold (e.g., tower cranes) to maintain a single common expiration date across all categories of crane a person is certified in — aiding accurate recordkeeping and simplifying the recertification process for multi-certified personnel.
- Certificants who have maintained 1,000 hours of crane-related experience in the preceding five years, and hold a current, completed CCO Physical Examination Form or a current DOT Medical Certificate, have historically not needed to retake certain practical examinations to recertify in some categories — though exact requirements should be confirmed per current category-specific NCCCO documentation, since program rules are periodically updated.

### Industry Relevance and Recognition

**Key Points**

- Federal facilities, state agencies, and Fortune 500 companies commonly require operators to be NCCCO certified, reflecting the credential's status as a de facto industry standard well beyond the baseline OSHA regulatory requirement.
- Some jurisdictions impose their own explicit requirements: all tower crane operators in the state of California must be certified by an accredited, third-party certification organization such as NCCCO, and many major employers throughout the U.S. require tower crane operators to be CCO-certified as a risk-management measure.
- NCCCO is not the only accredited crane-certification body — NCCER (National Center for Construction Education and Research) also offers ANAB-accredited, OSHA-compliant crane operator, rigger, and signal person certifications, including equipment-specific mobile crane categories (Industrial/Carry Deck, Rough Terrain/All Terrain, Rubber Tire Truck Mount, Crawler Mount) and a Tower Crane Operator Certification Program covering luffer, hammerhead, and self-erect tower crane types. Heavy-lift and specialized logistics employers should confirm which accredited body's certification is required or preferred for a given jobsite, client, or jurisdiction, since requirements are not universally standardized to a single certifying organization.
- **Behavior may vary**: [Behavioral disclaimer] Specific certification requirements, fee structures, recertification mechanics, and jurisdiction-specific mandates change periodically; the details above should be verified directly against current NCCCO program documentation (nccco.org) before being used for compliance, hiring, or training-program planning purposes.
