## Renewable Energy Growth as a Demand Driver


### Overview

The buildout of wind, solar, and grid-scale battery storage capacity has become one of the primary demand drivers for the heavy-lift and specialized logistics sector — both ocean-going heavy-lift shipping and land-based heavy transport (SPMT, multi-axle trailer moves, escorted heavy-haul). As renewable generation and storage assets have grown in individual unit size (larger wind turbines, bigger grid transformers, larger battery enclosures), the components require increasingly specialized transport capacity, directly linking global decarbonization policy to heavy-lift equipment and fleet investment decisions documented elsewhere in this chapter.

---

### Offshore Wind: Turbine Growth and Installation Vessel Demand

**Key Points**

- Annual offshore wind installations are expected to double in 2026, while capacity is forecast to grow at around 24% a year through 2030; as offshore wind expands, turbines themselves are becoming larger and more powerful, with today's 15 MW turbines having taller towers, longer blades, and heavier components than those installed a decade ago, while projects move farther offshore into deeper water.
- These changes have driven the evolution of wind turbine installation vessels (WTIVs), which require greater lifting capacity, larger deck space, and the ability to operate safely in more challenging offshore environments.
- Offshore wind deployment has surpassed 83 GW of cumulative installed capacity and requires more than 10,000–12,000 heavy turbine lifts annually for new projects and component replacement cycles; the active global fleet includes 60+ purpose-built WTIVs and 20+ converted heavy-lift vessels, while the orderbook features 45–50 next-generation units scheduled for delivery before 2028 to support turbines rated between 15 MW and 20 MW.
- Crane lifting capacities on WTIVs have increased by 65% over the last decade, moving from roughly 1,200 tons to more than 2,000–3,000 tons, while leg lengths now exceed 105–120 meters to operate in water depths of up to 70 meters.
- Floating offshore wind projects are projected to require installation support for water depths exceeding 60 meters, with mooring system deployment increasing by 41% between 2023 and 2026; heavy-lift vessels capable of handling floating substructures weighing over 10,000 tons represent 27% of newbuild orders in the WTIV segment.
- Port infrastructure upgrades across sites including New York, New Jersey, Virginia, Massachusetts, and Texas have increased component marshalling capacity by 30–40%, enabling simultaneous storage of blades longer than 115 meters and correspondingly heavy nacelles — directly extending the heavy-lift demand chain from the vessel itself to shoreside marshalling-yard logistics.
- Some WTIVs are outfitted with cranes able to lift up to 3,000 tons at heights above 150 meters, and industry players are increasingly building vessels specifically for floating-wind-farm installation, incorporating dynamic positioning systems, heavy-lift cranes, and subsea mooring capability to install floating turbines in deeper water.

### Wind Component Manufacturing and Land Transport Implications

**Key Points**

- The wind turbine market exceeded $170.9 billion in 2025 and is estimated to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of roughly 7.3% from 2026 to 2034 (with a separate estimate placing 2025 market size at $178.89 billion, growing to $283.8 billion by 2030 at a 9.9% CAGR), driven by rising renewable energy adoption and expanding offshore wind projects — the range across sources reflects differing market-research methodologies rather than a single confirmed figure. [Note: cite the range, not a single point estimate, given the spread between sourced reports.]
- Horizontal-axis wind turbines (HAWTs) dominate offshore installations because they efficiently capture stronger, more consistent sea-based winds, reinforcing their role in continued market growth; the horizontal-axis segment specifically is projected to exceed $312 billion by 2034.
- Individual turbine scale continues to grow: as one example, an 18 MW offshore turbine installed at a test site in Shantou, Guangdong Province (China) in June 2024 had a rotor diameter of 260 meters, illustrating the scale of individual components now requiring specialized handling and transport.
- Manufacturers are also pursuing modular nacelle designs specifically to ease transport constraints: a modular nacelle design introduced by a major turbine manufacturer allows customization and is intended to be transportable more easily over rail systems, bridges, and through tunnels, requiring less specialized heavy-transport handling — directly paralleling the "modularization" trend addressed elsewhere in this chapter, but here motivated specifically by a need to reduce transport complexity for wind components rather than increase fabrication-yard completeness.

### Grid Infrastructure: Transformers and Battery Storage as Heavy-Haul Cargo

Beyond wind turbine components, the renewable energy buildout is driving substantial heavy-haul demand for grid-connection equipment — principally high-voltage power transformers required to connect new solar, wind, and battery storage capacity to the grid.

**Key Points**

- In the United States, utility-scale battery storage capacity grew by 10.4 GW in 2024, bringing cumulative storage to more than 26 GW by year-end — a 66% jump — with developers reporting plans to add approximately 19.6 GW more in 2025; expected 2025 capacity additions of roughly 63 GW are approximately 81% solar plus batteries, following a record 30 GW of utility-scale solar added in 2024.
- This capacity growth is directly generating heavy-haul transport demand: high-voltage transformers required to connect new wind, solar, and battery capacity to the grid can be the size of a large truck and must be custom-built, and supply-chain problems have tightened availability of these transformers, forcing some project developers to order equipment before commercial power-sale agreements are even in place.
- Demand for "Generation Step-Up Transformers" — the specific transformer class used to connect new generation to the grid — increased 274% between 2019 and 2025, far outstripping other electrical equipment categories; "Power Transformers" and "Wire & Cable" demand grew 116% and 109% respectively, and high-voltage switchgear demand grew 96% over the same period, together signalling a sustained, structural increase in the volume of oversized/overweight electrical equipment requiring heavy-haul transport.
- Over 2,500 GW of solar, wind, and storage projects are stalled in global interconnection queues, a backlog directly exacerbated by the lack of available transformers needed to connect new generation sources to the grid — indicating that transformer transport and delivery timelines are now a material bottleneck constraining renewable energy deployment itself, not merely a downstream consequence of it.

#### Case example: Huntly grid-scale battery transformer move (New Zealand)

A 172-ton transformer was hauled by three trucks through the night for Genesis Energy's $135 million grid-scale battery project at Huntly Power Station, which will connect a 100 MW battery with 200 MWh of storage to New Zealand's national grid, expected to begin operating later in 2026 and able to supply approximately 60,000 households for two hours during peak demand.

**Key Points**

- The transformer was manufactured in Indonesia and moved from the Port of Auckland to Huntly between midnight and 6 a.m. specifically to reduce traffic disruption, requiring a 17-axle trailer, three pilot vehicles, and an additional truck to assist the load in climbing and descending the Bombay Hills — a concrete illustration of the specialized routing, escort, and multi-axle trailer engineering that individual renewable/storage-grid-connection moves now require.
- A separate, earlier example — a 326-tonne transformer delivered to Powerlink's Greenbank Substation for a grid-scale battery connection project — further illustrates that transformer weights for battery-storage grid connections commonly fall well into heavy-haul (100+ tonne) territory, requiring the same class of specialized multi-axle trailer and permitting expertise used for other heavy-lift project cargo.

### Practical Implications for Heavy-Lift Operators

**Key Points**

- **Marshalling and port capacity planning**: With blades exceeding 115 meters and correspondingly heavy nacelles now common, operators and port authorities need simultaneous storage capacity for multiple oversized components — the cited 30–40% port marshalling capacity increases across several U.S. East Coast and Gulf sites reflect direct infrastructure investment responding to this need.
- **Land-haul route and timing planning**: The Huntly case (multi-axle trailer, multiple pilot vehicles, overnight routing to manage terrain and traffic) illustrates that individual grid-connection transformer moves for renewable/storage projects now warrant the same level of route survey, escort planning, and off-peak timing as traditional heavy-lift project cargo.
- **Early equipment ordering as a hedge**: Because transformer lead times are long and supply-constrained, some project developers are now ordering transformers before commercial power-sale agreements are finalized — heavy-lift/transport providers serving this segment should expect commissioning and transport-planning engagement to begin earlier in a project's commercial lifecycle than has historically been typical.
- **Vessel specification alignment**: WTIV crane capacity, leg length, and water-depth capability requirements are rising in step with turbine size and the shift toward floating offshore wind — operators and shipowners planning newbuild or conversion investment in this space should align vessel specifications with the turbine classes (15–20 MW) that dominate current orderbooks rather than legacy turbine-size assumptions.
- **Behavior may vary**: [Behavioral disclaimer] Market-size figures, capacity-growth percentages, and project-specific weights/dimensions cited here are drawn from market-research and news reporting current as of their publication dates; given the pace of change in this sector, figures should be reverified against the most current market data before use in commercial planning, bidding, or investment decisions.
