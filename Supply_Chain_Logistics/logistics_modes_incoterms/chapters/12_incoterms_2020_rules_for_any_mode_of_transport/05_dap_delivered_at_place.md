## DAP (Delivered at Place)

### Overview

DAP (Delivered at Place) requires the seller to deliver goods by making them available to the buyer, ready for unloading, at the named place of destination. DAP belongs to Group D (Arrival), meaning the seller bears both cost and risk all the way to destination — unlike Group C terms, there is no cost/risk split, since delivery, risk transfer, and cost responsibility all converge at the same point.

### Applicable Transport Mode

- DAP belongs to the "Any Mode or Modes of Transport" category, suitable for road, rail, air, sea, or multimodal combinations.

### Key Points: Obligation Summary

- **Delivery point**: the named place of destination specified in the contract — this can be any location agreed by the parties (e.g., buyer's warehouse, a distribution center, or a specific address), not limited to a port or terminal.
- **Risk transfer**: occurs at the named destination, when the goods are made available to the buyer on the arriving means of transport, ready for unloading — critically, the seller does NOT unload the goods under DAP (this is what distinguishes DAP from DPU).
- **Unloading**: the buyer's responsibility and cost; the seller's obligation ends once goods are ready for unloading, still on the arriving vehicle.
- **Export clearance**: the seller's responsibility.
- **Import clearance**: the buyer's responsibility (this distinguishes DAP from DDP, where the seller handles import clearance).
- **Carriage**: the seller arranges and pays for the entire carriage to the named destination.
- **Insurance**: neither party is obligated to insure under DAP itself; the seller bears the practical risk of the entire journey and should insure accordingly, since the seller retains risk until destination.

### Example

A furniture exporter in Vietnam sells to a retail chain in France under "DAP Lyon Distribution Center Incoterms® 2020." The seller arranges and pays for the entire multimodal journey — trucking to the port, ocean freight, and inland trucking to Lyon. The seller's obligation and risk continue throughout this entire journey. Upon arrival at the named distribution center, the truck is positioned for unloading, and risk transfers to the buyer at that moment — before the goods are physically unloaded. The buyer's warehouse staff must then unload the truck, and the buyer separately handles French import clearance and customs duties.

### DAP vs. DPU vs. DDP: Distinguishing the Group D Terms

| Feature | DAP | DPU | DDP |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unloading responsibility | Buyer | **Seller** (only D-term requiring seller unloading) | Buyer |
| Import clearance | Buyer | Buyer | **Seller** |
| Import duties | Buyer | Buyer | **Seller** |
| Risk transfer point | Ready for unloading, on vehicle | After unloading complete | Ready for unloading, on vehicle |
| Seller's overall obligation level | High | High | Maximum (highest of all 11 rules) |

- [Inference] DAP is often selected as a middle-ground option between DPU and DDP: it relieves the seller of unloading responsibility (unlike DPU) while still leaving import clearance and duty payment to the buyer (unlike DDP), making it attractive to sellers who want to avoid unfamiliar destination-country customs and tax regimes.

### Practical Considerations

- **Named place specificity**: [Inference] Because DAP allows the named place to be virtually any location (not restricted to a terminal or port), contracts should specify the destination with precision (e.g., a full street address) to avoid disputes over exactly where the seller's delivery obligation is fulfilled.
- **Import duty exposure risk for buyers**: since the buyer handles import clearance and duties under DAP, buyers should have import compliance capability or a customs broker in the destination country; unexpected delays in import clearance do not shift risk back to the seller, since risk transferred upon arrival.
- **Interaction with unloading equipment**: because the seller does not unload under DAP, the buyer must ensure appropriate unloading equipment and personnel are available upon the carrier's arrival; delays caused by the buyer's unpreparedness may still result in demurrage or detention charges depending on the carriage contract terms.

### When DAP Is Typically Used

- Sellers with strong logistics capability who want to offer a "delivered" service level to buyers without assuming import clearance and duty payment responsibility.
- B2B transactions where the buyer has established import compliance processes but prefers not to arrange international carriage themselves.
- E-commerce or distribution scenarios where sellers ship to a buyer's warehouse or distribution center but the buyer's local team handles customs formalities.

### Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception**: DAP requires the seller to unload the goods at destination.

  **Clarification**: The seller's obligation ends when goods are ready for unloading, still on the arriving vehicle; unloading is the buyer's responsibility under DAP (this is the key distinction from DPU).
- **Misconception**: DAP and DDP are functionally the same since both involve delivery to a named place.

  **Clarification**: DAP leaves import clearance and duty payment to the buyer, while DDP places this responsibility on the seller — a substantial difference in regulatory and cost exposure.
- **Misconception**: Risk under DAP transfers only once the buyer has physically taken possession of the unloaded goods.

  **Clarification**: Risk transfers when goods are made available for unloading at destination, still on the arriving transport — not after unloading is complete.

**Key Points**

- DAP requires the seller to deliver goods ready for unloading at a named destination, bearing all cost and risk of the journey until that point.
- Unloading is the buyer's responsibility (unlike DPU); import clearance and duties are the buyer's responsibility (unlike DDP).
- Applicable to any transport mode; the named place can be any location agreed by the parties.
- DAP represents a middle-ground obligation level among the three Group D terms.
