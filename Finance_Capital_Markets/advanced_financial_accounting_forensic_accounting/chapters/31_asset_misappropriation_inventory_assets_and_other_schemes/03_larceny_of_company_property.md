## Larceny of Company Property

### Conceptual Framework

Larceny of company property, within the ACFE Fraud Tree, refers specifically to the simple, direct, and outright theft of company assets that have already been recorded on the company's books and are already in the company's possession — distinguishing it from asset requisition and transfer schemes (which use fraudulent internal paperwork to move an asset out of custody) and from false sales/shipping schemes (which exploit the sales and shipping cycle). Larceny is the most unadorned form of asset theft: an employee with physical access simply takes the property, without necessarily generating any fraudulent transaction, requisition, or shipping document at the point of theft itself.

This places company property larceny in the same conceptual position, within the noncash asset branch, that cash larceny occupies within the cash branch: theft of an asset *after* it has already been recorded and brought into company custody, as opposed to schemes that intercept an asset before it is recorded (analogous to skimming) or that use fraudulent documentation to justify its removal (analogous to false sales/shipping or asset requisition schemes).

$$\text{Larceny} = \text{Direct physical taking of already-recorded company property, without fraudulent transaction documentation at the point of theft}$$

### Distinguishing Larceny from Related Noncash Asset Schemes

| Scheme | Documentation generated at point of theft | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Larceny of company property | None | Direct physical removal |
| Asset requisition and transfer schemes | Fraudulent internal requisition/transfer form | Internal paperwork moves asset out of custody |
| False sales and shipping schemes | Fraudulent sales order/shipping document | Exploits sales/shipping cycle to release asset externally |
| Purchasing and receiving schemes | False receiving report | Diverts a portion of an incoming shipment before recording |

Because larceny generates no documentation at all at the moment of theft, it is detected exclusively through the resulting discrepancy — a physical asset count or fixed asset verification exercise revealing the item is missing — making it structurally similar to cash larceny in its reliance on post-theft reconciliation for detection, rather than document review.

### Common Contexts and Methods

**1. Warehouse and storeroom larceny**

Direct removal of inventory, supplies, or equipment from storage areas by employees with unsupervised physical access — loading dock personnel, warehouse staff, or maintenance workers removing items during or after shifts, sometimes concealed in personal bags, vehicles, or trash receptacles scheduled for removal.

**2. Retail floor larceny (employee theft distinct from customer shoplifting)**

Employees removing merchandise directly from the sales floor, stockroom, or back office without any accompanying sale, refund, or void transaction — distinguished from register disbursement schemes precisely because no register transaction is involved at all; the item is simply taken.

**3. Larceny during receiving/unloading**

An employee involved in unloading an incoming shipment diverts a portion of the goods before they are ever logged into the receiving system or physically placed into inventory storage — overlapping conceptually with purchasing/receiving schemes but distinguished when no false receiving report is actually generated (the goods are simply never counted at all, rather than being falsely counted as fully received).

**4. Larceny of tools and equipment**

Removal of company-owned tools, machinery components, or equipment from job sites, maintenance shops, or facilities — common in construction, field service, and manufacturing environments where tools may be checked out informally or not tracked at an individual-item level at all.

**5. Larceny facilitated by collusion with outside parties**

An employee with physical access colludes with an outside accomplice (a delivery driver, a trash/recycling hauler, or an external buyer) to remove property from the premises disguised as legitimate waste disposal, recycling pickup, or an authorized outbound shipment.

### Why Larceny Is Often the Hardest Scheme to Detect Proactively

**Key Points**

- Because no fraudulent document is created at the point of theft, there is no false transaction, altered record, or fabricated paperwork for an auditor or investigator to examine directly — the *only* evidence is the physical absence of the item itself
- Detection is therefore entirely dependent on the frequency and rigor of physical verification (counts, asset tagging reconciliation, surveillance) rather than document-based audit procedures
- The interval between the theft and the next physical count/reconciliation determines the maximum possible delay before detection, meaning organizations with infrequent physical counts have a correspondingly longer exposure window
- Larceny is generally opportunistic and access-driven rather than requiring any particular financial sophistication, meaning it can be committed by employees at any organizational level with physical proximity to the asset — unlike shell company or check tampering schemes, which typically require some position of financial or approval authority

### Detection Techniques

**1. Physical inventory and fixed asset verification**

The primary detection mechanism: periodic (and ideally unpredictable/surprise) physical counts of inventory and fixed asset verification against the asset register, since larceny's only observable trace is the resulting shortage or missing item.

**2. Surveillance and access control monitoring**

- Video surveillance coverage of storage areas, loading docks, and high-value asset locations, reviewed both reactively (following a discovered shortage) and, where feasible, proactively through periodic sampling
- Badge access log review, correlating physical entry/exit patterns against shift schedules and job responsibilities, flagging after-hours access inconsistent with an employee's normal duties

**3. Shrinkage and variance analytics**

As with inventory theft generally, statistical review of shrinkage rates and variance patterns by location, department, and time period, since larceny (even without any accompanying fraudulent transaction) still manifests as an unexplained quantity discrepancy in the same way other inventory theft methods do.

**4. Trash and waste disposal monitoring**

Given the common practice of concealing stolen items in trash or recycling awaiting removal, periodic inspection of waste disposal contents (particularly in high-shrinkage locations) and review of waste hauling contracts/schedules for unusual patterns or unauthorized personnel involvement.

**5. Employee tip and hotline reporting**

Consistent with the broader finding across occupational fraud categories, direct physical larceny is frequently first identified through coworker observation and tips rather than through analytical or audit procedures, since a coworker witnessing an item being physically removed is often the most direct available evidence. [Inference: consistent with the general ACFE finding that tips are the leading detection method across occupational fraud categories broadly; the specific proportion for larceny specifically versus other schemes varies by survey year and should be verified against the current ACFE Report to the Nations]

### Red Flags Checklist

| Category | Indicator |
| --- | --- |
| Physical count results | Unexplained shortages concentrated at a specific location, shift, or department |
| Access patterns | After-hours facility or storage area access inconsistent with job role or shift schedule |
| Behavioral | Employees resistant to bag/vehicle checks, or observed leaving with unexplained packages |
| Waste disposal | Trash/recycling volume or scheduling inconsistent with normal operational patterns |
| Tool/equipment tracking | High rate of tools/equipment reported "lost" or "never returned" concentrated with specific individuals |
| Collusion indicators | Unusual familiarity or unscheduled contact between employees and delivery/waste hauling personnel |

### Internal Controls

**Preventive controls**

1. **Physical access restrictions** — locked storage areas, badge-controlled entry to high-value asset locations, and restricted after-hours access aligned to actual job requirements
2. **Bag/vehicle inspection policies** at facility exit points, applied consistently and disclosed as company policy to all employees
3. **Individual tool/equipment check-out systems** with signed accountability logs for higher-value tools and equipment, rather than informal or untracked distribution
4. **Camera coverage** of loading docks, storage areas, and waste disposal/staging areas, with signage disclosing surveillance
5. **Segregation of duties** ensuring the individual with physical custody of an asset is not also the sole individual responsible for its recordkeeping and periodic verification

**Detective controls**

1. **Unpredictable, surprise physical counts and fixed asset verification exercises**, rather than exclusively relying on a single scheduled annual count
2. **Routine review of shrinkage and variance analytics** by location/department, independent of local management
3. **Periodic waste disposal audits**, particularly at locations exhibiting elevated shrinkage
4. **Anonymous tip/hotline mechanisms** actively promoted and genuinely protected from retaliation, given the outsized role of tips in surfacing this scheme type
5. **Exit interview and property return checklists** for departing employees, particularly those with equipment or tool custody responsibilities

### Illustrative Example

A construction company's tool inventory shows a persistent pattern of specialized power tools reported as "lost on job site" at a rate significantly higher for one project foreman's crew compared to other crews working comparable projects. No fraudulent requisition, transfer, or purchasing documentation is involved — the tools are simply never returned to the equipment yard at project completion. Investigation, prompted by a coworker tip describing tools being loaded into a personal truck at the end of a shift, combines badge access records (showing after-hours yard access by the foreman inconsistent with any scheduled activity) with equipment check-out logs, ultimately substantiating a pattern of direct theft accumulated over multiple projects.

**Conclusion**

Larceny of company property represents the most conceptually direct form of noncash asset misappropriation — theft without any accompanying fraudulent transaction, requisition, or documentation at the point of taking — which paradoxically makes it both the easiest scheme to understand and among the hardest to detect proactively, since there is no false document or altered record for an auditor to examine; the shortage itself is the only evidence. This structural characteristic shifts the entire control emphasis toward physical prevention (access restriction, surveillance, custody accountability) and detection through periodic, ideally unpredictable, physical verification and shrinkage analytics, supplemented heavily by tip-based reporting, rather than the document-and-transaction-based audit procedures that are more effective against the other, more procedurally sophisticated noncash asset schemes in this chapter.
