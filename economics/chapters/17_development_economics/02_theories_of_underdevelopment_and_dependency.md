## Theories of Underdevelopment and Dependency


### Definition and Core Concept

Theories of underdevelopment and dependency seek to explain why certain countries, predominantly in the Global South, have remained persistently poor or grown slowly relative to industrialized nations, and whether this condition arises from internal structural characteristics or from the nature of their historical and ongoing economic relationships with wealthier, industrialized "core" countries. These theories emerged largely as critiques of, and alternatives to, mainstream modernization theory, which viewed underdevelopment as an early, transitional stage that all countries pass through on a linear path toward industrialization.

### Modernization Theory: The Precursor Framework

Modernization theory, dominant in development economics through the 1950s-60s, viewed underdevelopment not as a distinct condition requiring separate analysis, but simply as an earlier stage of a universal development process that all economies traverse.

**Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth**

Economist Walt Rostow's influential 1960 framework proposed five linear stages through which all economies progress:

1. **Traditional society**: Subsistence agriculture, limited technology, pre-Newtonian science, rigid social hierarchies.
2. **Preconditions for take-off**: Emergence of a modernizing elite, growth of infrastructure, development of a manufacturing sector, and increased savings/investment rates.
3. **Take-off**: A relatively short period (Rostow suggested roughly two decades) of rapid growth, driven by a sharp rise in the investment rate (typically cited as needing to reach 10% or more of national income) and the emergence of leading growth sectors.
4. **Drive to maturity**: Diversification of the economy beyond initial leading sectors, application of modern technology across a broad range of industries, and rising sophistication of production.
5. **Age of high mass consumption**: Shift toward consumer durables and services as the dominant economic activity, characteristic of mature industrialized economies.

**Key Points**

- Modernization theory implicitly assumes that underdeveloped countries can and should follow the same developmental trajectory historically taken by Western industrialized nations, given sufficient capital investment, technology transfer, and institutional modernization.
- The framework places heavy emphasis on domestic savings and investment rates as the primary bottleneck to development, aligning closely with Harrod-Domar-style growth models.
- Critics, including dependency theorists, argue this framework ignores the historical context of colonialism and the specific structural position of developing countries within the global economic system, treating "underdevelopment" as simply "not yet developed" rather than as a condition actively produced by international economic relationships.

### Dependency Theory: Core Concept

Dependency theory, emerging primarily from Latin American economists in the 1950s-60s (notably associated with the **Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean, or ECLAC/CEPAL**) and later developed by scholars such as Andre Gunder Frank, Fernando Henrique Cardoso, and Immanuel Wallerstein, argues that underdevelopment in poorer nations ("the periphery") is not an original or transitional condition, but is actively *produced and reproduced* by their structural relationship with wealthier, industrialized nations ("the core") within the global capitalist system.

**Key Points**

- Underdevelopment is viewed as a **relational condition**, not an independent starting point — peripheral countries are underdeveloped *because of*, not merely *prior to*, their integration into the world economy on unfavorable terms.
- The global economic system is characterized by a structural division between a **core** (industrialized, capital-intensive, high-wage economies) and a **periphery** (primary-commodity-exporting, low-wage, capital-scarce economies), with **semi-peripheral** economies occupying an intermediate position in some formulations (particularly in Wallerstein's World-Systems Theory).
- Trade and investment relationships between core and periphery are argued to systematically transfer economic surplus from periphery to core, perpetuating the periphery's underdevelopment rather than facilitating its "catch-up."

### Raúl Prebisch and the Prebisch-Singer Hypothesis

Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch, along with British economist Hans Singer, independently developed a key empirical and theoretical pillar of dependency theory: the argument that the **terms of trade** (the ratio of export prices to import prices) for primary-commodity-exporting countries exhibit a long-run tendency to deteriorate relative to manufactured-goods-exporting countries.

$$\text{Terms of Trade} = \frac{P_x}{P_m} \times 100$$

Where $P_x$ is an index of export prices and $P_m$ is an index of import prices.

**Key Points**

- **Income elasticity of demand differences**: As global income rises, demand for manufactured goods and services tends to rise faster (higher income elasticity of demand) than demand for primary commodities (which have relatively low income elasticity, per Engel's Law-type reasoning extended to raw materials), causing commodity prices to lag manufactured goods prices over time.
- **Market structure asymmetries**: Manufacturing sectors in industrialized "core" countries were argued to be more oligopolistic (with stronger labor unions and greater pricing power), allowing productivity gains to translate into higher wages and stable or rising prices, whereas competitive primary commodity markets in the periphery transmit productivity gains directly into lower prices rather than higher incomes for producers.
- **Policy implication**: If the terms of trade for primary-commodity exporters systematically deteriorate over time, then peripheral countries relying on primary export-led growth (as recommended by classical trade theory and comparative advantage) will face a persistent structural disadvantage, providing the theoretical rationale for **import substitution industrialization (ISI)** as an alternative development strategy.
- [Inference] Empirical support for the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis has been mixed and contested across different time periods and commodity types in subsequent research; some studies find long-run downward trends in specific commodity terms of trade, while others find the relationship is sensitive to the historical period examined, the specific commodities and manufactured goods included, and methods used to deflate and index prices.

### Import Substitution Industrialization (ISI)

ISI is the policy strategy most closely associated with dependency theory and structuralist economics, advocating that developing countries should industrialize by replacing imported manufactured goods with domestically produced substitutes, typically behind protective trade barriers.

**Key Points**

- **Tariffs and quotas on manufactured imports**: Used to protect nascent ("infant") domestic industries from foreign competition while they develop.
- **State-directed industrial policy**: Governments often took an active role in directing credit, establishing state-owned enterprises, and coordinating investment in targeted industrial sectors.
- **Overvalued exchange rates**: Frequently maintained to reduce the cost of imported capital goods and inputs needed for domestic industrialization, often at the expense of traditional export sectors' competitiveness.
- **Widely adopted across Latin America** (Brazil, Argentina, Mexico) and parts of South Asia and Africa from the 1950s through the 1970s-80s.

**Criticisms and Outcomes of ISI**

[Inference] ISI strategies are widely, though not universally, viewed in the subsequent economic literature as having produced significant inefficiencies over the medium-to-long term, including domestic industries that remained uncompetitive and reliant on continued protection ("infant industries" that never matured), balance of payments pressures due to continued reliance on imported capital goods and intermediate inputs, and resource misallocation driven by rent-seeking behavior around import licenses and protection. The comparative success of East Asian economies (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore) that pursued **export-oriented industrialization** instead, achieving faster and more sustained growth from roughly the 1960s onward, is frequently cited in mainstream development economics as evidence against the ISI approach, though this comparison and its causal interpretation remain subjects of ongoing debate, including disagreement over the extent to which East Asian success also involved substantial state intervention and selective protection rather than pure free-market liberalization.

### André Gunder Frank and the "Development of Underdevelopment"

Andre Gunder Frank offered one of the most radical formulations of dependency theory, arguing directly that underdevelopment is not a natural or original condition of peripheral economies but is actively created through their historical incorporation into the capitalist world system, primarily through **colonialism** and continued neocolonial economic relationships.

**Key Points**

- Frank's "metropolis-satellite" framework argued that surplus extraction occurs at multiple hierarchical levels — from local peasant producers, to regional trading centers, to national capitals, to the global metropolis — with each level extracting surplus from the level below it.
- This framework was heavily applied to the historical economic experience of Latin America, where colonial-era institutions were argued to have established extractive economic structures that persisted long after formal independence.
- Frank's analysis suggested that even seemingly "isolated" or "backward" regions within peripheral countries were often historically the most fully integrated into extractive colonial trade networks, contradicting modernization theory's assumption that isolation from the global economy (rather than integration into it) was the primary cause of underdevelopment.

### World-Systems Theory (Immanuel Wallerstein)

Wallerstein's World-Systems Theory extends dependency theory into a more comprehensive historical and sociological framework, analyzing the capitalist world economy as a single integrated system since roughly the "long 16th century," structured around three tiers.

**Key Points**

- **Core**: Economically diversified, capital-intensive, high-wage regions that dominate the system's manufacturing and financial sectors and exercise significant political and military power.
- **Periphery**: Economically specialized (often in primary commodity production), low-wage, coercive labor systems (historically including slavery and indentured labor in colonial contexts), and politically weaker states.
- **Semi-periphery**: An intermediate category, exhibiting characteristics of both core and periphery, sometimes serving as a buffer that stabilizes the overall system and occasionally allowing limited upward mobility between tiers (e.g., some newly industrialized economies).
- Unlike simpler dependency formulations, World-Systems Theory allows for the possibility of nations moving between tiers over long historical periods, though it maintains that the overall core-periphery structure of the system as a whole tends to persist.

### Dependency Theory and Modernization Theory Compared

| Feature | Modernization Theory | Dependency Theory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| View of underdevelopment | Early/transitional stage on a universal path | Actively produced, relational condition |
| Unit of analysis | Individual nation-states in isolation | Global system of core-periphery relationships |
| Prescribed strategy | Capital investment, institutional modernization, integration into global trade | Reduce reliance on core (ISI), restructure terms of engagement with global economy |
| View of international trade | Generally beneficial (comparative advantage) | Often exploitative, reinforces unequal exchange |
| Historical/colonial context | Largely ignored or treated as incidental | Central to explaining current structural position |

### Structuralist Economics

Closely related to dependency theory, **structuralist economics** (also associated with ECLAC/CEPAL and economists such as Prebisch) emphasizes that developing economies possess distinct structural characteristics — rigidities in factor markets, dualistic economic structures (a modern sector coexisting with a traditional/subsistence sector), and external dependence — that make standard neoclassical policy prescriptions (e.g., simple trade liberalization) less applicable or even counterproductive without accompanying structural transformation.

**Key Points**

- **Structural bottlenecks**: Inflexible supply responses in agriculture, foreign exchange constraints, and fiscal rigidities are argued to require targeted government intervention rather than reliance on market-clearing price adjustments alone.
- **Dual economy models**: Related frameworks (e.g., the Lewis model of economic development with unlimited supplies of labor) analyze the coexistence of a traditional, low-productivity agricultural sector and a modern, higher-productivity industrial sector, with development involving the gradual transfer of labor between sectors.
- **Foreign exchange constraints ("two-gap" models)**: Structuralist growth models often highlight that developing economies can face a binding foreign exchange constraint on growth even when domestic savings are theoretically sufficient, since imported capital goods essential for investment require foreign currency.

### Criticisms of Dependency Theory

**Key Points**

- **East Asian counterexamples**: The rapid growth of the "East Asian Tigers" (South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore) through export-oriented, globally integrated strategies is frequently cited as a major empirical challenge to dependency theory's core prediction that integration into the global capitalist system perpetuates underdevelopment.
- **Insufficient attention to domestic institutional factors**: Critics, particularly from the New Institutional Economics tradition, argue dependency theory understates the role of domestic institutional quality, governance, property rights, and rule of law in explaining divergent development outcomes, in favor of external/structural explanations.
- **Overly deterministic framework**: Some critics argue dependency theory's emphasis on structural constraints can understate the scope for successful policy agency and reform within developing countries, potentially fostering a fatalistic view of development prospects.
- **Empirical heterogeneity**: The wide variation in outcomes among countries that pursued similar ISI or dependency-informed strategies (some experiencing prolonged stagnation, others achieving periods of substantial growth before crises) complicates simple generalizations about the framework's predictive validity.
- [Inference] Despite these criticisms, dependency theory's influence persists in framing ongoing debates about global economic governance, fair trade, unequal exchange in global value chains, and the terms on which developing countries integrate into globalized production networks, even though its specific ISI-based policy prescriptions have been largely abandoned by mainstream development policy since the 1980s-90s shift toward market-oriented reforms (associated with the Washington Consensus).

### Legacy and Contemporary Relevance

**Key Points**

- **Global value chains and unequal exchange**: Contemporary research on global value chains examines how value is distributed across different stages of production located in different countries, echoing dependency theory's concerns about surplus distribution, even within a more globally integrated and less overtly colonial framework.
- **Debt dependency debates**: Concerns about developing country debt burdens to international creditors and institutions (extending into contemporary discussions of debt sustainability and structural adjustment) draw on dependency-theoretic concerns about asymmetric power relationships in the global financial system.
- **Resource curse literature**: The "resource curse" literature, examining why countries rich in natural resources often experience slower growth and weaker institutional development than resource-poor counterparts, shares intellectual lineage with dependency theory's concerns about primary-commodity export dependence.
- **Influence on New Institutional Economics**: Even critics of dependency theory's specific claims have engaged with its emphasis on historical and structural context, contributing to the broader development economics shift toward examining the historical origins of institutions (e.g., research on the long-run economic effects of colonial institutions).
