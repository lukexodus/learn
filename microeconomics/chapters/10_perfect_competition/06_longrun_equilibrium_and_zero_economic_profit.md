## Long-run Equilibrium and Zero Economic Profit


### Definition

Long-run equilibrium in a perfectly competitive market is the state toward which the industry converges once firms have had sufficient time to enter, exit, and adjust the scale of all inputs (including capital, plant size, and technology). In this equilibrium, every active firm produces at the minimum point of its long-run average cost curve, earns zero economic profit, and there is no further incentive for firms to enter or exit the industry.

### The Three Conditions for Long-run Competitive Equilibrium

For an industry to be in full long-run equilibrium, three conditions must simultaneously hold:

**1. Profit-Maximization Condition**

$$P = MR = MC = LMC$$

Each firm produces the output level where price equals long-run marginal cost.

**2. Zero Economic Profit Condition (No Entry/Exit Incentive)**

$$P = LAC_{min}$$

Price equals the minimum point of the long-run average cost curve. This ensures firms earn zero *economic* profit, so there is no incentive for outside firms to enter or existing firms to exit.

**3. Short-run and Long-run Consistency**

$$SMC = LMC = SAC = LAC = P$$

Each firm's plant size (short-run scale) is optimally adjusted such that short-run marginal and average costs equal their long-run counterparts at the equilibrium output level. This means every firm is operating its *currently sized* plant at the most efficient scale.

Combining all three: at long-run equilibrium,

$$P = MR = SMC = LMC = SAC_{min} = LAC_{min}$$

### Understanding Zero Economic Profit

**Economic profit** differs from **accounting profit** by including implicit (opportunity) costs:

$$\text{Economic Profit} = \text{Total Revenue} - \text{Explicit Costs} - \text{Implicit Costs}$$



$$\text{Accounting Profit} = \text{Total Revenue} - \text{Explicit Costs}$$

Zero economic profit does **not** mean the firm is making no money. It means the firm's total revenue exactly covers all explicit costs *plus* the opportunity cost of the resources the owner has invested — including a "normal profit" (the return the owner could earn in their next-best alternative use of capital and labor). A firm earning zero economic profit is earning exactly enough to keep its owner from switching to another industry, but not enough to attract new entrants.

### The Adjustment Process (Entry and Exit Dynamics)

Long-run equilibrium is reached through a self-correcting mechanism:

**Case 1: Positive Economic Profit in the Short Run**

- If $P > LAC_{min}$, existing firms earn positive economic profit.
- Because entry is free (a defining assumption of perfect competition), new firms are attracted into the industry.
- As new firms enter, market supply increases (the market supply curve shifts right).
- This increase in supply drives the market price down.
- Entry continues until price falls back to $LAC_{min}$, eliminating economic profit.

**Case 2: Economic Losses in the Short Run**

- If $P < LAC_{min}$, firms earn negative economic profit (losses).
- Firms exit the industry over time (existing firms do not renew leases, replace worn capital, or continue operating once fully variable costs are unavoidable).
- As firms exit, market supply decreases (shifts left).
- This decrease in supply drives the market price up.
- Exit continues until price rises back to $LAC_{min}$, eliminating losses.

**Case 3: Zero Economic Profit**

- If $P = LAC_{min}$, there is no incentive for entry or exit.
- The industry has reached long-run equilibrium — a stable state absent external shocks.

### Numerical Example

Suppose every firm in a perfectly competitive industry has the identical long-run total cost function:

$$LTC(q) = q^3 - 12q^2 + 60q$$

**Step 1 — Derive LAC:**

$$LAC(q) = \frac{LTC(q)}{q} = q^2 - 12q + 60$$

**Step 2 — Find the minimum of LAC** by taking the derivative and setting it to zero:

$$\frac{d(LAC)}{dq} = 2q - 12 = 0 \implies q^* = 6$$

**Step 3 — Calculate LAC at $q^* = 6$:**

$$LAC(6) = 6^2 - 12(6) + 60 = 36 - 72 + 60 = 24$$

So the long-run equilibrium price is $P^* = \$24$, and each firm produces $q^* = 6$ units.

**Step 4 — Verify using LMC:**

$$LMC(q) = \frac{d(LTC)}{dq} = 3q^2 - 24q + 60$$



$$LMC(6) = 3(36) - 24(6) + 60 = 108 - 144 + 60 = 24$$

Confirmed: $P^* = LMC(6) = LAC(6) = \$24$, satisfying all long-run equilibrium conditions.

**Step 5 — Determine the number of firms**, if market demand is known. Suppose market demand at $P = 24$ is $Q_D = 3{,}600$ units. The equilibrium number of firms is:

$$n = \frac{Q_D}{q^*} = \frac{3{,}600}{6} = 600 \text{ firms}$$

### Productive and Allocative Efficiency in Long-run Equilibrium

Long-run competitive equilibrium delivers two efficiency properties simultaneously:

- **Productive Efficiency**: Firms produce at $LAC_{min}$, meaning output is produced at the lowest possible average cost given existing technology. No resources are wasted in production.
- **Allocative Efficiency**: Since $P = MC$, the price consumers pay exactly reflects the marginal cost (opportunity cost of resources) of producing one more unit. Consumer willingness to pay equals the cost of production at the margin, so resources are allocated to their highest-valued use across the economy.

$$P = MC = LAC_{min}$$

This dual efficiency result is a central welfare property of perfectly competitive markets and underlies the First Welfare Theorem's application to this market structure.

### Firm Size and the Minimum Efficient Scale

The output level $q^*$ at which $LAC$ is minimized is called the **minimum efficient scale (MES)**. In long-run equilibrium, every firm operates precisely at its MES. If firms were producing at a lower output (on the falling portion of $LAC$, exhibiting economies of scale) or higher output (on the rising portion, exhibiting diseconomies of scale), they would not be minimizing average cost, and competitive pressure would push them toward $q^*$.

### Distinguishing Short-run vs. Long-run Equilibrium

| Feature | Short-run Equilibrium | Long-run Equilibrium |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of firms | Fixed | Variable (free entry/exit) |
| Economic profit | Can be positive, negative, or zero | Always zero |
| Plant size | Fixed (at least one input fixed) | Fully variable (optimal scale chosen) |
| Price relation | $P = SMC$ | $P = SMC = LMC = SAC_{min} = LAC_{min}$ |
| Firm output | May be above/below $LAC_{min}$ | Exactly at $LAC_{min}$ |

### Common Misconceptions

- Zero economic profit is often mistaken for the firm "barely surviving" or being unprofitable in an ordinary sense. In fact, it includes a normal return to the entrepreneur's capital and labor, so the firm is fully compensating its owner for their opportunity cost — it simply earns nothing *above* that.
- Students sometimes assume long-run equilibrium is a one-time event. In practice, it is a theoretical benchmark the industry tends toward; real-world markets are continuously perturbed by demand and cost shocks, so exact long-run equilibrium is rarely observed but serves as the reference point for analyzing adjustment dynamics.
- **[Unverified — pedagogical simplification]** The assumption of identical cost structures across all firms (used above for tractability) is a simplifying assumption; in reality, firms may differ in efficiency, and the marginal (least efficient) firm still able to survive determines the zero-profit condition at the industry level, while more efficient firms may earn positive economic rents tied to a scarce input (e.g., superior management or location) rather than true "economic profit" in the competitive sense.
