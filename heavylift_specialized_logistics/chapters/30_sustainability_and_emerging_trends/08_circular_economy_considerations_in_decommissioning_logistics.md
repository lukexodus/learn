## Circular Economy Considerations in Decommissioning Logistics


### Overview

As the first generation of major energy infrastructure — offshore oil and gas platforms and, increasingly, early offshore wind farms — reaches end of life, decommissioning has become a significant and growing logistics workstream for the heavy-lift sector. This section documents both sides of that workstream: the physical heavy-lift removal logistics (jackets, topsides, transport to shore) developed and refined by the oil and gas sector, and the circular economy frameworks now being proposed and applied to offshore wind decommissioning specifically, which explicitly seek to avoid repeating the oil and gas sector's largely recycling-and-disposal-oriented approach.

---

### Decommissioning as a Logistics and Waste-Management Project

**Key Points**

- A decommissioning project consists of the elements planning and approval, topside and jacket preparatory work, topside removal, jacket removal, transport, and onshore dismantling and recycling.
- At its core, decommissioning is essentially a waste management project — however, this framing is not always recognized within the oil and gas sector, where greater focus is commonly given to offshore engineering activities, including in how decommissioning contracts are awarded and scoped.
- In the North Sea alone, around 470 installations will require decommissioning over the next 30 to 40 years, indicating this is a sustained, multi-decade logistics demand category rather than a short-term or one-off activity.
- Decommissioning is described industry-wide as a growing market, with individual projects now representing among the largest tonnage decommissioning campaigns a contractor has handled since earlier landmark projects (e.g., the Frigg platform recycling roughly 15 years prior, referenced as a scale benchmark by one major contractor).

### Heavy-Lift Removal Methods

**Key Points**

- There are two main concepts of removal: reverse installation (essentially the installation sequence performed in reverse, typically using conventional crane vessels and often requiring cutting structures into smaller sections) and single lift (removing an entire topside or jacket as one piece using a purpose-built heavy-lift vessel). The elements of the project are the same regardless of which concept is used, but the vessels involved need large lifting capacities in either case.
- Single-lift designs that can remove topsides and jackets in one piece can substantially improve project efficiency, because they significantly reduce the man-hours spent on offshore cleaning, partitioning, installing lifting points, and rigging platform modules — work that would otherwise need to occur offshore under higher health, safety, and environmental (HSE) risk.
- Shifting work from offshore to onshore is a recurring theme in modern decommissioning method selection: onshore work is generally less expensive, faster, cleaner, and safer than equivalent offshore work, giving single-lift vessel concepts a better overall HSE and cost profile compared to methods requiring extensive at-sea dismantling.

#### Case example: Allseas Pioneering Spirit — Brent field decommissioning

**Key Points**

- Allseas' heavy-lift vessel Pioneering Spirit lifted 31,000 tonnes of Brent Charlie platform topsides in a single lift on 9 July (2024), transporting the load to a decommissioning and recycling yard in northeast England — described by Allseas as the heaviest offshore lift ever performed, concluding years of engineering and planning and completing removal of topsides from all four Brent field production platforms.
- Pioneering Spirit was purpose-built to install and remove offshore platforms in a single lift using an advanced motion-compensation system, and is capable of lifting entire topsides up to 48,000 tonnes (an upgrade in progress at time of reporting was set to increase this to 60,000 tonnes) and jackets up to 20,000 tonnes.
- Earlier in the Brent field decommissioning programme, Pioneering Spirit set a world lifting record removing the 24,000-tonne Brent Delta topside on 28 April 2017, with the entire lift operation — including move-in around the platform, yoke connection, de-ballasting, and the "fast lift" itself — taking just 12 hours; this followed the vessel's first decommissioning assignment, removal of the 13,500-tonne Repsol Norge-operated Yme mobile offshore production unit (MOPU) in August 2016.
- The same vessel is also actively used for installation work, illustrating a degree of asset flexibility between construction and decommissioning phases: prior to the Brent Charlie removal, Pioneering Spirit installed an offshore wind substation jacket and topside for Ocean Winds' Eoliennes en Mer Iles d'Yeu et de Noirmoutier windfarm project, transferring components directly in the field and installing them with its 5,000-tonne crane.

#### Additional decommissioning removal case examples

**Key Points**

- Allseas has been contracted to remove and decommission TAQA UK's Cormorant Alpha and Tern platform topsides from the North Sea starting in 2027, subcontracting UK fabrication yard Able Group to dismantle, reuse, and recycle the topsides at Able Seaton Port in Teesside; Able anticipates up to 97% of the recovered topsides' combined 47,500-tonne material weight will be reused or recycled. This follows a separate September 2025 contract for Allseas to remove the 33,000-tonne topside and 12,000-tonne upper jacket of TAQA's Brae Alpha platform in two separate campaigns.
- Heerema's heavy-lift vessels Sleipnir and Thialf were contracted to remove three North Sea platforms (Heimdal and Veslefrikk fields) with a combined topside-and-jacket weight of approximately 68,000 tonnes, with Aker Solutions handling reception, dismantling, and recycling, targeting recycling of about 98% of all materials.
- Sarens worked in cooperation with Allseas on the Valhall field decommissioning, outfitting its barge Louis with grillages to support approximately 3,850-tonne topside modules; Pioneering Spirit removed the topsides from the jacket and transferred them to the Sarens barge positioned below it, which then transported them to a disposal yard at Stord, Norway — illustrating a multi-contractor logistics chain (heavy-lift vessel operator, barge/transport contractor, and onshore recycling yard) typical of major decommissioning projects.
- For smaller platform fields, alternative barge-based concepts have been developed as a cost-effective alternative to conventional reverse installation using heavy-lift crane barges — one such concept was developed for removal of 90–100 minimum-facilities platform assets in the Gulf of Thailand with topside weights up to 800 tonnes and jacket dry weight up to 1,000 tonnes, using a barge design combining reverse float-over and on-board lifting methods to remove both topside and substructure with the same vessel.

### Circular Economy Framework: Learning from Oil & Gas for Offshore Wind

Oil and gas decommissioning has to date primarily focused on recycling as much of the resulting waste as possible; while preferable to disposal, this is not fully aligned with a genuine circular economy, where reuse is the optimal outcome. Industry analysis explicitly frames offshore wind's approaching decommissioning wave as an opportunity to apply lessons from oil and gas and avoid repeating a recycling-first, rather than reuse-first, approach.

**Key Points**

- Reusing oil and gas assets poses several challenges, including asset age and the loss of technical knowledge as assets change ownership over time — a knowledge-management problem as much as a physical/logistics one.
- The circular economy should be front and center of developers' minds from the earliest project stages; wind farms should be designed to maximize asset reuse at end of life, planning backward from the eventual decommissioning phase so that project life-cycle waste is minimized throughout.
- The ideal approach prioritizes reuse of entire assets where possible, followed by partial reuse or repurposing of individual components, with recycling considered only as a last resort once other options have been exhausted.
- During construction, methods that allow the removal, reuse, and repair of assets should be prioritized; during operation and maintenance, practices should ensure components are repaired or repurposed rather than simply replaced and scrapped; maintaining detailed maintenance records throughout the asset's life is equally important to prevent the knowledge loss that has hampered oil and gas asset reuse.
- A circular economy approach goes far beyond recycling alone — it also encompasses lifetime extension of components (e.g., through refurbishment), reuse and remanufacturing, and designing out waste and hard-to-recycle materials from the earliest design stage.

**Key Points**

- This hierarchy reflects the sourced framework directly: disassembly (taking components apart to enable repair, reuse, upgrading, remanufacturing, and recycling) should be considered at the design stage; repowering extends a wind farm's service life by replacing some or all turbine components; decommissioning itself covers de-energising, dismantling, and removal of some or all parts of a wind farm, followed by site restoration and monitoring; site recovery returns a site to a state similar to before development; and recycling covers collection and preparation of wastes into materials that can re-enter production.
- Current decommissioning plans for offshore wind are, per academic and industry review, at best formulaic and at worst perfunctory, and the offshore wind industry is described as almost exclusively planning end-of-life within the lower reaches of this hierarchy (i.e., recycle, energy recovery, and landfill) rather than the higher-value reuse and repowering tiers — indicating a gap between the circular economy framework being proposed and current standard industry practice.

### Material Recovery Challenges — The Blade Recycling Bottleneck

**Key Points**

- Around 85–90% of the weight of a wind turbine is already recyclable in theory; the major hurdle to full recyclability is the lack of at-scale recycling capacity for turbine blades, which are made of a composite of resins and fibres — a materials-science and industrial-capacity gap rather than a logistics gap per se, but one with direct logistics implications (blade transport to specialized recycling/processing facilities, rather than conventional scrap-metal recycling routes).
- Millions of tonnes of composites, precious metals, and rare earth materials are being extracted, processed, and deployed in offshore wind infrastructure, with — per the cited academic review — nothing yet in place at scale to suggest these materials can be sustainably recovered, managed, and returned to productive use at the scale required to match accelerating low-carbon infrastructure deployment.
- Beyond wind-specific materials, broader waste-electronics recovery context is instructive: less than 1% of rare earth elements (REEs) are currently recovered from end-of-life magnets, and only approximately 12.5% of all metals are recovered from all waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE) sources, with roughly 50 million tonnes of WEEE still sent to landfill annually — illustrating that the technical/industrial capacity gap for high-value material recovery is not unique to wind turbines but reflects a broader materials-recovery infrastructure challenge that offshore wind decommissioning will need to overcome at much larger physical scale.
- Industry programmes such as the UK's Circular Economy in the Wind Sector joint industry programme have targeted at-scale demonstration of wind turbine blade recycling within a five-year horizon from initiation, reflecting active investment in closing this specific capability gap.
- With an average lifespan of 20–25 years, many offshore wind turbines installed during the 1990s and early 2000s are now reaching end of life, and estimates suggest at least 22 GW of installed wind capacity will reach end of use by 2024 — indicating the logistics and materials-recovery infrastructure gap needs to close on a timeline that is already active rather than a distant future concern.

### Logistics and Transport Implications

**Key Points**

- **Heavy-lift vessel role in circular outcomes**: Because single-lift removal methods (e.g., Pioneering Spirit-class vessels) reduce offshore cutting and partitioning, they preserve components in larger, more intact sections when transported to shore — which directly supports higher-value reuse and repurposing pathways (as opposed to reverse-installation methods requiring extensive offshore cutting into smaller sections, which tends to push outcomes toward recycling or disposal by fragmenting components before they even reach an onshore facility capable of assessing reuse potential).
- **Multi-contractor logistics chains**: As illustrated by the Valhall case (heavy-lift vessel operator, barge/transport contractor, onshore disposal/recycling yard), decommissioning logistics typically require coordination across several specialized contractors, each handling a distinct phase (offshore lift, marine transport, onshore reception and processing) — planning for circular-economy outcomes (sorting for reuse vs. recycling vs. disposal) needs to be embedded across this full contractor chain, not left solely to the final onshore recycling yard.
- **Onshore reception facility capability as a logistics constraint**: The stated 97–98% material reuse/recycling rates achieved in cited North Sea projects (TAQA/Able Group, Heimdal/Veslefrikk/Aker Solutions) depend on the receiving onshore facility's capability to sort, dismantle, and route materials to appropriate reuse or recycling pathways — meaning heavy-lift transport planning (vessel selection, section sizing, transport routing) and onshore facility capability need to be co-planned rather than treated as sequential, independent phases.
- **Emerging wind-specific logistics gap**: Unlike oil and gas decommissioning, which has established heavy-lift vessel and onshore recycling yard infrastructure built up over decades, offshore wind decommissioning logistics infrastructure — particularly for blade transport to specialized composite-recycling facilities — is comparatively immature, representing both a current capability gap and a growth opportunity for heavy-lift and specialized logistics providers positioning early in this emerging segment.
- **Behavior may vary**: [Behavioral disclaimer] Decommissioning project scope, vessel selection, and achievable reuse/recycling percentages are highly project- and jurisdiction-specific, depending on structure design, material composition, regulatory requirements, and the receiving facility's capability; the figures and case examples cited here should be treated as illustrative of current practice and verified against project-specific decommissioning plans before being used for planning or bidding purposes.
