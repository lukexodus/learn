## The Kinked Demand Curve Model of Price Rigidity

### Overview

The kinked demand curve model is a theory of oligopoly behavior developed independently by **Paul Sweezy** (1939) in the United States and by **R.L. Hall and C.J. Hitch** (1939) in the United Kingdom. It seeks to explain a commonly observed phenomenon in oligopolistic markets: **price rigidity** — prices tend to remain stable at a prevailing level even when costs or demand conditions change modestly, without requiring formal collusion between firms.

The model does not explain how the prevailing price is initially determined; instead, it explains why, once established, that price tends to persist.

### Core Behavioral Assumption

The model rests on an **asymmetric conjecture** each oligopolist holds about how rivals will react to a price change:

- **If a firm raises its price above the prevailing level**: rivals will **not** follow, hoping to capture the price-raising firm's customers. The firm loses substantial market share, so demand is **highly elastic** above the current price.
- **If a firm lowers its price below the prevailing level**: rivals **will** match the cut to avoid losing their own customers. The firm gains little market share because competitors retaliate, so demand is **relatively inelastic** below the current price.

This asymmetric response creates a demand curve that is **kinked** at the prevailing market price $P^*$: flatter (more elastic) above $P^*$ and steeper (less elastic) below it.

### Deriving the Kinked Demand Curve

Let the prevailing price be $P^*$ and prevailing quantity be $Q^*$.

**Above $P^*$** (segment $D_1$): Since rivals do not follow a price increase, the firm faces the market's full elastic demand response — a small price increase causes a large loss of quantity demanded as customers switch to competitors.

**Below $P^*$** (segment $D_2$): Since rivals match a price decrease, the firm's relative price versus competitors doesn't change, so the quantity gained from a price cut is limited to overall market expansion — demand is comparatively inelastic.

The two segments meet at $(Q^*, P^*)$, forming a **kink**, with $D_1$ (elastic) above the kink and $D_2$ (inelastic) below it.

**Key Points:**

- The kink exists precisely because rival reactions differ depending on the *direction* of the price change.
- The steeper segment below the kink reflects the fact that undercutting rivals fails to generate the expected sales gain once they retaliate.
- The flatter segment above the kink reflects the harsh penalty for unilaterally raising price while rivals hold theirs steady.

### The Discontinuous Marginal Revenue Curve

The most important analytical feature of this model is the **marginal revenue (MR) curve**, which is **discontinuous** at the output level $Q^*$ corresponding to the kink.

Because the demand curve has two different slopes at $Q^*$, the corresponding MR curve has two separate segments:

- The MR segment associated with the **elastic** upper demand segment $D_1$ lies higher.
- The MR segment associated with the **inelastic** lower demand segment $D_2$ lies lower.
- At output $Q^*$, MR **drops vertically** — jumping down from the upper segment's MR value to the lower segment's MR value. This vertical drop is often called the **"gap"** in the MR curve.

### Why Prices Remain Rigid: The Marginal Cost Argument

The central conclusion of the model follows directly from the discontinuity in MR. A profit-maximizing firm sets output where $MC = MR$. Because the MR curve has a vertical gap at $Q^*$, the **marginal cost curve can shift up or down within that gap** without changing the profit-maximizing price or quantity.

$$MC_1 = MC_2 = MR \text{ (within the gap)} \implies P^*, Q^* \text{ unchanged}$$

This means that **moderate fluctuations in marginal cost** (e.g., due to input price changes) will **not** induce the firm to change its price, as long as the new MC curve still intersects the discontinuous MR segment somewhere within the vertical gap. Only a **sufficiently large** cost shock — one that pushes the MC curve outside the bounds of the gap — will cause the firm to change its price and output.

**Key Points:**

- Price rigidity emerges as a **consequence** of the discontinuous MR curve, not an independent assumption.
- The model similarly implies that **moderate shifts in demand** may leave the kink price unchanged, provided the general shape and position of the kink persist.
- This explains empirically observed "sticky prices" in many oligopolistic industries without requiring explicit collusion, menu costs, or contractual constraints.

### Numerical Illustration

Suppose an oligopolist's kinked demand curve is:

- **Above the kink** ($Q < Q^* = 100$): $P = 120 - 0.8Q$ (elastic segment)
- **Below the kink** ($Q > Q^* = 100$): $P = 100 - 0.4Q$ (inelastic segment, calibrated so both segments pass through $(100, 40)$)

**Marginal revenue for the elastic segment**: For $P = 120 - 0.8Q$, total revenue $TR = 120Q - 0.8Q^2$, so:

$$MR_1 = 120 - 1.6Q$$

At $Q = 100$: $MR_1 = 120 - 160 = -40$

**Marginal revenue for the inelastic segment**: For $P = 100 - 0.4Q$, $TR = 100Q - 0.4Q^2$, so:

$$MR_2 = 100 - 0.8Q$$

At $Q = 100$: $MR_2 = 100 - 80 = 20$

**The MR gap** at $Q^* = 100$ spans from $-40$ to $20$ — a vertical range of $60$. Any marginal cost curve that intersects $Q = 100$ at an MC value between $-40$ and $20$ (in this illustrative setup) leaves price and quantity unchanged. Only if MC rises above $20$ or falls below $-40$ at that output would the firm find it profitable to adjust price.

**[Inference]** The specific numerical width of the MR gap is highly sensitive to the assumed elasticities of the two demand segments; empirical estimation of a real firm's "gap" would require estimating both segments' elasticities separately, which is difficult in practice.

### Strengths of the Model

- Provides an intuitive, low-information explanation for observed price stability in oligopolistic markets without invoking formal collusion.
- Consistent with survey-based evidence (originally gathered by Hall and Hitch) showing that many business managers were reluctant to change prices in response to modest cost or demand shifts.
- Highlights the importance of **conjectural variation** — how a firm's strategy depends on beliefs about rivals' likely responses — as a general principle in oligopoly theory.

### Criticisms and Limitations

- **Does not explain how $P^*$ is originally determined.** The model only explains price *stickiness* around an existing equilibrium price, not how that price arose in the first place — a significant theoretical gap.
- **Weak empirical support for the specific asymmetric-conjecture mechanism.** Some studies find that price changes in oligopolies are not necessarily rarer than in other market structures, and that observed rigidity may stem from other sources such as **menu costs**, **long-term contracts**, or **implicit collusion**, rather than the specific kink mechanism.
- **Static model.** It does not incorporate dynamic or repeated-game considerations, nor does it explain how firms coordinate on $P^*$ over time.
- **Kink may not always exist as sharply as modeled.** Rival responses to price changes may be more nuanced or gradual than the strict "always match cuts, never match increases" assumption.
- Critics (e.g., George Stigler, in later empirical work) questioned whether real-world pricing data actually display the sharp asymmetric response the model predicts.

### Distinguishing the Kinked Demand Model from Other Oligopoly Models

| Feature | Kinked Demand | Cournot | Stackelberg | Cartel/Collusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strategic variable | Price (with conjectures about rivals) | Quantity | Quantity (sequential) | Price/output (joint) |
| Explains price level | No | Yes (via reaction functions) | Yes | Yes (joint profit max) |
| Explains price rigidity | Yes (central result) | No | No | Partially (via enforcement) |
| Requires explicit coordination | No | No | No | Yes |

### Real-World Relevance

- Frequently cited in discussions of **sticky prices** in industries such as airlines, gasoline retailing (asymmetric "rockets and feathers" pricing), and consumer packaged goods, where firms are hesitant to be the first mover on a price increase.
- Used pedagogically to introduce the broader concept that in oligopoly, **a firm's optimal action depends critically on its beliefs about rivals' reactions** — a foundational idea later formalized more rigorously in game theory (e.g., Nash equilibrium, conjectural variations models).

**[Inference]** Because the model does not specify a dynamic adjustment process or a game-theoretic equilibrium concept, most modern industrial organization economists treat it as a useful heuristic for describing price stickiness rather than as a rigorous predictive model of oligopoly pricing.
