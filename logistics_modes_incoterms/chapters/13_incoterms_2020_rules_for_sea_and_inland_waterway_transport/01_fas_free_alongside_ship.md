## FAS (Free Alongside Ship)

### Overview

FAS (Free Alongside Ship) requires the seller to deliver goods by placing them alongside the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment — for example, on a quay or barge alongside the ship. FAS is restricted to sea and inland waterway transport and is most commonly used for bulk or break-bulk cargo where goods are loaded directly at the ship's side without being placed in a container.

### Applicable Transport Mode

- FAS belongs to the "Sea and Inland Waterway Transport" category exclusively. It should not be used for containerized cargo delivered to a container terminal in advance of vessel loading, since the delivery point (alongside the vessel) does not correspond to typical container-yard handover practices.

### Key Points: Obligation Summary

- **Delivery point**: alongside the vessel nominated by the buyer, at the named port of shipment (e.g., on the quay, a barge, or lighter positioned next to the ship).
- **Risk transfer**: occurs when the goods are placed alongside the ship — notably, this is *before* the goods are loaded onto the vessel, distinguishing FAS from FOB.
- **Loading onto vessel**: the buyer's responsibility and risk once goods are alongside the ship, even though the buyer typically also arranges the vessel itself.
- **Export clearance**: the seller's responsibility.
- **Import clearance**: the buyer's responsibility.
- **Carriage**: the buyer arranges and pays for the ocean/waterway carriage from the port of shipment onward; the seller has no carriage obligation.
- **Insurance**: neither party is obligated to insure under FAS itself.

### Article-by-Article Breakdown (Seller/Buyer)

| Article | Seller Obligation (A) | Buyer Obligation (B) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. General Obligations | Provide goods and invoice per contract | Pay the price |
| 2. Delivery | Place goods alongside vessel at named port | Take delivery once alongside vessel |
| 3. Transfer of Risk | Ends when goods placed alongside vessel | Begins at same point |
| 4. Carriage | No obligation to contract carriage | Contract and pay for carriage from port of shipment |
| 5. Insurance | No obligation | No obligation, but buyer bears risk from alongside-vessel point |
| 6. Delivery/Transport Document | Provide proof goods delivered alongside vessel | Accept conforming document |
| 7. Export/Import Clearance | Handle export clearance | Handle import clearance |
| 8. Checking/Packaging/Marking | Package and mark goods appropriately | No standard obligation |
| 9. Allocation of Costs | Costs up to placing goods alongside vessel | Loading costs, carriage, and costs from that point onward |
| 10. Notices | Notify buyer once goods delivered alongside vessel | Notify seller of vessel name, loading point, and required delivery time |

### Example

A timber exporter in Malaysia sells raw logs to a buyer in China under "FAS Port Klang Incoterms® 2020." The seller transports the logs to Port Klang and positions them alongside the vessel nominated by the buyer, typically on the quay or via barge. At that moment, risk transfers to the buyer — even though the logs have not yet been loaded onto the ship. The buyer's stevedores or the vessel's crew then load the logs on board, and if any damage occurs during this loading process, that loss falls on the buyer, since risk had already transferred at the alongside-vessel point.

### FAS vs. FOB: Key Distinction

| Feature | FAS | FOB |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delivery/risk transfer point | Goods placed alongside vessel | Goods loaded on board vessel |
| Loading onto vessel | Buyer's risk and typically buyer's cost | Seller's risk and cost (loading is part of seller's delivery obligation) |
| Typical cargo | Bulk/break-bulk requiring dockside handling before loading | Bulk/break-bulk loaded directly aboard |

- [Inference] The distinction between FAS and FOB is often less significant in practice for many bulk cargoes than the formal rule text suggests, since loading typically occurs shortly after goods are positioned alongside the vessel; however, the allocation of risk during that loading operation itself is the legally meaningful difference between the two terms.

### Why FAS Is Rarely Used for Containerized Cargo

- [Inference] Container shipments are typically delivered to a container terminal or yard well in advance of vessel loading — often days ahead — making the "alongside the vessel" concept impractical or ambiguous to apply, since there is no clear moment when containerized goods are positioned directly next to a specific ship.
- For this reason, **FCA** is the standard recommended alternative for containerized sea freight, since its delivery point (handover to the carrier at a named place) aligns with actual container-yard logistics practice.

### When FAS Is Typically Used

- Bulk commodity trade (grain, timber, ore, minerals) where goods are delivered to the dockside or barge alongside the vessel before loading.
- Break-bulk cargo (large individual items not containerized) where dockside positioning precedes crane-loading onto the vessel.
- Trades where the seller has strong port-side logistics capability but prefers the buyer to arrange and control the vessel and loading operation.

### Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception**: FAS can be used for any ocean shipment, including containers.

  **Clarification**: FAS is designed for cargo delivered directly alongside a vessel (typically bulk or break-bulk); container shipments delivered to a terminal in advance of loading are better served by FCA.
- **Misconception**: Under FAS, the seller is responsible for loading the goods onto the vessel.

  **Clarification**: The seller's obligation ends once goods are placed alongside the vessel; loading onto the vessel is the buyer's risk and responsibility under FAS.
- **Misconception**: FAS and FOB have the same risk-transfer point.

  **Clarification**: FAS transfers risk when goods are placed alongside the vessel (before loading); FOB transfers risk only once goods are loaded on board — a materially later point.

**Key Points**

- FAS requires the seller to deliver goods alongside the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment; risk transfers before loading onto the vessel.
- Restricted to sea and inland waterway transport; best suited to bulk or break-bulk cargo, not containerized shipments.
- Buyer arranges and pays for loading onto the vessel and all subsequent ocean carriage.
- FCA is the recommended alternative for containerized cargo where the "alongside vessel" concept does not apply practically.
