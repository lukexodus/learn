## DDP (Delivered Duty Paid)

### Overview

DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) represents the maximum obligation for the seller among all 11 Incoterms 2020 rules. Under DDP, the seller must deliver goods to the named destination, cleared for import, with all import duties and taxes paid, ready for unloading by the buyer. DDP is the direct mirror image of EXW: where EXW places nearly every obligation on the buyer, DDP places nearly every obligation on the seller.

### Applicable Transport Mode

- DDP belongs to the "Any Mode or Modes of Transport" category, suitable for road, rail, air, sea, or multimodal combinations.

### Key Points: Obligation Summary

- **Delivery point**: the named place of destination specified in the contract, which may be any location the parties agree upon.
- **Risk transfer**: occurs at the named destination, when goods are made available to the buyer, ready for unloading, having been cleared for import — the seller does not unload under DDP (unlike DPU).
- **Export clearance**: the seller's responsibility.
- **Import clearance**: the seller's responsibility, including obtaining any import licenses or permits required.
- **Import duties and taxes**: the seller's responsibility — this is DDP's defining feature and the source of its maximum-obligation status. This typically includes customs duties, and may include import-stage value-added tax (VAT) or goods and services tax (GST) depending on the destination country's tax regime and how the contract characterizes the seller's obligations.
- **Carriage**: the seller arranges and pays for the entire carriage to the named destination.
- **Unloading**: the buyer's responsibility (goods are made available ready for unloading, not already unloaded).
- **Insurance**: neither party is obligated to insure under DDP itself; the seller bears risk for the entire journey and should insure accordingly.

### Article-by-Article Breakdown (Seller/Buyer)

| Article | Seller Obligation (A) | Buyer Obligation (B) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. General Obligations | Provide goods and invoice per contract | Pay the price |
| 2. Delivery | Make goods available at named destination, cleared for import, ready for unloading | Take delivery at named destination |
| 3. Transfer of Risk | Ends when goods ready for unloading, import-cleared, at destination | Begins at same point |
| 4. Carriage | Contract and pay for carriage to named destination | No obligation to contract carriage |
| 5. Insurance | No obligation, but bears full transit risk | No obligation |
| 6. Delivery/Transport Document | Provide transport document as needed | Accept conforming document |
| 7. Export/Import Clearance | Handle BOTH export AND import clearance, including licenses | Assist with information seller may need; no clearance duty |
| 8. Checking/Packaging/Marking | Package and mark goods appropriately | No standard obligation |
| 9. Allocation of Costs | All costs to named destination, including duties and import taxes | Unloading costs only |
| 10. Notices | Notify buyer of dispatch and expected arrival | Notify seller of any buyer-controlled requirements |

### Example

A consumer electronics distributor in South Korea sells to a retail buyer in the United States under "DDP Los Angeles Warehouse Incoterms® 2020." The seller arranges and pays for the entire journey: trucking to the port, ocean freight, Korean export clearance, U.S. import clearance, and payment of all applicable U.S. customs duties and import taxes. The goods arrive at the buyer's warehouse ready for unloading; the buyer's only obligation is to unload the truck. If U.S. customs authorities assess unexpected additional duties due to a tariff classification dispute, the seller — not the buyer — bears that cost under DDP.

### Practical Risks and Considerations

- **Regulatory and tax complexity**: [Inference] DDP requires the seller to navigate the buyer's country's import regulations, customs classification (e.g., Harmonized System codes), applicable tariffs, and potentially import-stage VAT/GST — areas where a foreign seller may lack the same familiarity as a local buyer, creating risk of misclassification, delays, or unexpected costs.
- **Sanctions and export control exposure**: sellers using DDP into unfamiliar jurisdictions should verify there are no import prohibitions, licensing requirements, or sanctions-related restrictions that could prevent customs clearance, since the seller bears the obligation and risk of completing this clearance.
- **VAT/GST treatment varies by jurisdiction**: **[Unverified]** In some jurisdictions, the seller may face practical difficulty becoming a registered importer of record or reclaiming import VAT, depending on local tax registration requirements; sellers should consult local tax and customs advisors in the destination country before committing to DDP, as this varies significantly by country and is subject to change.
- **Use of a local customs broker or import agent**: sellers frequently engage a licensed customs broker or agent in the destination country to handle the practical mechanics of import clearance and duty payment, since acting as importer of record often requires local registration or representation.
- **Alternative for uncertain jurisdictions**: sellers uncertain about a destination country's import requirements often prefer **DAP**, which shifts import clearance and duty payment back to the buyer while still offering a delivered service level for carriage and risk.

### DDP vs. DAP vs. DPU: Full Comparison

| Feature | DAP | DPU | DDP |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unloading | Buyer | **Seller** | Buyer |
| Import clearance | Buyer | Buyer | **Seller** |
| Import duties/taxes | Buyer | Buyer | **Seller** |
| Risk transfer point | Ready for unloading | After unloading | Ready for unloading, import-cleared |
| Overall seller obligation | High | High | **Maximum (all 11 rules)** |

### When DDP Is Typically Used

- Sellers offering a "landed cost" or "delivered price" commercial model, where the buyer pays one all-inclusive price with no additional customs or duty surprises.
- E-commerce and direct-to-consumer international sales, where individual consumer buyers are unequipped to handle customs clearance and duty payment themselves.
- Sellers with established logistics infrastructure, customs brokerage relationships, or in-house compliance expertise in the destination market.
- Situations where the buyer explicitly requires a fully delivered, duty-paid price for budgeting or regulatory reasons.

### Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception**: DDP means the seller has no further obligations once goods depart the seller's country.

  **Clarification**: The seller retains risk and responsibility for the entire journey, including import clearance and duty payment, until goods are made available at the named destination.
- **Misconception**: DDP requires the seller to unload the goods, like DPU.

  **Clarification**: DDP requires goods to be made available ready for unloading; the buyer performs the actual unloading, unlike DPU where the seller unloads.
- **Misconception**: DDP is always the best option for buyers since it shifts maximum obligation to the seller.

  **Clarification**: [Inference] While DDP minimizes buyer logistics burden, sellers unfamiliar with the buyer's customs regime may face delays, misclassification, or compliance issues that could ultimately affect delivery timing or costs passed through in pricing; DAP or CIP may be more practical where the buyer has stronger local import capability.

**Key Points**

- DDP represents the maximum seller obligation among all Incoterms rules: seller handles carriage, export and import clearance, and all import duties/taxes to the named destination.
- Applicable to any transport mode; buyer's only responsibility is unloading upon arrival.
- Sellers should verify customs, tax registration, and import compliance capability in the destination country before committing to DDP, often via a local customs broker.
- DAP is a common alternative when the seller wants to avoid unfamiliar import clearance and duty obligations while still offering delivered carriage.
