## EXW (Ex Works)

### Overview

EXW (Ex Works) represents the minimum obligation for the seller among all 11 Incoterms 2020 rules. Under EXW, the seller's sole duty is to make the goods available at their own premises (or another named place, such as a factory or warehouse) — the buyer assumes responsibility for essentially the entire logistics chain from that point forward, including loading, export clearance, main carriage, import clearance, and final delivery.

### Applicable Transport Mode

- EXW belongs to the "Any Mode or Modes of Transport" category, meaning it can be used regardless of whether the goods move by road, rail, air, sea, or a multimodal combination.

### Key Points: Obligation Summary

- **Delivery point**: the seller's premises or another named place (e.g., factory, warehouse, or production facility) specified in the contract.
- **Risk transfer**: at the moment the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the named place, before loading onto any collecting vehicle.
- **Loading**: the seller is not obligated to load the goods onto the buyer's collecting vehicle; loading is the buyer's responsibility and risk, unless the parties otherwise agree by contract modification.
- **Export clearance**: the buyer is responsible for export clearance formalities, licenses, and any related costs — an unusual feature since the seller, being local to the origin country, is typically far better positioned to manage this.
- **Import clearance**: the buyer's responsibility.
- **Carriage**: the buyer arranges and pays for the entire transport chain from the seller's premises to final destination.
- **Insurance**: neither party is obligated to insure under the rule itself; each party bears the practical risk exposure created by the risk-transfer point and should insure accordingly.

### Article-by-Article Breakdown (Seller/Buyer)

| Article | Seller Obligation (A) | Buyer Obligation (B) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. General Obligations | Provide goods and invoice per contract | Pay the price |
| 2. Delivery | Place goods at buyer's disposal at named place | Take delivery once placed at disposal |
| 3. Transfer of Risk | Risk ends when goods placed at buyer's disposal | Risk begins at same point |
| 4. Carriage | No obligation to contract carriage | Arrange and pay for entire carriage |
| 5. Insurance | No obligation | No obligation, but buyer bears full transit risk exposure |
| 6. Delivery/Transport Document | No obligation to provide transport document | Provide seller proof of collection, if relevant |
| 7. Export/Import Clearance | No obligation (buyer handles export clearance) | Handle export AND import clearance |
| 8. Checking/Packaging/Marking | Package goods appropriately (unless custom of trade suggests otherwise) | No standard obligation |
| 9. Allocation of Costs | Costs only up to placing goods at buyer's disposal | All costs from that point, including loading, export/import duties, and carriage |
| 10. Notices | Notify buyer of when/where goods are available | Notify seller of time for collection |

### Example

A small electronics component manufacturer in Shenzhen sells to a buyer in Mexico under "EXW Shenzhen Incoterms® 2020." The seller's obligation ends once the goods are packaged and made available for collection at the factory gate. The buyer must arrange a trucking company to collect the goods, handle Chinese export clearance and any required export licenses, book ocean freight to Mexico, pay for marine insurance if desired, and handle Mexican import clearance and duties upon arrival.

### Practical Limitations and Common Modifications

- [Inference] EXW's strict application creates significant practical friction because most sellers, being physically located at the point of origin, have far greater knowledge of local customs procedures, licensing requirements, and export documentation than a foreign buyer would. As a result, EXW is frequently modified in practice through side agreements or explicit contract language where the seller agrees to assist with loading and export formalities despite this exceeding the rule's literal minimum obligations.
- Loading risk is a frequently cited point of dispute: because the seller has no obligation to load goods onto the collecting vehicle, if the seller's staff assist with loading as a courtesy and damage occurs during that process, liability can become contested unless the contract clarifies this scenario.
- **[Unverified]** Some jurisdictions or standard trade financing arrangements (e.g., certain letter of credit structures) may be difficult to satisfy under strict EXW terms because the buyer, not the seller, controls export documentation — parties relying on documentary credit financing should confirm with their bank whether EXW creates practical complications before finalizing contract terms.
- For containerized cargo or situations requiring export compliance expertise, **FCA** is frequently recommended as a more practical alternative, since it shifts export clearance back to the seller while still transferring risk relatively early in the logistics chain.

### When EXW Is Typically Used

- Domestic or regional transactions where the buyer has strong local logistics capability or its own freight-forwarding network in the seller's country.
- Transactions where the buyer wants maximum control over the entire supply chain, including carrier selection and routing.
- Situations where the seller has minimal logistics infrastructure or expertise and prefers to limit involvement to production and packaging only.

### Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception**: EXW is the cheapest or most favorable option for sellers in all cases.

  **Clarification**: While EXW minimizes the seller's logistics obligations, it does not necessarily minimize commercial risk — sellers may face customs or contractual complications since they are relying entirely on the buyer's compliance capability for export formalities occurring in the seller's own country.
- **Misconception**: The seller must load the goods onto the truck or container under EXW.

  **Clarification**: Under the strict rule, loading is the buyer's risk and responsibility; any seller assistance with loading is typically a practical courtesy, not a contractual obligation, unless the contract specifies otherwise.
- **Misconception**: EXW works well for letter-of-credit-financed transactions.

  **Clarification**: **[Unverified]** Because the buyer, not the seller, controls export clearance and shipping documentation under EXW, this can create friction with certain trade finance structures that expect the seller to provide specific shipping documents; parties should verify compatibility with their financing arrangement.

**Key Points**

- EXW is the minimum-obligation term: seller delivers by making goods available at their own premises; buyer bears virtually all subsequent cost, risk, loading, and clearance responsibility.
- Applicable to any transport mode.
- Export clearance falls on the buyer under strict EXW, which is often impractical and commonly modified in practice.
- FCA is the commonly recommended alternative when export clearance expertise or documentation needs favor seller involvement.
