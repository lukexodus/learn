## Realism and Neorealism

### Overview

Realism is a paradigm in International Relations (IR) theory that centers state behavior on the pursuit of power and security within an anarchic international system. Neorealism (also called Structural Realism), developed primarily by Kenneth Waltz, reformulates classical realism by grounding state behavior in the structure of the international system rather than in human nature. Together these frameworks form one of the oldest and most influential lenses for analyzing interstate conflict, alliance formation, and the balance of power.

### Classical Realism

**Key Points**

- Rooted in the belief that human nature is inherently self-interested and power-seeking (a claim traced to Thucydides, Machiavelli, and Hobbes)
- Hans Morgenthau's *Politics Among Nations* (1948) is considered the foundational text of 20th-century classical realism
- States are treated as unitary actors whose foreign policy is driven by leaders pursuing "interest defined in terms of power"
- Morality in international affairs is subordinated to the requirements of survival and prudence (raison d'état)
- International politics is a perpetual struggle for power because the desire to dominate is embedded in human psychology, not merely circumstance

**Morgenthau's Six Principles of Political Realism**

1. Politics is governed by objective laws rooted in human nature
2. The main signpost of political realism is the concept of interest defined as power
3. Interest defined as power is a universal category, but its content is not fixed
4. Universal moral principles cannot be applied to state actions in their abstract form
5. Realism refuses to identify the moral aspirations of a particular nation with universal moral law
6. The political sphere is autonomous from other spheres (legal, economic, moral)

### Neorealism (Structural Realism)

**Key Points**

- Introduced by Kenneth Waltz in *Theory of International Politics* (1979)
- Shifts explanation from human nature to the **structure of the international system**
- Three defining structural elements:
  1. **Ordering principle**: Anarchy (absence of a central authority above states)
  2. **Character of units**: States are functionally similar (all perform the same basic tasks of survival)
  3. **Distribution of capabilities**: The relative power distribution among states (unipolar, bipolar, multipolar) is the primary variable explaining outcomes
- States are treated as "black boxes" — internal regime type, ideology, and leadership are considered secondary to systemic pressures
- The central goal of states is **survival**, not power maximization for its own sake (a key distinction from classical realism)

**Waltzian Structure Formula**

$$S = f(O, U, D)$$

Where system outcomes ($S$) are a function of the ordering principle ($O$), the character of units ($U$), and the distribution of capabilities ($D$). Waltz treats $U$ as effectively constant across the system, leaving $D$ as the primary explanatory variable.

### Defensive vs. Offensive Neorealism

| Dimension | Defensive Realism (Waltz) | Offensive Realism (Mearsheimer) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core motivation | Security maximization | Power maximization |
| State behavior | Seeks "appropriate" amount of power to maintain security | Seeks hegemony as the ultimate guarantee of survival |
| Key text | *Theory of International Politics* (1979) | *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001) |
| View on expansion | Excessive expansion is often counterproductive (triggers balancing) | Expansion is rational when it can be gotten away with |
| Systemic tendency | Balance of power tends toward equilibrium | Great powers perpetually seek regional/global hegemony |

John Mearsheimer's offensive realism argues that under anarchy, uncertainty about others' intentions makes maximizing relative power the most rational survival strategy, since a state can never be certain it has "enough" power.

### Core Assumptions Shared Across Realist Variants

- **Anarchy**: No supranational authority enforces rules or guarantees state security
- **State-centrism**: States are the principal actors in international politics
- **Rationality**: States act as rational, unitary actors pursuing their interests
- **Self-help**: States must rely on their own capabilities for security, since no external body will reliably intervene
- **Primacy of security/survival**: Security concerns dominate the state's hierarchy of interests

### The Security Dilemma

A central mechanism in realist and neorealist thought, described by John Herz:

**Key Points**

- Under anarchy, one state's efforts to increase its own security (e.g., through military buildup) are perceived as threatening by other states
- This perception triggers reciprocal buildups, resulting in a net decrease in security for all parties despite no state seeking conflict
- Explains arms races and spiraling tensions even between states with defensive intentions

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[State A increases military capability for defense] --> B[State B perceives threat]
    B --> C[State B increases military capability]
    C --> D[State A perceives renewed threat]
    D --> A
    D --> E[Net system-wide insecurity rises]
```

### Balance of Power Theory

**Key Points**

- Central proposition: states will act to prevent any single state or coalition from achieving preponderant power
- Mechanisms:
  - **Internal balancing**: increasing one's own military/economic capabilities
  - **External balancing**: forming alliances against a rising threat
- Waltz argues balancing is more common than "bandwagoning" (aligning with the stronger power) because balancing better preserves state autonomy
- **Bandwagoning** (associated with Stephen Walt's *balance of threat* refinement) may occur when a state perceives greater safety in aligning with, rather than against, a rising power

**Balance of Threat Refinement (Walt)**

Walt argued raw power distribution is insufficient; states balance against **threat**, a function of:

- Aggregate power
- Geographic proximity
- Offensive capability
- Perceived intentions

### Polarity and System Stability

**Key Points**

- **Unipolarity**: one dominant power (debate exists over its stability; realists disagree on whether unipolar systems are inherently unstable due to counterbalancing)
- **Bipolarity**: two dominant powers (Waltz argued this was the most stable configuration, citing the Cold War's "long peace")
- **Multipolarity**: three or more great powers (considered by many neorealists to be more prone to miscalculation and shifting alliances, though this remains contested)

[Inference] The claim that bipolarity is inherently more stable than multipolarity is a widely cited but empirically contested proposition within the field, and other scholars (e.g., using different case selections) have reached different conclusions about relative stability.

### Example: Applying Neorealism to a Case

**Example**

Cold War bipolarity (1947–1991) is a canonical neorealist case study:

- Two superpowers (US, USSR) with roughly comparable capabilities produced a stable, if tense, structure
- Proxy conflicts (Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan) allowed competition without direct superpower war, consistent with balance-of-power logic
- The relative absence of great-power war during this period is cited by Waltz as evidence for bipolar stability, though the causal weight of nuclear deterrence versus polarity itself remains debated among scholars [Unverified as a matter of settled causal attribution]

### Major Critiques of Realism/Neorealism

**Key Points**

- **Liberal institutionalist critique**: underestimates the role of international institutions, interdependence, and cooperation in mitigating anarchy's effects
- **Constructivist critique** (Alexander Wendt): "anarchy is what states make of it" — structure does not have fixed, material effects independent of intersubjective norms and identities
- **Domestic-politics critiques**: neorealism's "black-boxing" of the state ignores regime type, bureaucratic politics, and leader psychology, all of which affect foreign policy outcomes (democratic peace theory is often raised as a direct counter-example)
- **Feminist IR critique**: challenges the gendered assumptions embedded in concepts like power, security, and autonomy
- **Neoclassical realist response**: attempts to reincorporate unit-level (domestic) variables as intervening factors between systemic pressures and actual foreign policy behavior, partially addressing this critique while retaining structural primacy

### Neorealism vs. Neoliberal Institutionalism (Summary Comparison)

| Dimension | Neorealism | Neoliberal Institutionalism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| View of anarchy | Generates persistent security competition | Can be mitigated through institutions and repeated interaction |
| Primary state concern | Relative gains | Absolute gains |
| Role of institutions | Epiphenomenal, reflect underlying power distribution | Independently shape state behavior and reduce transaction costs |
| Cooperation prospects | Limited, fragile | Achievable and durable under the right institutional design |
