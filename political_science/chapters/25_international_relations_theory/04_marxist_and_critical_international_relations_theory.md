## Marxist and Critical International Relations Theory


### Overview

Marxist and Critical International Relations Theory encompasses a family of approaches that reject the state-centric, materially-narrow, and purportedly value-neutral premises of mainstream (realist, liberal) IR theory. These approaches analyze international politics through the lens of class relations, capitalist production, structural inequality, and emancipation, treating theory itself as inherently tied to political interests rather than a neutral description of "how the world works."

### Classical Marxist Foundations

**Key Points**

- Rooted in Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels' historical materialism: political and social structures are ultimately shaped by the mode of production and class relations within it
- Marx himself wrote relatively little directly on interstate relations; the application to IR was developed substantially by later theorists (Lenin, Luxemburg, Hilferding)
- V.I. Lenin's *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917) reframed WWI-era great-power conflict as a product of capitalist competition for markets, raw materials, and investment outlets, driven by monopoly finance capital seeking overseas expansion
- Core claim: interstate conflict and colonial expansion are not autonomous political phenomena but expressions of underlying capitalist economic dynamics

### World-Systems Theory (Wallerstein)

**Key Points**

- Developed by Immanuel Wallerstein, most comprehensively in *The Modern World-System* (1974)
- The global economy is conceived as a single integrated capitalist system divided into three structural zones:
  - **Core**: technologically advanced, capital-intensive, high-wage economies that dominate global production and finance
  - **Periphery**: economies subordinated to producing raw materials and low-wage labor for core consumption, structurally locked into dependency
  - **Semi-periphery**: an intermediate zone exhibiting characteristics of both, often serving as a buffer that stabilizes the system by preventing a stark core-periphery polarization
- Unlike Marxist theories centered on the nation-state, world-systems theory analyzes capitalism as a **single historical system operating at the global level since roughly the "long 16th century"**
- Position within this global division of labor, rather than internal national policy alone, is treated as the principal determinant of a country's developmental trajectory [Inference: this is the central and most contested claim of the theory, disputed by scholars emphasizing domestic institutions as at least co-equal determinants]

### Dependency Theory

**Key Points**

- Emerged primarily from Latin American structuralist economics (Raúl Prebisch) and was subsequently radicalized by dependency theorists such as André Gunder Frank
- Central claim: underdevelopment in the periphery is not a "stage" that peripheral states will naturally pass through en route to development (contra modernization theory), but is actively **produced and reproduced** by their structural relationship with core economies
- Frank's concept of the "development of underdevelopment": peripheral economies are systematically drained of surplus value through unequal terms of trade, extraction, and capital flight to the core
- Distinguished from world-systems theory primarily by its narrower geographic/historical focus (originally Latin America) and its more explicit policy prescriptions (e.g., import substitution industrialization)

### Gramscian/Neo-Gramscian IR (Critical Theory, Cox)

**Key Points**

- Robert Cox's foundational distinction, from "Social Forces, States and World Orders" (1981): **problem-solving theory** versus **critical theory**
  - **Problem-solving theory** takes existing institutions and power relations as the given framework for action and seeks to make that framework function more smoothly (associated with mainstream realism/liberalism)
  - **Critical theory** questions how existing institutions and power relations came about in the first place and whether they are in the process of changing, explicitly oriented toward emancipatory possibility
- Cox's central methodological claim: "theory is always for someone and for some purpose" — there is no view from nowhere; all theory reflects and serves particular social and political interests
- Draws on Antonio Gramsci's concept of **hegemony**: dominant classes and states maintain control not primarily through coercion but through the construction of **consent**, achieved by embedding their particular interests within institutions, ideas, and culture that come to be seen as universal common sense
- Applied to IR, hegemony describes how a dominant state (e.g., historically the U.S. in the post-WWII order) sustains international order less through raw coercive power than through building a broadly accepted set of institutions, norms, and ideological common sense (e.g., liberal free-trade orthodoxy) that serves core capitalist interests while appearing neutral or beneficial to all

### The Frankfurt School Connection

**Key Points**

- Neo-Gramscian and critical IR theory more broadly draws on the **Frankfurt School** tradition (Horkheimer, Adorno, Habermas), particularly its distinction between traditional and critical theory
- Jürgen Habermas's concept of **emancipatory knowledge interest** underlies critical IR's normative commitment: theory should serve human emancipation from unnecessary domination, not merely explain or predict state behavior
- This traces critical IR's lineage away from positivist social science and toward a explicitly normative, transformative theoretical project

### Comparison of Major Strands

| Strand | Key Theorist(s) | Unit of Analysis | Central Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classical Marxist/Leninist Imperialism | Lenin, Luxemburg, Hilferding | Capitalist states and finance capital | Interstate conflict driven by competition for markets/resources |
| World-Systems Theory | Wallerstein | Single global capitalist system | Structural position (core/semi-periphery/periphery) determines development |
| Dependency Theory | Prebisch, Frank | Peripheral (originally Latin American) economies | Unequal exchange actively produces underdevelopment |
| Neo-Gramscian/Critical Theory | Cox, Gill | Social forces, ideas, institutions | Hegemony via manufactured consent, not just coercion |
| Frankfurt School-influenced Critical IR | Habermas (via Cox, Linklater) | Knowledge and normative theory itself | Emancipatory interest as the goal of theorizing |

### Example: Applying Neo-Gramscian Analysis to a Case

**Example**

The post-1945 Bretton Woods order (IMF, World Bank, GATT/WTO) is a frequently cited case in neo-Gramscian scholarship:

- These institutions are read not merely as neutral, efficiency-enhancing arrangements (as neoliberal institutionalists would frame them) but as instruments that embedded U.S.-led liberal capitalist norms as the presumptively universal framework for the postwar international economy
- The relative ease with which many states across the ideological spectrum accepted this framework is interpreted by neo-Gramscians as evidence of successful hegemonic consent-building rather than pure coercive imposition [Inference: this interpretation is one reading among competing accounts, including realist explanations emphasizing U.S. material power and public-goods-based liberal institutionalist accounts]

### Major Critiques of Marxist and Critical IR Theory

**Key Points**

- **Realist/liberal critique**: economic reductionism — critics argue class and capitalist dynamics cannot fully explain interstate conflict, which persists even among states with similar or shared economic systems
- **Empirical critique of dependency theory**: the rapid industrialization of several East Asian economies (the so-called "Asian Tigers") starting in the periphery/semi-periphery is frequently cited as a challenge to strict dependency claims about structural lock-in, though dependency theorists have offered various rejoinders regarding the specific historical conditions of those cases
- **Constructivist critique**: underplays the independent causal role of ideas, identity, and norms not directly reducible to class or material economic interest
- **Poststructuralist critique (from within critical theory)**: even Marxist and Gramscian approaches retain a totalizing, structurally deterministic account of history that other critical approaches (post-Marxism, poststructuralism) find insufficiently attentive to contingency and discursive power
- **Normative critique from mainstream IR**: explicit emancipatory commitment is sometimes viewed by positivist scholars as compromising claims to scientific objectivity, though critical theorists respond that all theory is unavoidably value-laden and that this transparency is itself an epistemic strength
