## Constructivism in International Relations


### Overview

Constructivism is a paradigm in International Relations theory holding that the fundamental structures of international politics are social rather than purely material, and that these structures shape actor identities and interests rather than merely constraining pre-given, fixed state preferences. Where neorealism and neoliberal institutionalism share a rationalist, materialist core and dispute only the consequences of anarchy, constructivism challenges the shared ontological premise itself: that state interests exist prior to and independent of social interaction.

### Foundational Premise

**Key Points**

- Alexander Wendt's famous formulation, "anarchy is what states make of it" (1992, *International Organization*), is the paradigm's signature claim
- Anarchy is not a fixed material condition with predetermined effects (as neorealists claim); its meaning and consequences depend on the shared understandings and social relationships among states
- Material factors (military capabilities, geography, resources) matter, but their significance is mediated by ideas, norms, and identities — a gun in the hands of a friend means something different than a gun in the hands of an enemy, even though the material fact (a gun) is identical

### Core Theoretical Commitments

**Key Points**

1. **Social construction of reality**: international structures are made of shared ideas, not just material capabilities
2. **Mutual constitution of agents and structures**: structures shape actors' identities and interests, while actors simultaneously reproduce or transform structures through their practices (a rejection of the one-directional causality found in neorealism)
3. **Identity precedes interest**: what a state wants (interest) is derived from who it understands itself to be (identity), which is itself socially constructed through interaction
4. **Norms matter**: shared expectations about appropriate behavior constrain and enable state action, sometimes independent of material incentives

### Wendt's Three Cultures of Anarchy

Wendt (*Social Theory of International Politics*, 1999) argues anarchy can take on qualitatively different "cultures" depending on the dominant intersubjective understanding among states:

| Culture | Dominant Logic | Role Relationship | Illustrative Historical Reference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hobbesian** | Enemy | States seek to destroy or dominate one another; war is unrestrained | European great-power conflict in certain historical periods |
| **Lockean** | Rival | States recognize each other's sovereignty and right to exist, but competition and limited conflict persist | The post-Westphalian state system broadly |
| **Kantian** | Friend | States settle disputes without war and cooperate on security; a genuine security community | Relations among contemporary Western European states |

**Key Points**

- Wendt argues the international system has historically moved from a Hobbesian toward a more Lockean and, in some regions, Kantian culture — a trajectory realists would consider theoretically anomalous
- The transition between cultures occurs through processes of identity change, not just shifts in material power distribution

### Constitutive vs. Causal Theorizing

**Key Points**

- Rationalist theories (realism, neoliberalism) ask **causal** questions: what factors produce a given outcome, treating actors and their interests as given?
- Constructivism additionally asks **constitutive** questions: what makes an actor, identity, or practice possible or intelligible in the first place?
- Example: rather than only asking "why did state sovereignty produce a particular balance-of-power outcome," constructivists ask "how did the very institution of sovereignty become the taken-for-granted unit of the international system?"

### Norm Life Cycle (Finnemore and Sikkink)

A widely used constructivist model for how international norms emerge, spread, and become embedded (*International Norm Dynamics and Political Change*, 1998):

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[Stage 1: Norm Emergence] --> B[Norm Entrepreneurs advocate via organizational platforms]
    B --> C[Tipping Point: Critical mass of state adopters reached]
    C --> D[Stage 2: Norm Cascade]
    D --> E[Socialization and imitation among states, often via international organizations]
    E --> F[Stage 3: Internalization]
    F --> G[Norm becomes taken-for-granted, no longer actively contested]
```

**Key Points**

- **Norm entrepreneurs**: individuals or organizations (often NGOs) that actively promote a new norm, frequently using moral argument and framing strategies
- **Tipping point**: typically theorized to occur once roughly one-third of relevant states adopt a norm, after which cascade dynamics accelerate adoption [Unverified as a precise universal threshold; the "one-third" figure is a heuristic offered in the original article rather than a strictly derived empirical constant]
- Examples commonly cited in the literature include the norm against chemical weapons use and the norm of decolonization/sovereign equality

### Securitization Theory (Copenhagen School)

A prominent constructivist-adjacent framework developed by Barry Buzan, Ole Wæver, and Jaap de Wilde:

**Key Points**

- Security is not an objective condition but a **speech act**: an issue becomes a "security" issue when a legitimate actor (the "securitizing actor") successfully frames it as an existential threat requiring extraordinary measures, and a relevant audience accepts that framing
- This reframes security studies constructively: what counts as a "threat" is discursively constructed, not simply discovered through objective threat assessment
- Desecuritization is the reverse process: moving an issue out of the emergency frame and back into normal political bargaining

### Constructivism vs. Rationalist Theories (Summary Comparison)

| Dimension | Constructivism | Realism/Neoliberalism (Rationalist) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ontology | Social/ideational | Material |
| State interests | Endogenous, socially constructed | Exogenous, given prior to interaction |
| Anarchy | Variable in meaning and effect | Fixed structural condition |
| Primary unit of analysis | Norms, identities, discourse | Material capabilities, institutions |
| Central question type | Constitutive ("how possible") | Causal ("what produces X") |
| Change over time | Structures can transform through identity shifts | Structures change mainly through shifts in material power distribution |

### Variants Within Constructivism

**Key Points**

- **Conventional/Modernist Constructivism** (Wendt, Finnemore): retains a moderately positivist epistemology, seeks generalizable claims about norm dynamics, compatible in method (if not ontology) with mainstream IR scholarship
- **Critical/Poststructuralist Constructivism**: more skeptical of stable causal claims, emphasizes discourse analysis, power/knowledge relations (drawing on Foucault), and the contingency of all social categories
- **Feminist Constructivism**: examines how gendered norms and identities shape state behavior and security practices (e.g., work by J. Ann Tickner)

### Example: Applying Constructivism to a Case

**Example**

The end of the Cold War is frequently cited as a case constructivism explains better than structural realism:

- Neorealism struggled to predict or fully explain the peaceful, negotiated end of Soviet-U.S. bipolar rivalry, since a change of this magnitude in relative material power was not anticipated by systemic balance-of-power logic alone
- Constructivists (e.g., work associated with Ted Hopf and others) point to Mikhail Gorbachev's introduction of "new thinking," which reconstructed the Soviet Union's identity and interests away from confrontation with the West, as a case of identity change driving a systemic transformation that material capability shifts alone do not fully account for [Inference: this remains one interpretation among several in a debated historiography, alongside economic-decline-centered and leadership-centered explanations]

### Major Critiques of Constructivism

**Key Points**

- **Rationalist critique**: constructivism is sometimes criticized for offering thick descriptive accounts of norm change without generating clearly falsifiable, predictive hypotheses comparable to rationalist models
- **Indeterminacy critique**: because constructivism allows for a wide range of possible identities and norms, critics argue it can explain almost any outcome after the fact, raising concerns about explanatory power versus post-hoc interpretation
- **Materialist critique** (from realists): underestimates the persistent weight of material capabilities and security competition, even in supposedly "Kantian" zones of cooperation
- **Internal critique** (from critical/poststructuralist constructivists): conventional constructivism's retained positivist methodology is seen as insufficiently attentive to power relations embedded in the production of knowledge itself
