## Compliance and Enforcement in International Law


### Definition and Scope

Compliance in international law refers to the degree to which states and other subjects of international law conform their behavior to the rules, obligations, and standards established by treaties, customary international law, and other sources of international legal obligation. Enforcement refers to the mechanisms, formal and informal, by which compliance is induced, monitored, or compelled, and by which violations are addressed. These two concepts are analytically distinct but functionally intertwined: compliance theory asks *why* states obey international law even in the absence of a centralized enforcer, while enforcement theory examines *how* obedience is secured or violations are sanctioned.

International law differs structurally from domestic law in lacking a centralized legislature, a compulsory judiciary, and a monopolized coercive apparatus. This "horizontal" structure, in which states are formally equal and sovereign, shapes every aspect of how compliance and enforcement operate.

### The Compliance Puzzle

Louis Henkin's often-cited observation that "almost all nations observe almost all principles of international law and almost all of their obligations almost all of the time" frames what scholars call the compliance puzzle: given the absence of a world government, why do states comply at all?

**Key Points**

- Realist theory (e.g., associated with scholars like Hans Morgenthau) holds that compliance occurs only when it aligns with state power and interest; international law is epiphenomenal to power politics.
- Institutionalist/rational-choice theory (e.g., Robert Keohane) argues compliance is driven by iterated interaction, reputation costs, and the value of stable institutions that reduce transaction costs.
- Managerial theory (Abram Chayes and Antonia Handler Chayes) contends that most non-compliance is not willful defiance but results from ambiguity in treaty language, limited state capacity, or unforeseen circumstances, and is best addressed through dialogue, capacity-building, and transparency rather than coercive sanctions.
- Legitimacy/fairness theory (Thomas Franck) suggests states comply more readily with rules perceived as legitimate, determinate, and adopted through fair procedures.
- Transnational legal process theory (Harold Koh) emphasizes internalization: repeated interaction among transnational actors leads norms to be internalized into domestic legal and political systems, after which compliance becomes habitual rather than calculated.
- Constructivist theory (e.g., associated with scholars like Martha Finnemore) emphasizes norm socialization, identity, and the "logic of appropriateness," where states comply because doing so is consistent with their self-understanding as legitimate members of international society.

### Sources of Compliance Pressure

**Reputational Mechanisms**

States that violate international obligations risk damage to their reputation for reliability, which can raise the cost of future treaty-making, credit, trade, and diplomatic cooperation. Reputational sanctioning is decentralized and relies on other states, media, and civil society to observe and publicize violations.

**Reciprocity**

Much of international law, particularly treaty law, is self-enforcing through reciprocity: a state that breaches a bilateral or multilateral obligation risks the same treatment from counterparties. Article 60 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) codifies this logic by permitting termination or suspension of a treaty in response to a material breach by another party.

**Domestic Incorporation and Internalization**

Compliance is often driven less by international pressure than by domestic legal and political structures: constitutional incorporation of treaty obligations, domestic courts applying international law, parliamentary ratification requirements, and civil society mobilization around treaty commitments.

**Institutional Monitoring and Reporting**

Many treaty regimes build in supervisory mechanisms: periodic state reporting (e.g., under UN human rights treaty bodies), peer review (e.g., the UN Human Rights Council's Universal Periodic Review), and independent fact-finding, which raise the political cost of non-compliance through transparency rather than coercion.

### Enforcement Mechanisms

International enforcement mechanisms can be organized along a spectrum from purely diplomatic/political tools to judicial mechanisms to coercive measures.

#### 1. Self-Help and Countermeasures

In the absence of a centralized enforcer, injured states may resort to lawful self-help measures, primarily:

- **Retorsion**: Unfriendly but lawful acts (e.g., withdrawing an ambassador, suspending aid) taken in response to another state's conduct.
- **Countermeasures**: Acts that would otherwise be unlawful but are rendered lawful because they are a proportionate response to a prior internationally wrongful act by another state, as codified in Articles 49-54 of the International Law Commission's (ILC) Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (ARSIWA, 2001). Countermeasures must be reversible, proportionate, and are subject to procedural conditions (prior notification, an opportunity to comply) except in urgent cases.
- Countermeasures cannot involve the threat or use of force, must not violate peremptory norms (jus cogens), and must not affect obligations regarding the protection of fundamental human rights or humanitarian obligations prohibiting reprisals.

#### 2. Judicial and Quasi-Judicial Mechanisms

**International Court of Justice (ICJ)**

The ICJ, the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, adjudicates disputes between consenting states. Its jurisdiction is fundamentally consensual, arising from special agreements (compromis), jurisdictional clauses in treaties, or declarations accepting compulsory jurisdiction under Article 36(2) of the ICJ Statute (the "Optional Clause"). ICJ judgments are binding on the parties (Article 59, ICJ Statute) but the Court has no independent enforcement arm. Article 94(2) of the UN Charter allows a party to bring non-compliance with an ICJ judgment before the UN Security Council, though this route is constrained by the veto power of the five permanent members.

**Dispute Settlement Bodies**

The World Trade Organization's Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU) is frequently cited as one of the more "enforcement-heavy" regimes in international law, permitting the Dispute Settlement Body to authorize trade retaliation (suspension of concessions) against a non-complying member, following a structured panel and Appellate Body process. **International Criminal Tribunals**

The International Criminal Court (ICC), established by the Rome Statute (1998, entered into force 2002), prosecutes individuals (not states) for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression. The ICC operates on the principle of complementarity: it acts only when national jurisdictions are unwilling or unable genuinely to prosecute. Enforcement of ICC arrest warrants depends on state cooperation, since the Court has no independent police force.

**Regional Human Rights Courts**

The European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR), Inter-American Court of Human Rights, and African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights issue binding judgments against states within their respective systems, with compliance monitored by political bodies (e.g., the Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe for ECtHR judgments).

#### 3. Institutional and Political Enforcement

**UN Security Council Action**

Under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, the Security Council may determine the existence of a threat to peace, breach of peace, or act of aggression (Article 39) and authorize measures ranging from economic sanctions (Article 41) to the use of force (Article 42). This is the closest analog international law has to a centralized coercive enforcement mechanism, but it is structurally limited by the veto power of the five permanent members (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States).

**Sanctions Regimes**

Both UN-mandated sanctions (targeted asset freezes, arms embargoes, travel bans) and unilateral or plurilateral sanctions (e.g., by the United States, European Union) function as enforcement tools, though unilateral sanctions raise contested questions about their legality under international law when not authorized by the Security Council.

**Treaty-Specific Compliance Committees**

Many multilateral environmental agreements employ non-adversarial compliance committees rather than punitive enforcement, exemplified by the Montreal Protocol's Implementation Committee and the Kyoto Protocol's Compliance Committee, which relies on facilitative and, in limited cases, enforcement branches.

### State Responsibility as the Legal Framework for Enforcement

The ARSIWA framework, though not a binding treaty itself but widely regarded as reflecting customary international law, establishes the secondary rules governing when a state's conduct constitutes an internationally wrongful act and what legal consequences follow.

**Key Points**

- An internationally wrongful act requires (1) conduct attributable to the state under international law, and (2) a breach of an international obligation (Article 2, ARSIWA).
- Legal consequences include cessation of the wrongful act, guarantees of non-repetition, and reparation (restitution, compensation, or satisfaction) as elaborated in Articles 34-37.
- Certain obligations, particularly those arising from peremptory norms (jus cogens) such as the prohibition of genocide, are owed to the international community as a whole (obligations erga omnes), meaning any state may invoke responsibility even without being individually injured (Article 48, ARSIWA).

### Worked Example

**Example**

Consider a hypothetical scenario: State A ratifies a bilateral investment treaty (BIT) with State B guaranteeing fair and equitable treatment of State B's investors. State A subsequently expropriates a State B investor's assets without compensation.

1. **Attribution**: The expropriation is carried out by State A's executive agency, satisfying attribution under Article 4 of ARSIWA (conduct of state organs).
2. **Breach**: The act breaches the fair and equitable treatment and expropriation clauses of the BIT.
3. **Available compliance/enforcement pathways**:
   - The investor may bring a claim directly against State A through investor-state dispute settlement (ISDS), commonly under ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes) arbitration rules, without needing State B's diplomatic espousal.
   - State B could exercise diplomatic protection on behalf of its national, though this is discretionary and constrained by the requirement that local remedies be exhausted (subject to treaty-specific exceptions).
   - If an ICSID award is rendered against State A and unpaid, enforcement proceeds through domestic courts of states that are parties to the ICSID Convention, which obliges recognition of ICSID awards as if they were final judgments of domestic courts (Article 54, ICSID Convention).
4. **Practical limits**: [Inference] Even with a favorable award, execution against sovereign assets may be frustrated by sovereign immunity doctrines in the enforcing state's domestic law, illustrating the persistent gap between a formal legal remedy and actual enforcement.

### Structural Limits on Enforcement

**Key Points**

- **Absence of compulsory jurisdiction**: Most international tribunals require state consent; there is no general compulsory jurisdiction analogous to domestic courts.
- **Veto-constrained Security Council**: The Council's Chapter VII powers are the most robust enforcement tool available but can be blocked by any permanent member, which has historically limited action in cases directly implicating P5 interests or allies.
- **Non-self-executing judgments**: Even binding judgments (e.g., of the ICJ) depend on voluntary compliance or, in rare instances, further political action, since there is no international marshal or bailiff.
- **Asymmetric power effects**: [Inference] Enforcement capacity in practice correlates strongly with relative power; powerful states face materially different practical enforcement exposure than weaker states, even under formally equal legal rules. This is a widely discussed critique in the literature rather than a settled empirical finding applicable uniformly across all regimes.
- **Selectivity and politicization concerns**: International criminal enforcement, in particular, has faced sustained criticism regarding the selection of situations for investigation and prosecution.

### Contemporary Debates

**Key Points**

- Whether "naming and shaming" by NGOs and international bodies (e.g., UN treaty bodies, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International) produces measurable behavioral change or primarily serves expressive/normative functions remains empirically contested in political science and international law scholarship.
- The rise of unilateral and extraterritorial sanctions regimes (e.g., by the US and EU) raises questions about the boundary between lawful enforcement and unlawful coercion or extraterritorial overreach.
- Debates persist over whether international criminal justice mechanisms deter atrocity crimes or primarily serve retrospective accountability and expressive functions; the empirical deterrence literature offers mixed and contested findings.
- Climate treaty enforcement (Paris Agreement) has moved toward a "facilitative, non-punitive" compliance model (Article 15) rather than sanctions, reflecting a broader trend in some regimes away from coercive enforcement toward transparency-and-dialogue-based compliance systems, in contrast to trade law's more adversarial retaliation model.

### Conclusion

Compliance and enforcement in international law operate through a decentralized, layered system combining self-help, reciprocity, reputational pressure, institutional monitoring, and, in limited domains, centralized coercive authority through the UN Security Council. No single mechanism dominates; instead, different regimes (trade, human rights, criminal justice, environmental law, investment law) have developed distinct enforcement architectures suited to their subject matter and the political will of their member states. Understanding this field requires distinguishing between the formal availability of a legal remedy and the practical likelihood of its execution, since the two frequently diverge in international practice.
