## Capability Approach and Amartya Sen's Contributions

### Overview

The capability approach is a normative framework for evaluating individual wellbeing, social arrangements, and development progress, developed primarily by economist and philosopher Amartya Sen from the late 1970s onward, and further elaborated in collaboration with philosopher Martha Nussbaum. It reframes the central question of development economics away from "how much income or utility does a person have?" toward "what is a person actually able to do and be?" The approach underpins the theoretical foundation of the Human Development Index (HDI) and has become one of the most influential paradigms in contemporary development economics and welfare economics.

### Intellectual Motivation and Critique of Prior Approaches

**Key Points**

- Sen developed the capability approach partly as a critique of two dominant evaluative frameworks in economics: **resourcism** (evaluating wellbeing based on the resources or primary goods a person holds, associated with John Rawls' theory of justice) and **utilitarianism/welfarism** (evaluating wellbeing based on subjective utility, happiness, or preference satisfaction).
- Sen's critique of resourcism: possessing the same bundle of resources (e.g., income) does not guarantee the same real freedom or outcome for different people, because individuals vary in their ability to convert resources into valuable outcomes. This is formalized as **conversion factors** — personal (e.g., metabolism, disability), social (e.g., discrimination, social norms), and environmental (e.g., climate, infrastructure) factors that affect how efficiently a person can transform a given resource into functioning.
- Sen's critique of utilitarianism: subjective utility or happiness measures can be distorted by **adaptive preferences**, a phenomenon in which persistently deprived individuals psychologically adjust their expectations and desires downward to cope with their circumstances, causing their reported satisfaction to understate their actual deprivation. A famous illustrative case Sen discusses is that surveys have sometimes found chronically deprived populations reporting similar or even higher subjective wellbeing than more materially secure populations, precisely because expectations have been scaled down to match limited circumstances.
- This dual critique motivates Sen's argument that the proper evaluative space for assessing wellbeing and justice should be **capabilities**, not resources or utility directly.

### Core Concepts: Functionings and Capabilities

**Key Points**

- **Functionings**: the various things a person actually manages to do or be — the "beings and doings" that constitute a life. Examples range from elementary functionings (being adequately nourished, being in good health, avoiding preventable morbidity) to more complex ones (having self-respect, participating in community life, being politically active).
- **Capabilities**: the set of alternative functioning combinations a person is realistically able to achieve — that is, the real, substantive **freedom** to achieve various functionings, not merely the achievement itself. The capability set represents a person's opportunity freedom.
- The key distinction: a person who is fasting and a person who is starving may have the identical functioning (not eating), but they have radically different capabilities — the fasting person had the real option to eat and chose not to, while the starving person lacked that option. Evaluating wellbeing purely on functioning outcomes would miss this crucial difference in freedom.
- **Agency**: Sen further distinguishes **wellbeing achievement** (functionings actually realized) from **agency achievement** (the realization of goals and values a person has reason to pursue, which may include goals beyond their own personal wellbeing, such as contributing to a cause or community). This distinguishes a person as a passive recipient of welfare from an active agent pursuing self-determined goals.

### Formal Representation

**Key Points**

- The capability approach can be represented schematically as a process moving from resources (commodities), through conversion factors, to a capability set, from which a person chooses a vector of achieved functionings:

$$\text{Resources (commodities)} \xrightarrow{\text{conversion factors}} \text{Capability Set} \xrightarrow{\text{choice}} \text{Achieved Functionings}$$

- The capability set can be denoted formally as $Q_i(X_i)$, representing the set of functioning vectors achievable by person $i$ given a commodity bundle $X_i$ and that person's specific personal, social, and environmental conversion factors.
- Development, in this framework, is defined as the process of **expanding the real freedoms (capabilities)** that people enjoy, most fully articulated in Sen's book *Development as Freedom* (1999), where he argues that both the primary end and the principal means of development are the expansion of human freedom.

### The Five Instrumental Freedoms (Development as Freedom)

**Key Points**

Sen identifies five distinct but interconnected types of freedom that both constitute development and instrumentally reinforce one another:

1. **Political freedoms**: civil rights, freedom of expression, and the ability to participate in political choice, including electoral accountability of governments.
2. **Economic facilities**: opportunities for individuals to utilize economic resources for production, consumption, or exchange.
3. **Social opportunities**: arrangements society makes for education, healthcare, and other services that affect substantive freedom to live better.
4. **Transparency guarantees**: the need for openness and trust in social interactions, including the disclosure needed to prevent corruption and financial malfeasance.
5. **Protective security**: social safety nets that prevent individuals from being reduced to abject deprivation, including famine relief and unemployment benefits.

- A central argument is that these freedoms are mutually reinforcing: for example, Sen's empirical work on famines argues that **no famine has ever occurred in a functioning multiparty democracy with a relatively free press**, because political freedoms (freedom of expression, electoral accountability) create incentives for governments to respond to early signs of famine, linking political freedom instrumentally to protective security. ### Sen's Famine Analysis and Entitlement Theory

**Key Points**

- In *Poverty and Famines* (1981), Sen developed the **entitlement approach**, arguing that famines are typically not caused solely by an aggregate decline in food availability (Food Availability Decline, FAD), but rather by a collapse in specific groups' **entitlements** — their ability to command food through production, trade, labor, or transfer.
- Sen's analysis of the 1943 Bengal famine is a key illustrative case: he argued that aggregate food supply had not collapsed catastrophically, but that certain occupational groups (e.g., agricultural laborers) experienced a collapse in their exchange entitlements (e.g., collapsing real wages relative to food prices), leaving them unable to acquire available food, even though it existed in the wider economy. [Inference: the precise quantitative causes of the 1943 Bengal famine remain a subject of ongoing historical and economic debate, and Sen's entitlement-based account, while highly influential, is one interpretation among several in the historical literature.]
- This entitlement framework reinforced Sen's broader argument that deprivation is often about a failure of access and freedom (entitlement relations), not merely a failure of aggregate resource availability, directly paralleling his critique of resourcism in the capability approach more generally.

### Relationship to the Human Development Index

**Key Points**

- The HDI, co-developed with economist Mahbub ul Haq, was explicitly designed to operationalize the capability approach in a measurable, comparable statistic, using life expectancy, education, and income as proxies for the capability to live long and healthy lives, to be knowledgeable, and to achieve a decent standard of living.
- Sen was reportedly initially skeptical that a single composite number could adequately capture the richness of the capability approach, given the approach's core insight that wellbeing is inherently multidimensional and resistant to simple aggregation; the HDI represents a deliberate, pragmatic simplification for policy communication and comparison purposes rather than a full operationalization of the theory. [Inference: Sen's degree of reservation about index construction is documented in secondary accounts of the HDI's development, and nuances of his position should be verified against his own published statements for precise attribution.]

### Martha Nussbaum's Extension: The Central Capabilities

**Key Points**

- Philosopher Martha Nussbaum extended Sen's capability approach by proposing a specific, fixed list of **central human capabilities** that she argues should be constitutionally guaranteed as a matter of basic justice, in contrast to Sen, who has generally resisted specifying a fixed universal list, preferring the approach's application to remain context-sensitive and subject to public reasoning and democratic deliberation.
- Nussbaum's list includes capabilities such as life, bodily health, bodily integrity, senses/imagination/thought, emotions, practical reason, affiliation, relationship with other species, play, and control over one's environment. [Inference: specific formulations and orderings of this list have been revised across Nussbaum's publications; the exact current wording should be checked against her most recent published work.]
- This divergence between Sen's more open-ended, deliberative approach and Nussbaum's more determinate, list-based approach represents a significant ongoing debate within capability approach scholarship.

### Critiques and Limitations of the Capability Approach

**Key Points**

- **Operationalization difficulty**: because capabilities represent unobserved freedoms (not just observed outcomes), they are inherently harder to measure empirically than income or realized functionings, complicating practical policy application and cross-country comparison.
- **Lack of a fixed capability list (in Sen's version)**: critics, including Nussbaum, argue that Sen's reluctance to specify a definitive list of central capabilities makes the framework harder to translate into concrete policy benchmarks or constitutional guarantees.
- **Aggregation problem**: even if individual capabilities can be identified, aggregating across multiple, potentially incommensurable capabilities (health vs. political freedom vs. affiliation) into a single evaluative judgment remains methodologically contested, mirroring debates around HDI's own aggregation choices.
- **Resource intensity of data collection**: capturing genuine capability sets (not just realized functionings) would require rich data on the choices available to individuals, not just the choices made, which is empirically demanding and rarely fully available in standard household survey instruments.

### Influence on Development Practice

**Key Points**

- Beyond the HDI, the capability approach has shaped the **human development paradigm** more broadly within UNDP publications, influenced the design of the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), and informed rights-based approaches to development that emphasize participation, empowerment, and freedom alongside material outcomes.
- The approach has also influenced fields beyond economics, including public health (defining health equity in capability terms), education policy (evaluating not just enrollment but substantive learning freedom), and disability studies (analyzing conversion factors relevant to persons with disabilities).
