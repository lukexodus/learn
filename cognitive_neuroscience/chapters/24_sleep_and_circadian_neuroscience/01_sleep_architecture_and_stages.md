## Sleep architecture and stages


### Overview and Foundational Concepts

Sleep architecture refers to the organized, cyclical pattern of sleep stages that occur across a night of sleep, characterized by distinct electrophysiological, behavioral, and neurochemical signatures. Sleep is not a uniform state of reduced consciousness but a highly structured sequence of discrete stages, each serving distinct physiological and cognitive functions.

Sleep is classified into two broad categories:

- **Non-rapid eye movement sleep (NREM):** Further subdivided into stages N1, N2, and N3
- **Rapid eye movement sleep (REM):** A physiologically distinct state characterized by cortical activation, muscle atonia, and vivid dreaming

### Historical Development

- Early 20th-century sleep research relied on behavioral observation alone
- 1937: Loomis and colleagues first classified sleep into stages using electroencephalography (EEG)
- 1953: Aserinsky and Kleitman discovered REM sleep, identifying it as a physiologically distinct state associated with vivid dreaming
- 1968: Rechtschaffen and Kales (R&K) published the first standardized manual for scoring sleep stages, dividing NREM into stages 1 through 4
- 2007: The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) published a revised scoring manual, consolidating R&K stages 3 and 4 into a single stage N3 and renaming stages using the N1–N3 and REM nomenclature still in use today

### Polysomnography: The Measurement Basis

Sleep stages are defined through polysomnography (PSG), which combines multiple simultaneous physiological recordings:

- **Electroencephalography (EEG):** Measures cortical electrical activity; the primary determinant of stage classification
- **Electrooculography (EOG):** Measures eye movements, critical for distinguishing REM sleep and detecting slow eye movements at sleep onset
- **Electromyography (EMG):** Measures muscle tone, typically from the chin (submental) and legs; essential for identifying REM atonia and movement disorders
- **Additional channels:** Electrocardiography (ECG), respiratory effort belts, pulse oximetry, and airflow sensors, used primarily for diagnosing sleep-disordered breathing rather than stage classification itself

**Key Points**

- Stage scoring is performed in 30-second epochs, with each epoch assigned a single predominant stage according to AASM criteria
- EEG frequency bands are central to staging: delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), sigma/spindle range (11–16 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz)

### NREM Stage N1

- **EEG signature:** Transition from alpha rhythm (relaxed wakefulness) to low-amplitude, mixed-frequency theta activity
- **Behavioral characteristics:** Lightest stage of sleep; easily aroused; may include hypnic jerks and hypnagogic sensations
- **Eye movements:** Slow, rolling eye movements
- **Muscle tone:** Gradual reduction from waking levels
- **Proportion of total sleep:** Typically 2 to 5 percent in healthy adults
- **Function:** Represents the sleep onset transition; prolonged or frequent N1 is associated with sleep fragmentation and poor sleep quality

### NREM Stage N2

- **EEG signature:** Defined by the presence of sleep spindles (11–16 Hz bursts, at least 0.5 seconds in duration) and K-complexes (high-amplitude biphasic waveforms, at least 0.5 seconds), superimposed on a background of theta activity
- **Behavioral characteristics:** Deeper than N1, moderate arousal threshold
- **Proportion of total sleep:** Largest proportion of total sleep in adults, approximately 45 to 55 percent
- **Neurophysiological role:** Sleep spindles are generated by thalamo-cortical circuits and are strongly implicated in memory consolidation, particularly procedural and declarative memory; spindle density correlates with certain measures of learning capacity [Inference — the precise causal contribution of spindles to memory consolidation versus correlation with other sleep processes remains under active investigation]

### NREM Stage N3 (Slow-Wave Sleep)

- **EEG signature:** Dominated by high-amplitude, low-frequency delta waves (0.5–4 Hz), comprising at least 20 percent of the epoch by AASM criteria
- **Also termed:** Slow-wave sleep (SWS) or deep sleep; historically subdivided into stages 3 and 4 under R&K criteria
- **Behavioral characteristics:** Highest arousal threshold; associated with sleep inertia and confusion if awakened
- **Proportion of total sleep:** Approximately 15 to 25 percent in young adults, declining substantially with age
- **Physiological correlates:** Reduced heart rate, blood pressure, and metabolic rate; peak secretion of growth hormone
- **Functional significance:** Associated with declarative memory consolidation, synaptic homeostasis (the proposed downscaling of synaptic strength accumulated during waking), and glymphatic clearance of metabolic waste products including beta-amyloid

### REM Sleep

- **EEG signature:** Low-amplitude, mixed-frequency activity resembling wakefulness, sometimes accompanied by sawtooth waves; historically termed "paradoxical sleep" due to the wake-like cortical activity co-occurring with behavioral sleep
- **Eye movements:** Rapid, conjugate eye movements in bursts, the defining feature from which the stage takes its name
- **Muscle tone:** Near-complete skeletal muscle atonia, mediated primarily by glycinergic and GABAergic inhibition of spinal motor neurons originating from the sublaterodorsal nucleus and ventromedial medulla
- **Autonomic characteristics:** Increased and variable heart rate, respiration, and blood pressure; penile or clitoral engorgement
- **Proportion of total sleep:** Approximately 20 to 25 percent in healthy adults
- **Distribution across the night:** REM periods lengthen and become more frequent toward the later portion of the sleep period
- **Functional associations:** Vivid dreaming, emotional memory processing, and procedural memory consolidation; theta rhythm prominent in rodent hippocampal recordings during REM, with less direct evidence in humans

**Key Points**

- REM atonia dysfunction underlies REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), in which patients physically enact dreams
- REM sleep is sometimes called "REM sleep without atonia" when this inhibitory mechanism fails, a hallmark finding in RBD and an early marker for synucleinopathies (Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, multiple system atrophy)

### The Ultradian Sleep Cycle

Sleep progresses through NREM and REM stages in a cyclical pattern approximately 90 to 110 minutes in duration, repeating 4 to 6 times per night.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    W[Wake] --> N1
    N1 --> N2
    N2 --> N3
    N3 --> N2b[N2]
    N2b --> REM
    REM --> N1b[N1/N2]
    N1b -->|repeat cycle| N2c[N2]
    N2c -.->|later cycles: shorter N3, longer REM| REM2[REM]
```

**Key Points**

- Slow-wave sleep predominates in the early cycles of the night, driven by high homeostatic sleep pressure
- REM sleep proportion increases progressively across successive cycles, with the longest REM periods occurring in the final third of the night
- This asymmetric distribution means partial sleep deprivation affects SWS and REM differently depending on which portion of the night is curtailed

### Regulatory Models: Two-Process Model

Sleep timing and structure are classically explained by the interaction of two processes, proposed by Borbély:

- **Process S (homeostatic drive):** Sleep pressure that accumulates during wakefulness, proportional to time spent awake, and dissipates during sleep; reflected in EEG delta power during NREM sleep
- **Process C (circadian drive):** An endogenous near-24-hour oscillation, generated primarily by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, that regulates the propensity for sleep and wakefulness independent of prior sleep history

The interaction of these two processes determines sleep timing, depth, and the pattern of stage distribution across the night.

**Example**

Following 24 hours of sleep deprivation, an individual will exhibit a rebound increase in slow-wave sleep (N3) during subsequent recovery sleep, reflecting elevated Process S pressure. REM sleep rebound tends to occur somewhat later in the recovery period, illustrating the differential regulation of NREM and REM sleep components.

### Neurochemical and Neuroanatomical Substrates

| Sleep State | Key Neurotransmitter/Neuromodulator Activity | Key Structures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wakefulness | High acetylcholine, norepinephrine, histamine, orexin | Ascending reticular activating system, locus coeruleus, tuberomammillary nucleus, lateral hypothalamus (orexin neurons) |
| NREM sleep | Reduced monoaminergic and cholinergic tone; GABAergic inhibition dominant | Ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO), thalamus |
| REM sleep | Cholinergic activity resumes; monoaminergic activity suppressed ("REM-on/REM-off" interaction) | Pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei, sublaterodorsal nucleus, locus coeruleus, raphe nuclei |

- The **flip-flop switch model** describes mutually inhibitory relationships between wake-promoting and sleep-promoting nuclei (analogous to the VLPO–arousal system interaction), producing rapid, stable state transitions rather than gradual ones
- **Orexin (hypocretin)** neurons in the lateral hypothalamus stabilize wakefulness and inhibit inappropriate transitions into REM sleep; loss of orexin neurons underlies narcolepsy type 1

### Developmental and Age-Related Changes

- Infants enter sleep through REM (historically termed "active sleep"), spending up to 50 percent of total sleep time in REM in the neonatal period
- SWS is most abundant in childhood and adolescence, corresponding with peak growth hormone secretion
- SWS declines progressively across the lifespan, with substantial reductions by middle and older adulthood
- Sleep becomes more fragmented with age, with increased N1 and reduced sleep continuity and efficiency
- REM proportion remains relatively stable across adulthood, though REM latency and distribution can shift with aging and certain pathologies

### Clinical and Research Relevance of Stage-Specific Disruption

- **Reduced SWS:** Associated with impaired glymphatic clearance and has been proposed as a contributing factor in neurodegenerative disease risk, particularly regarding beta-amyloid accumulation [Inference — the causal direction and clinical magnitude of this relationship in humans remains an active research question]
- **Shortened REM latency:** A classic finding in major depressive disorder, sometimes used as a biological marker in research contexts
- **Disrupted REM atonia:** Diagnostic hallmark of REM sleep behavior disorder
- **Sleep spindle deficits:** Observed in schizophrenia and associated with impaired sleep-dependent motor learning
- **Sleep architecture fragmentation:** Common in obstructive sleep apnea, where repeated arousals prevent progression into deeper NREM stages and consolidated REM periods
