## Marginal Excess Burden

### Definition and Conceptual Overview

Marginal excess burden (MEB) measures the additional deadweight loss (efficiency cost) generated by raising one more unit of tax revenue through a small increase in an existing tax rate. Unlike total excess burden, which measures the aggregate welfare loss from a tax already in place, MEB is a derivative concept — it answers the question: "What is the efficiency cost of the *next* dollar of tax revenue?"

This distinction is central to public economics because deadweight loss from taxation does not grow linearly with the tax rate. It grows approximately with the **square** of the tax rate, meaning MEB rises as the tax rate itself rises. Policymakers evaluating tax reform, base broadening, or the incidence of raising a specific tax must use MEB rather than average excess burden, since average burden understates the true cost of incremental taxation at higher rates.

### Mathematical Derivation

**[Confirmed]** For a market with a single tax $t$ on a good with compensated (Hicksian) demand elasticity $\varepsilon$, the standard Harberger triangle approximation for total excess burden $EB$ is:

$$EB \approx \frac{1}{2} \varepsilon \cdot p \cdot q \cdot t^2$$

where $p$ is the pre-tax price, $q$ is the equilibrium quantity, and $t$ is the ad valorem tax rate.

Tax revenue $R$ is:

$$R = t \cdot p \cdot q(t)$$

**Marginal excess burden** is defined as the ratio of the change in excess burden to the change in revenue as the tax rate changes marginally:

$$MEB = \frac{dEB/dt}{dR/dt}$$

Differentiating the excess burden expression with respect to $t$:

$$\frac{dEB}{dt} \approx \varepsilon \cdot p \cdot q \cdot t$$

This result shows **[Confirmed]** that the marginal deadweight loss is proportional to $t$, while total excess burden is proportional to $t^2$. Consequently, MEB per dollar of revenue rises linearly with the tax rate — the core reason economists caution against pushing any single tax rate too high in pursuit of revenue.

### The MEB-to-Revenue Ratio

A commonly used simplified formulation, holding quantity effects on revenue approximately constant at the margin, gives:

$$MEB \approx \frac{\varepsilon \cdot t}{1 - \varepsilon \cdot t}$$

or, in the small-tax approximation:

$$MEB \approx \varepsilon \cdot t$$

**[Inference]** This approximation assumes a starting point without pre-existing distortions in related markets; interaction effects with other taxed goods (cross-price elasticities) can raise or lower the true MEB depending on whether goods are complements or substitutes to the taxed good.

### Numerical Example

**Example**

Suppose a government taxes gasoline at $t = 0.20$ (20%), with a compensated price elasticity of demand $\varepsilon = -0.4$ (using the magnitude, $0.4$), pre-tax price $p = \$3.00$, and quantity $q = 100$ billion gallons.

Total excess burden:

$$EB \approx \frac{1}{2}(0.4)(3.00)(100\text{B})(0.20)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.4)(300\text{B})(0.04) = 2.4\text{B}$$

Marginal excess burden (using $dEB/dt$):

$$\frac{dEB}{dt} = (0.4)(300\text{B})(0.20) = 24\text{B per unit }t$$

If $dR/dt \approx p \cdot q \cdot (1 - \varepsilon t) = 300\text{B} \times (1 - 0.08) = 276\text{B}$, then:

$$MEB = \frac{24\text{B}}{276\text{B}} \approx 0.087$$

This means the last dollar of gasoline tax revenue costs society approximately **8.7 cents** in additional deadweight loss — distinct from the *average* excess burden per dollar, which would be $EB/R = 2.4\text{B}/(0.20 \times 300\text{B}) = 2.4/60 = 0.04$, or 4 cents. The marginal cost exceeds the average cost, consistent with a convex (quadratic) excess burden function.

The MEB curve is increasing and convex in $t$. Near $t = 0$, small taxes generate almost no marginal distortion (this is the theoretical basis for broad-based, low-rate taxation — spreading the tax burden across many goods each taxed lightly minimizes total MEB). As $t$ rises toward the revenue-maximizing rate on the Laffer curve, MEB approaches infinity, since at that point $dR/dt = 0$ while $dEB/dt > 0$.

### Applications in Tax Policy

**Key Points**

- **Optimal tax design**: The Ramsey rule for optimal commodity taxation sets tax rates such that the MEB per dollar of revenue is equalized across all taxed goods, weighted by their contribution to social welfare — not by setting a single uniform rate.
- **Marginal Cost of Public Funds (MCPF)**: MEB is a core input into MCPF, defined as $MCPF = 1 + MEB$, used in cost-benefit analysis to determine whether a public project's benefits exceed the true resource cost of financing it through taxation.
- **Tax reform evaluation**: When comparing "raise tax A by $1" versus "raise tax B by $1" to fund the same expenditure, the tax with the lower MEB is more efficient, even if its average excess burden is similar.
- **Base broadening vs. rate increases**: Because MEB rises with $t$, broadening the tax base (taxing more goods at lower rates) is generally more efficient than raising the rate on an already-taxed narrow base, holding revenue fixed.

### Relationship to the Laffer Curve

**[Confirmed]** As $t$ increases toward the revenue-maximizing rate $t^*$ (where $dR/dt = 0$), the denominator of the MEB ratio approaches zero while the numerator $dEB/dt$ remains positive, so:

$$\lim_{t \to t^*} MEB = \infty$$

Beyond $t^*$, raising the tax rate further reduces revenue while increasing deadweight loss — a strictly dominated policy region. MEB analysis therefore provides a rigorous efficiency-based explanation of why rates should not approach the top of the Laffer curve, independent of any revenue-maximization argument alone.

### Factors Affecting Magnitude

**Key Points**

- **Elasticity of demand/supply**: Higher $|\varepsilon|$ mechanically increases MEB, since deadweight loss scales with elasticity.
- **Initial tax rate**: MEB is increasing in the pre-existing tax rate on the same good (higher starting $t$ means the marginal unit of taxation is more distortionary).
- **Cross-market distortions**: **[Inference]** If the taxed good is a substitute for another already-taxed good, MEB calculated in isolation may understate the true marginal cost, since consumers shift toward the second distorted market. General equilibrium tax interaction models (e.g., Ballard, Shoven, and Whalley-style computable general equilibrium studies) are used to capture these effects empirically.
- **Behavioral responses beyond simple demand shifts**: For labor and capital income taxes, MEB estimates often incorporate broader behavioral elasticities (e.g., taxable income elasticity) rather than simple commodity demand elasticities, since labor supply and reporting responses can be more complex than a single-good demand curve.

### Empirical Estimates

**[Unverified]** Empirical MEB estimates vary substantially across studies and tax types; commonly cited ranges in the U.S. public finance literature place MEB for labor income taxation somewhere between $0.20 and $0.50 per dollar of revenue at typical marginal rates, though these figures are highly sensitive to the assumed elasticity of taxable income and the specific tax instrument modeled. Because these figures depend on parameter assumptions and modeling choices that vary by study, they should be treated as illustrative orders of magnitude rather than precise, universally agreed-upon values.

### Common Pitfalls in Analysis

**Key Points**

- Confusing **average** excess burden ($EB/R$) with **marginal** excess burden ($dEB/dR$) — these are numerically different and lead to different policy conclusions.
- Ignoring the **starting tax rate**: MEB calculated from a zero-tax baseline is much lower than MEB calculated as an increment to an already substantial existing tax.
- Treating MEB as **constant** across the revenue range — it is a function of $t$, not a fixed parameter.
- Applying partial equilibrium MEB formulas to taxes with significant **cross-elasticities** or general equilibrium feedback without adjustment.
