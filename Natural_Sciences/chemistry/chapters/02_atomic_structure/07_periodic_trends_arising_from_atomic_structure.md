## Periodic Trends Arising from Atomic Structure


### Overview

Periodic trends are systematic patterns in atomic properties that emerge across periods (rows) and down groups (columns) of the periodic table. These trends arise directly from underlying atomic structure—specifically, the interplay between nuclear charge, electron shielding, and the principal quantum number of the outermost electrons—rather than being arbitrary empirical patterns.

### Underlying Concepts: Effective Nuclear Charge and Shielding

**Effective nuclear charge ($Z_{eff}$)** is the net positive charge experienced by an electron in a multi-electron atom, after accounting for the repulsive shielding (screening) effect of other electrons.

$$Z_{eff} = Z - S$$

where $Z$ is the actual nuclear charge (atomic number) and $S$ is the shielding constant, representing the reduction in effective charge due to inner-shell (core) electrons repelling outer electrons.

**Key Points**

- Core electrons (inner shells) shield outer electrons from the full nuclear charge effectively, while electrons in the same shell shield each other only partially.
- Across a period (left to right), protons are added to the nucleus while electrons are added to the same outer shell; since same-shell shielding is weak, $Z_{eff}$ increases steadily across a period.
- Down a group, although both protons and electrons increase, the addition of complete new inner shells provides strong shielding, so $Z_{eff}$ experienced by valence electrons increases only slightly or remains relatively stable.

### Atomic Radius

Atomic radius is a measure of the size of an atom, typically defined as half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded or adjacent identical atoms.

**Trend Across a Period (Left to Right)**: Atomic radius decreases.

- As $Z_{eff}$ increases across a period, the increased nuclear pull draws valence electrons closer to the nucleus, while electrons remain in the same principal energy level ($n$ unchanged), resulting in a smaller atomic radius.

**Trend Down a Group (Top to Bottom)**: Atomic radius increases.

- Each successive element adds a new principal energy level (higher $n$), placing valence electrons farther from the nucleus; this outweighs the modest increase in $Z_{eff}$, resulting in larger atoms.

### Ionic Radius

Ionic radius follows related but distinct trends depending on whether an ion is a cation or anion.

**Key Points**

- **Cations** are smaller than their parent atoms, since removing electrons reduces electron-electron repulsion and, in many cases, eliminates an entire outer shell.
- **Anions** are larger than their parent atoms, since added electrons increase electron-electron repulsion, causing the electron cloud to expand, while nuclear charge remains constant.
- Across an isoelectronic series (ions with equal electron count), ionic radius decreases as nuclear charge (atomic number) increases, since a higher $Z_{eff}$ pulls the same number of electrons more tightly toward the nucleus.

**Example**

For the isoelectronic series $\text{N}^{3-} > \text{O}^{2-} > \text{F}^- > \text{Na}^+ > \text{Mg}^{2+} > \text{Al}^{3+}$ (all with 10 electrons), ionic radius decreases steadily as nuclear charge increases from 7 to 13 protons.

### Ionization Energy

Ionization energy is the minimum energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion.

$$\text{X(g)} \rightarrow \text{X}^+\text{(g)} + e^-$$

**Trend Across a Period (Left to Right)**: Ionization energy generally increases.

- Higher $Z_{eff}$ binds valence electrons more tightly, requiring more energy for removal.

**Trend Down a Group (Top to Bottom)**: Ionization energy decreases.

- Valence electrons are farther from the nucleus and more effectively shielded by inner shells, making them easier to remove.

**Key Points**

- Successive ionization energies for a given atom always increase (removing each subsequent electron becomes progressively harder, since the remaining electron cloud has an increasing net positive charge relative to electron count).
- A particularly large jump in successive ionization energy occurs when an electron is removed from a full inner shell (core electron) rather than the valence shell, since core electrons are held much more tightly.

**Example**

For magnesium ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2$), the first and second ionization energies (removing 3s electrons) are relatively modest, but the third ionization energy (requiring removal of a 2p core electron) increases dramatically, reflecting the jump from valence to core electron removal.

### Exceptions to Ionization Energy Trends

Two notable irregularities occur within periods due to electron configuration stability effects:

- **Group 2 to Group 13 dip**: Removing a single p electron (Group 13) requires less energy than removing a paired s electron (Group 2), because the p electron is slightly higher in energy and less shielded from same-subshell repulsion.
- **Group 15 to Group 16 dip**: Removing an electron from Group 16 (which has a paired p orbital) requires less energy than removing from Group 15 (which has a stable, half-filled p subshell), because electron-electron repulsion in the paired orbital makes that electron easier to remove despite the overall higher $Z_{eff}$.

[Inference] These dips are well-documented, standard exceptions taught in most general chemistry curricula; the precise magnitude of each dip can vary slightly between specific element pairs.

### Electron Affinity

Electron affinity is the energy change that occurs when an electron is added to a gaseous atom to form a negative ion.

$$\text{X(g)} + e^- \rightarrow \text{X}^-\text{(g)}$$

**Trend Across a Period (Left to Right)**: Electron affinity generally becomes more negative (more energy released), reflecting a stronger attraction for additional electrons.

**Trend Down a Group (Top to Bottom)**: Electron affinity generally becomes less negative (less energy released), as added electrons are farther from the nucleus and more shielded.

**Key Points**

- Electron affinity trends are less regular than ionization energy or atomic radius trends, with several notable exceptions.
- Noble gases have electron affinities near zero or positive (energy must be absorbed), since adding an electron would require occupying a new, higher-energy subshell.
- Group 15 elements (e.g., nitrogen) have lower (less negative) electron affinity than expected, since their half-filled p subshell configuration is already relatively stable, making additional electron addition less favorable.

### Electronegativity

Electronegativity is a measure of an atom's ability to attract shared electrons within a chemical bond, most commonly quantified using the Pauling scale.

**Trend Across a Period (Left to Right)**: Electronegativity increases, following the same $Z_{eff}$ logic as ionization energy.

**Trend Down a Group (Top to Bottom)**: Electronegativity decreases, as increased atomic size and shielding reduce the nucleus's pull on shared bonding electrons.

**Key Points**

- Fluorine has the highest electronegativity of any element (approximately 3.98 on the Pauling scale).
- Cesium and francium have among the lowest electronegativities (approximately 0.7–0.79).
- Noble gases are typically excluded from electronegativity trend comparisons since they rarely form conventional chemical bonds.

### Metallic and Nonmetallic Character

Metallic character reflects an element's tendency to lose electrons and exhibit properties such as malleability, conductivity, and luster.

**Trend Across a Period (Left to Right)**: Metallic character decreases; nonmetallic character increases.

**Trend Down a Group (Top to Bottom)**: Metallic character increases; nonmetallic character decreases.

This trend correlates directly with ionization energy and electronegativity: elements with low ionization energy and low electronegativity (lower left of the periodic table) readily lose electrons and are strongly metallic, while elements with high ionization energy and high electronegativity (upper right) tend to gain electrons and are strongly nonmetallic.

### Worked Example: Comparing Atoms

**Example**

Rank the following in order of increasing atomic radius: $\text{Cl}$, $\text{Na}$, $\text{Mg}$, $\text{K}$.

- Na, Mg, Cl are all in period 3; across this period, radius decreases left to right: Cl < Mg < Na.
- K is directly below Na in group 1, so K has a larger radius than Na (new principal shell added).

Increasing order: $\text{Cl} < \text{Mg} < \text{Na} < \text{K}$

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Assuming atomic radius increases across a period (it decreases, due to increasing $Z_{eff}$ with electrons in the same shell).
- Forgetting that cations are always smaller and anions always larger than their neutral parent atom.
- Applying ionization energy or electronegativity trends to noble gases without noting they are frequently excluded or behave atypically.
- Overlooking the two standard exceptions (Group 2→13 and Group 15→16 dips) in the otherwise increasing ionization energy trend across a period.
- Confusing electron affinity (energy change when gaining an electron) with electronegativity (relative ability to attract shared electrons in a bond); the two are related but conceptually distinct.
