## The Shingo Model's Guiding Principles, Systems, Tools, and Results


### Overview

The Shingo Model is a framework developed by the Shingo Institute at Utah State University's Jon M. Huntsman School of Business, based on the teachings of Shigeo Shingo, designed to guide the transformation of an organization's culture toward achieving ideal results and operational excellence. Unlike approaches that focus primarily on implementing tools or hitting targets, the model holds that sustainable excellence emerges from the daily behaviors of people throughout an organization, and it explicitly positions culture — not any specific tool set — as the foundation of the entire model. The Shingo Model does not replace Lean, Six Sigma, or other improvement methodologies; instead, it strengthens an organization's existing values, systems, tools, results, and culture by explaining *why* improvement tools succeed or fail, adding an understanding of how guiding principles transform culture and make results sustainable.

The model and its assessment guidelines were first published in January 2008, developed by Shaun Barker, Randy Cook, Robert Miller, and Jacob Raymer during their tenure at the Shingo Institute, with Dr. Brian Atwater and Brent Allen contributing to the systems-thinking dimension.

### The Three Insights of Organizational Excellence

**Key Points**

While analyzing what excellent organizations had in common, the Shingo Institute identified three insights that form the logical basis of the entire model — principles inform systems, systems drive behaviors, and behaviors produce results:

1. **Ideal Results Require Ideal Behaviors** — The results of an organization depend on the way its people behave; to achieve ideal results, leaders must do the hard work of creating a culture where ideal behaviors are expected and evident in every team member.
2. **Purpose and Systems Drive Behavior** — Most systems are designed to create a specific business result without regard for the behavior that the system drives, so managers have an enormous job to realign management, improvement, and work systems to drive the ideal behavior required by all people to achieve ideal results.
3. **Principles Inform Ideal Behaviors** — Principles are foundational rules that govern consequences; the more deeply one understands principles, the more clearly one understands ideal behavior, and the better one can design systems to drive that behavior toward ideal results.

This insight helps leaders understand why improvement initiatives often fail despite using the right tools: the tools were not addressing the underlying systems and principles that drive sustainable behavior change.

### The Shingo Guiding Principles

**Key Points**

There are ten Shingo Guiding Principles, organized into three dimensions:

**Cultural Enablers**

- Respect Every Individual
- Lead with Humility

**Continuous Improvement**

- Assure Quality at the Source
- Improve Flow & Pull
- Seek Perfection
- Embrace Scientific Thinking
- Focus on Process

**Enterprise Alignment**

- Create Value for the Customer
- Create Consistency of Purpose
- Think Systemically

The principles are described as universal, timeless concepts that govern consequence, rather than arbitrary rules — meaning they apply regardless of industry, culture, or specific methodology chosen for implementation. Because the model is based on guiding principles rather than a fixed tool set, the Shingo Institute positions it as applicable to all industries and all people, including healthcare, government, finance, higher education, service industries, and nonprofits, not only manufacturing.

### The Diamond: Relationship of Culture, Principles, Systems, Tools, and Results

**Key Points**

The Shingo Model is commonly represented graphically as a diamond, capturing the critical relationship between principles, systems, tools, and results, with **culture** at the foundation/heart of the entire model.

The dimensions and their described function within the model:

- **Culture** — the foundation of the enterprise and the heart of the entire model. All guiding principles need to be embedded in the culture. Principles inform ideal behaviors (the behavioral goals); cultural transformation requires a shift in behaviors, and systems drive behavior. Systems must therefore align with the principles through the ideal behaviors they inform, shifting the culture ever closer to ideal behavior — which typically requires an organization to adjust old systems, create new systems, and eliminate systems that no longer drive desired behavior or are misaligned.
- **Guiding Principles** — the basis for building a sustainable culture of organizational excellence, organized into the three dimensions above (Cultural Enablers, Continuous Improvement, Enterprise Alignment). Principles *align* systems.
- **Systems** — a successful enterprise is usually made up of complex systems, which can be divided into layers of subsystems, each containing the necessary tools to enable a successful outcome — defined in both performance and behavioral terms. Systems *select* and *drive* tools and behavior.
- **Tools** — organizations often make the mistake of focusing too heavily on a specific set of improvement tools as the foundation of their efforts; while tools help teams improve processes, they only answer the question of "how," not "why." Without a clear understanding of why improvement matters, teams may become dependent on instructions rather than empowered to think and act for themselves. When tools are used within a broader framework of guiding principles and purpose, they become far more powerful. Tools *enable* systems.
- **Results** — the focus of most leadership is on results, commonly measured as key performance indicators (KPIs). An organization must have performance results to succeed, and value needs to be considered from the customer's perspective rather than from enterprise leadership's perspective — but lagging-indicator KPIs are usually what an organization uses to measure behavior, and therefore culture, meaning results are best understood as evidence of the underlying behavioral and cultural state rather than as an end pursued directly. Results *refine* the whole system, closing the loop back to principles and culture.

| Enterprise Alignment | Think Systemically | Understand how the interconnections between parts of the organization affect overall performance |

### Relationship to Lean and Other Excellence Frameworks

**Key Points**

- The Shingo Model does not replace Lean or Six Sigma; it strengthens an organization's existing improvement methodology by explaining *why* those tools succeed or fail — Lean and Six Sigma provide powerful methods for problem-solving, waste reduction, and process optimization, but organizations often struggle to sustain improvements because the underlying culture, behaviors, and systems across organizational levels have not been aligned to support them. When Shingo principles and behaviors become deeply rooted in an organization's culture, Lean and Six Sigma tools become more consistent, effective, and sustainable.
- Compared to other well-known excellence frameworks such as **EFQM** (European Foundation for Quality Management) or the **Baldrige** framework, the Shingo Model's distinguishing focus is on explaining the "why" through guiding principles and cultural transformation, whereas those other frameworks primarily evaluate organizational performance criteria; the Shingo Model is positioned as strengthening, rather than competing with, any organizational excellence methodology already in use.

### The Shingo Assessment and Recognition Process

**Key Points**

- The **Shingo Prize** (now formalized through the **Shingo Challenge**) is based on a complete assessment of an organization's culture and how well it drives world-class results. Examiners observe behavior and rate the frequency, duration, intensity, and scope of required principle-based behavior, and examine how managers focus on principles and culture and how well systems align with ideal behaviors.
- To qualify for a Shingo Challenge, an organization must provide evidence of at least three years of sustained transformational improvement; the overall time to complete a Challenge is variable, since each organization's improvement journey differs.
- Preparation typically begins with the **DISCOVER EXCELLENCE** and **SYSTEMS DESIGN** workshops — the first two of the six-workshop Shingo Workshop Series (DISCOVER EXCELLENCE, SYSTEMS DESIGN, CULTURAL ENABLERS, CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT, ENTERPRISE ALIGNMENT, BUILD EXCELLENCE) — along with completing **Shingo Insight**, an anonymous web-based survey used to assess an organization's current culture, systems, and results.
- Organizations recognized under the Shingo Challenge span manufacturing, healthcare, financial services, government, education, and other sectors, reflecting the model's cross-industry applicability.

### Illustrative Example

**Example**

A plant has implemented visual Kanban boards (a tool) and trained supervisors on standard PDCA problem-solving (a system) but continues to see quality escapes recur. Applying the Shingo Model diagnostic lens:

1. **Check Results**: Defect rate (a lagging KPI) remains elevated despite the tools being technically in place.
2. **Check Systems**: The escalation system rewards supervisors primarily for hitting daily output targets, with no formal recognition tied to problems caught and resolved at the source — meaning the system is not actually driving the "Assure Quality at the Source" principle in practice, regardless of the tools present.
3. **Check Behaviors**: Because the system rewards throughput over root-cause resolution, operators are observed passing marginal parts downstream rather than stopping the line, a behavior inconsistent with the ideal behavior the "Assure Quality at the Source" principle calls for.
4. **Check Principles**: Leadership had not clearly connected "Assure Quality at the Source" and "Respect Every Individual" (psychological safety to stop the line without penalty) to the design of the performance management system.
5. **Root correction**: Rather than adding another quality-control tool, leadership redesigns the performance management *system* so that stopping the line to resolve a defect is visibly rewarded, aligning the system with the stated principle — after which the desired behavior (operators proactively flagging defects) becomes the norm, and the defect-rate KPI improves as a downstream consequence.

This demonstrates the model's central diagnostic move: when results are poor despite the presence of tools, the model directs the investigator upstream — through systems, to behaviors, to the underlying principle — rather than assuming a tooling gap.

### Common Misunderstandings

**Key Points**

- **Misconception**: "The Shingo Model is a set of lean tools like 5S or Kanban." **Correction**: The model explicitly warns against tool-centric thinking; tools only answer "how," not "why," and organizations that focus primarily on tools without addressing principles and systems risk producing team members who are dependent on instructions rather than empowered to think and act for themselves.
- **Misconception**: "The Shingo Model is only for manufacturing companies." **Correction**: Because it is principle-based rather than tool-based, the model has been applied across healthcare, government, finance, higher education, service industries, and nonprofits.
- **Misconception**: "Achieving good KPIs means the culture is healthy." **Correction**: The model treats KPIs (Results) as lagging indicators — useful evidence of underlying culture and behavior, but not the primary object of management attention; the model directs attention instead toward whether systems and principles are driving the behaviors that produce those results sustainably.

### Practical Application Steps

**Next Steps**

1. Begin with the DISCOVER EXCELLENCE workshop or equivalent study of the Shingo Guiding Principles to build a shared organizational vocabulary before introducing or auditing any specific tool set.
2. Conduct an honest assessment (formally via Shingo Insight, or informally via direct observation) of whether current management systems reward the behaviors the organization says it values, rather than assuming alignment exists.
3. When a result (KPI) is underperforming, diagnose upstream through the model's chain — examine the behavior first, then the system driving that behavior, then the principle the system should be reinforcing — rather than defaulting to adding a new tool.
4. Use the ten guiding principles, grouped into Cultural Enablers, Continuous Improvement, and Enterprise Alignment, as a checklist when designing or revising any management system, to verify the system reinforces rather than undermines the intended principle.
5. Treat existing Lean/Six Sigma tools as already appropriate in most cases; the priority under the Shingo Model is typically to realign the *systems* surrounding those tools with principles, not to replace the tools themselves.
