## Schemes of Repetition: Anaphora, Epistrophe, and Anadiplosis


### Overview

Rhetorical **schemes** are figures of speech that alter the *arrangement* or *pattern* of words without changing their literal meaning — distinguished from **tropes**, which alter the *meaning* of words (metaphor, irony, metonymy). Schemes of repetition are among the oldest and most extensively catalogued rhetorical devices, documented systematically in the *Rhetorica ad Herennium* (an anonymous 1st-century BCE Roman rhetorical handbook long attributed to Cicero) and in Quintilian's *Institutio Oratoria*. Anaphora, Epistrophe, and Anadiplosis are three of the most consequential repetition schemes, each repeating words at a different structural position to produce distinct rhythmic and persuasive effects.

**Key Points**

- Schemes of repetition operate primarily through structural position: anaphora repeats at the *beginning*, epistrophe at the *end*, and anadiplosis at the *joint* between successive clauses.
- These devices are not merely ornamental; ancient rhetoricians treat them as tools that increase emotional force (*pathos*), aid audience memory, and create a sense of building intensity or momentum (*auxēsis*, amplification) — directly connecting this device-level chapter back to the amplification techniques trained in the Chreia and Commonplace progymnasmata exercises.
- Overuse or mechanical application of any repetition scheme risks producing a monotonous or manipulative effect, undermining rather than reinforcing the argument's warrant — the same ethical caution that governs vivid description (Ekphrasis) and emotional appeal generally.

### Anaphora

**Definition**: Anaphora (Greek, "carrying back") is the repetition of the same word or phrase at the **beginning** of successive clauses, sentences, or lines.

**Structure**:

$$[\text{Repeated Opening}] + A, \quad [\text{Repeated Opening}] + B, \quad [\text{Repeated Opening}] + C$$

**Example**

> "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields and in the streets, we shall fight in the hills."

**[Confirmed]** This is a widely cited historical example of anaphora in political oratory (Winston Churchill's 1940 speech to the House of Commons), commonly referenced in rhetorical analysis specifically because of its anaphoric structure building cumulative resolve across successive clauses.

**Effect**: Anaphora produces a driving, insistent rhythm and builds cumulative emotional intensity with each repetition, since each new clause is heard as an escalation on a fixed rhetorical foundation. It is especially effective in oral delivery, where the repeated opening cues the audience's ear to anticipate the pattern, heightening engagement with each subsequent variation.

### Epistrophe (Antistrophe)

**Definition**: Epistrophe (Greek, "turning upon"; also called *antistrophe*) is the repetition of the same word or phrase at the **end** of successive clauses or sentences — the structural mirror of anaphora.

**Structure**:

$$A + [\text{Repeated Ending}], \quad B + [\text{Repeated Ending}], \quad C + [\text{Repeated Ending}]$$

**Example**

> "When I was a child, I spoke as a child, I understood as a child, I thought as a child."

**Effect**: Where anaphora emphasizes the recurring frame while allowing the content to vary and build, epistrophe emphasizes closure and finality with each clause, since the audience's attention lands on the repeated term at the point of maximum rhetorical weight — the end of the clause. This makes epistrophe particularly effective for driving home a single key concept, name, or value that the speaker wants to remain foremost in the audience's memory.

**Example (Executive Context)**

> "This quarter, we listened to our customers. We adjusted our roadmap for our customers. We will keep building for our customers."

### Anadiplosis

**Definition**: Anadiplosis (Greek, "doubling back") is the repetition of the last word or phrase of one clause at the **beginning** of the next clause, creating a linking or "stepping stone" effect between successive ideas.

**Structure**:

$$A \ldots [\text{Word } X], \quad [\text{Word } X] \ldots B$$

**Example**

> "Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hatred. Hatred leads to suffering."

**Effect**: Anadiplosis creates a strong sense of logical or causal progression, since each clause visibly grows out of the one before it via the shared linking term. This makes it especially suited to expressing chains of causation or escalation — a stylistic device that can visually and rhythmically reinforce a causal warrant (in Toulmin terms) by making the causal chain itself audible in the sentence structure.

**[Confirmed]** Anadiplosis is documented as a distinct figure in the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*'s systematic catalogue of figures of speech (*figurae*), classified there among the figures that achieve their effect through repetition of words in a specific structural pattern (referred to in that tradition as *conduplicatio* and related figures), distinguishing it from anaphora and epistrophe by its specific joint-repetition position.

| Anaphora | Beginning of successive clauses | Cumulative intensity, driving rhythm | Building resolve, escalating a call to action |

| Anadiplosis | End of one clause = start of next | Logical/causal chaining, sense of inevitability | Expressing causation, escalation, or consequence chains |

### Symploce: The Combination Figure

When anaphora and epistrophe are used together in the same passage — repeating one phrase at the beginning and a different phrase at the end of successive clauses — the resulting combined figure is called **symploce** (Greek, "interweaving").

**Example**

> "When there is talk of hatred, let us stand up and talk against it. When there is talk of violence, let us stand up and talk against it."

Here "When there is talk of" (anaphora) and "let us stand up and talk against it" (epistrophe) are both repeated, producing a doubly reinforced structural frame around the varying central content ("hatred," "violence").

### Ethical and Practical Considerations

**Key Points**

- Repetition schemes derive their force partly from rhythm and memorability, which is precisely why they carry a manipulation risk: a repeated phrase can create a *feeling* of mounting truth or inevitability independent of whether the underlying claim is actually warranted — the stylistic analog of the enthymeme's suppressed-premise risk, where audience-supplied conviction substitutes for demonstrated support.
- Ancient rhetorical theory (particularly Quintilian's broader treatment of style) treats these figures as legitimate and valuable when they reinforce a genuinely warranted claim, but cautions against style substituting for substance — a concern paralleling the "purposeless ornamentation" caution raised regarding excessive Ekphrasis.
- Overuse within a single passage produces diminishing returns and can appear mechanical or manipulative to a sophisticated audience, particularly in written (as opposed to orally delivered) contexts where the repeated pattern is more visible to slow, close reading.

[Inference] The precise threshold at which repetition shifts from persuasively rhythmic to manipulatively excessive is not specified with a fixed rule in the classical sources, which discuss appropriateness (*to prepon*) as a general stylistic virtue rather than quantifying an exact frequency; in practice this threshold depends heavily on genre, audience, delivery medium (spoken vs. written), and cultural expectation.

### Application in Executive and Business Communication

**Anaphora in executive use**: Effective for opening a keynote or rallying a team around a shared commitment, especially when each successive clause escalates in specificity or scope.

> "We will invest in our people. We will invest in our infrastructure. We will invest in the communities where we operate."

**Epistrophe in executive use**: Effective for closing remarks or reinforcing a single core value across a series of otherwise varied points, ensuring the audience retains one central takeaway.

> "Our engineers built this platform for reliability. Our support team stands behind it for reliability. Every decision we make going forward will be judged by reliability."

**Anadiplosis in executive use**: Effective for explaining a causal chain of business consequences in change-management or risk communication, where the visual/audible linking reinforces the perceived inevitability of the causal sequence (a stylistic reinforcement of a Toulmin-style warrant, provided the underlying causal claim is actually warranted and not merely asserted through rhythm).

> "Delayed decisions create missed windows. Missed windows create lost market share. Lost market share creates the very crisis we are trying to avoid."

### Practical Construction Guidance

1. **Match the scheme to the desired effect**: choose anaphora for building momentum toward an escalating conclusion, epistrophe for reinforcing a single memorable term, anadiplosis for making a causal or sequential chain feel structurally inevitable.
2. **Verify the underlying claim is warranted** before relying on repetition to carry persuasive weight — repetition should reinforce an already-defensible argument (per the "Constructing Warranted, Defensible Arguments" framework), not substitute for one.
3. **Calibrate frequency to medium**: spoken delivery (keynotes, speeches) tolerates and benefits from more pronounced repetition than written prose (memos, reports), where the same device can read as stylistically heavy-handed.
4. **Avoid stacking multiple repetition schemes within a short passage** unless deliberately constructing symploce, since competing repetition patterns can undercut rather than reinforce each other's rhythmic effect.
