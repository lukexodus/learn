## Quantitative Strategic Planning Matrix

### Overview

The Quantitative Strategic Planning Matrix (QSPM) is a strategy formulation tool used to objectively evaluate and rank alternative strategies based on the key external and internal success factors identified in earlier stages of the strategic planning process. Developed by Fred R. David and presented extensively in his widely used textbook *Strategic Management: Concepts and Cases*, the QSPM sits at the final "Decision Stage" of David's three-stage strategy formulation framework, following the "Input Stage" (data-gathering matrices) and the "Matching Stage" (strategy-generation matrices).

### Position Within David's Three-Stage Strategy Formulation Framework

| Stage | Purpose | Representative Tools |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stage 1: Input Stage** | Summarize basic input information needed to formulate strategies | External Factor Evaluation (EFE) Matrix, Internal Factor Evaluation (IFE) Matrix, Competitive Profile Matrix (CPM) |
| **Stage 2: Matching Stage** | Generate feasible alternative strategies by matching internal and external key factors | SWOT Matrix, Strategic Position and Action Evaluation (SPACE) Matrix, Boston Consulting Group (BCG) Matrix, Internal-External (IE) Matrix, Grand Strategy Matrix |
| **Stage 3: Decision Stage** | Objectively evaluate and rank the alternative strategies generated in Stage 2 | **Quantitative Strategic Planning Matrix (QSPM)** |

**Key Points**

- The QSPM does not generate new strategic alternatives; it evaluates alternatives that have already been generated by Stage 2 matching tools, using the same critical success factors already identified in the Stage 1 input matrices.
- This positioning makes the QSPM the culminating quantitative decision tool in David's framework, designed to bring analytical rigor and objectivity to the final choice among competing strategic alternatives.

### Structure of the QSPM

The QSPM is organized as a matrix with critical success factors listed down the left column, and alternative strategies (typically 2-3, organized into strategy sets that are mutually exclusive within a set) listed across the top as column headers.

| Component | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Key External Factors** | Opportunities and threats, carried over directly from the EFE Matrix, each with an assigned weight |
| **Key Internal Factors** | Strengths and weaknesses, carried over directly from the IFE Matrix, each with an assigned weight |
| **Weight** | The relative importance of each factor (identical to the weight assigned in the EFE/IFE matrices, summing to 1.0 within each factor category) |
| **Attractiveness Score (AS)** | A rating (typically 1 to 4) of how attractive each specific strategy is relative to that specific factor, assigned only to factors judged to actually affect the strategic choice being made |
| **Total Attractiveness Score (TAS)** | The product of the Weight and the Attractiveness Score for each factor-strategy combination |
| **Sum Total Attractiveness Score (STAS)** | The sum of all TAS values in a strategy's column, representing that strategy's overall relative attractiveness |

### Calculation Methodology

**Attractiveness Score (AS) Scale**

- 1 = Not attractive
- 2 = Somewhat attractive
- 3 = Reasonably attractive
- 4 = Highly attractive
- A dash (—) is used, rather than a numeric score, when a given key factor has no effect on the choice being made between the specific strategies being compared; this is a critical methodological rule, since assigning an AS of 1 (as opposed to a dash) implies the factor is relevant but the strategy scores poorly on it, which is a different judgment than the factor being irrelevant.

$$TAS_{i,j} = W_i \times AS_{i,j}$$

Where $W_i$ is the weight of factor $i$ and $AS_{i,j}$ is the attractiveness score of strategy $j$ with respect to factor $i$.

$$STAS_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n} TAS_{i,j}$$

The strategy with the highest Sum Total Attractiveness Score is considered the most attractive relative alternative among those being compared, given the weighted key factors under consideration.

### Step-by-Step Construction Process

**Next Steps for Building a QSPM**

1. **List key external and internal factors**: Carry forward the same opportunities, threats, strengths, and weaknesses (and their associated weights) used in the EFE and IFE matrices from the Input Stage — the QSPM requires this prior analysis to already exist.
2. **Assign weights**: Use the identical weights from the EFE and IFE matrices (each factor category's weights sum to 1.0), ensuring consistency between the input analysis and the final decision tool.
3. **List alternative strategies from the Matching Stage**: Group strategies generated by SWOT, SPACE, BCG, IE, or Grand Strategy matrices into sets of mutually exclusive alternatives that the organization is realistically choosing among (e.g., "Strategy 1: Acquire a regional competitor" vs. "Strategy 2: Organic expansion into adjacent geographic markets").
4. **Determine Attractiveness Scores**: For each key factor, ask "Does this factor affect the choice being made?" If yes, assign an AS from 1 to 4 for each strategy, comparing strategies against each other only relative to that specific factor. If the factor is irrelevant to distinguishing among the strategies, mark it with a dash and move to the next factor.
5. **Compute Total Attractiveness Scores**: Multiply each factor's weight by its AS to produce the TAS for each factor-strategy pair.
6. **Sum the Total Attractiveness Scores**: Add the TAS values down each strategy's column to compute the STAS, revealing the relative attractiveness ranking among the strategy alternatives.
7. **Interpret results in context**: Use the STAS as one significant input to the final strategic decision, not as an automatic, mechanical decision rule that overrides managerial judgment.

### Worked Example (Illustrative)

**Example**

Consider a mid-sized specialty retailer evaluating two strategic alternatives: **Strategy 1** (open new physical stores in underserved regional markets) versus **Strategy 2** (invest heavily in e-commerce platform capability and digital marketing).

In this illustrative partial calculation, Strategy 2 (e-commerce investment) shows a somewhat higher weighted attractiveness score, suggesting — pending a complete factor analysis — a relative lean toward digital investment over physical expansion, though the two scores are close enough that qualitative judgment and factors not fully captured numerically would remain important to the final decision.

### Strengths and Limitations

**Key Points**

*Strengths*

- Introduces a structured, semi-quantitative discipline into what is otherwise often a purely subjective final strategic choice, requiring decision-makers to explicitly connect strategic alternatives back to the specific external and internal factors already identified.
- Can be applied continuously (evaluating any number of strategies simultaneously) and works across virtually any organization type, size, or industry, since it is built entirely from the organization's own prior EFE/IFE analysis rather than external benchmarks.
- The requirement to justify each AS score against a specific factor creates an auditable rationale trail for the strategic decision, useful for internal communication and board-level justification.
- Encourages simultaneous examination of both external (EFE-derived) and internal (IFE-derived) factors within a single evaluative framework, rather than treating external and internal analysis as fully separate exercises.

*Limitations*

- [Inference] The AS ratings, despite producing a numeric output, remain fundamentally subjective judgments made by the strategists involved; the appearance of quantitative rigor (weighted sums, decimal-precision scores) can create a false sense of objectivity if the underlying AS judgments are not well-grounded or are influenced by the preferences of whoever is facilitating the exercise.
- The tool is entirely dependent on the quality and completeness of the prior EFE and IFE matrices; errors, omissions, or biased weightings carried over from the Input Stage propagate directly into the QSPM's conclusions.
- Only evaluates strategies already generated by Stage 2 matching tools — a strong alternative strategy not identified during the Matching Stage will never appear in the QSPM regardless of how good it might be.
- Best suited to evaluating a small number (typically 2-3) of clearly defined mutually exclusive strategic alternatives at a time; it becomes unwieldy as a tool for comparing large numbers of dissimilar strategic options simultaneously.
- Requires meaningful time investment from the strategic planning team to complete rigorously, particularly for organizations with long factor lists carried over from the Input Stage, and rushed AS-scoring session can undermine the tool's core value proposition of forcing careful discrimination between the closely reasoned specific effect of each factor.

### Relationship to Other Strategy Formulation Tools

**Key Points**

- Directly dependent on the **EFE Matrix** and **IFE Matrix** (Input Stage tools) for its factor list and weights — the QSPM cannot be meaningfully constructed without these preceding analyses.
- Evaluates strategic alternatives typically generated by **SWOT Matrix**, **SPACE Matrix**, **BCG Matrix**, **Internal-External (IE) Matrix**, or **Grand Strategy Matrix** analyses conducted during the Matching Stage.
- Distinct from the **Balanced Scorecard** and **Strategy Maps**, which are used *after* a strategy is selected to translate it into measurable objectives and execution plans; the QSPM operates strictly at the point of choosing *among* candidate strategies, prior to implementation planning.
- Complements (rather than replaces) qualitative strategic judgment; David explicitly frames the QSPM as a tool that should inform, not mechanically dictate, the final strategic decision, given the subjective nature of the underlying AS scores.

### Conclusion

The Quantitative Strategic Planning Matrix serves as the culminating "Decision Stage" tool in Fred David's three-stage strategy formulation framework, bringing a structured, semi-quantitative discipline to the final choice among strategic alternatives generated during earlier matching-stage analysis. By requiring strategists to explicitly score each candidate strategy against every relevant external and internal key factor — and to weight those scores according to factor importance already established in the EFE and IFE matrices — the QSPM produces an auditable, factor-grounded rationale for strategic choice. Its principal value lies in the discipline of the process (forcing explicit, factor-by-factor comparison) rather than in the precision of the resulting numeric score, which should be treated as one significant input to, rather than a substitute for, sound managerial judgment.
