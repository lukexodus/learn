## Servant Leadership and Coaching

### Definition and Core Philosophy

Servant leadership is a leadership philosophy in which the leader's primary role is to serve the team, remove obstacles, and enable individual and collective growth, rather than to direct through positional authority. The concept was formalized by Robert K. Greenleaf in his 1970 essay "The Servant as Leader," which posits that the servant-leader is servant first, and the aspiration to lead follows that initial desire to serve.

In project management, this philosophy has become central to Agile and adaptive delivery frameworks, where the Project Manager, Scrum Master, or Team Lead operates as a facilitator rather than a commander. The PMBOK Guide (7th Edition) explicitly incorporates servant leadership as a core principle, shifting away from the traditional "waterfall command-and-control" model.

### Servant Leadership vs. Traditional Leadership

| Dimension | Traditional/Directive Leadership | Servant Leadership |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Power Source | Positional authority | Trust and influence |
| Primary Focus | Organizational goals first | People first, goals follow |
| Decision-Making | Top-down | Collaborative, decentralized |
| Motivation Style | Extrinsic (rewards/punishment) | Intrinsic (purpose, autonomy, mastery) |
| Information Flow | Restricted, need-to-know | Transparent, shared |
| Success Metric | Output/compliance | Team growth + output |
| Role of Leader | Director | Facilitator/Coach |

### The Ten Characteristics of Servant Leadership (Spears' Framework)

Larry Spears, former CEO of the Greenleaf Center, distilled ten characteristics from Greenleaf's writings:

1. **Listening** — Seeking to identify and clarify the will of the group before acting
2. **Empathy** — Understanding others' perspectives and intentions
3. **Healing** — Helping team members work through emotional or interpersonal harm
4. **Awareness** — Self-awareness and situational awareness strengthen the leader's positions
5. **Persuasion** — Relying on convincing argument rather than positional coercion
6. **Conceptualization** — Balancing day-to-day operational focus with long-term vision
7. **Foresight** — Anticipating likely outcomes based on past and current realities
8. **Stewardship** — Holding resources and the organization in trust for the greater good
9. **Commitment to the Growth of People** — Nurturing personal, professional, and spiritual growth of team members
10. **Building Community** — Fostering a sense of community among people who work within an institution

### The Servant Leader's Core Responsibilities in Project Management

**Key Points**

- Remove impediments blocking the team's progress (blockers, resource gaps, cross-team dependencies)
- Shield the team from unnecessary organizational noise and scope churn
- Facilitate rather than dictate decision-making processes
- Foster psychological safety so team members raise risks and problems early
- Develop team members' skills through delegation and coaching, not just task assignment
- Model the behaviors and values expected of the team

### Coaching as a Servant Leadership Competency

Coaching, in this context, refers to a facilitative communication approach that helps individuals discover their own solutions rather than being told what to do. It is distinct from **mentoring** (sharing direct experience/advice), **teaching** (transferring knowledge), and **directing** (issuing instructions).

**Coaching vs. Mentoring vs. Directing**

| Approach | Leader's Role | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Directing | Tells what to do | Crisis, compliance, novice + high-risk task |
| Teaching | Transfers specific knowledge/skill | Skill gap, onboarding |
| Mentoring | Shares experience, offers guidance | Career development, informal guidance |
| Coaching | Asks questions, facilitates self-discovery | Skilled individual needing perspective, growth mindset situations |

### The GROW Coaching Model

The GROW model, developed by Sir John Whitmore and colleagues in the 1980s, is the most widely used coaching framework in professional and project settings.

- **G — Goal**: What does the individual want to achieve? (Specific, time-bound)
- **R — Reality**: What is the current situation? (Facts, obstacles, resources)
- **O — Options**: What possible approaches exist? (Brainstormed without judgment)
- **W — Will (or Way Forward)**: What will the individual commit to doing, and by when?

**Example**

> A team member struggles to estimate tasks accurately, consistently underestimating by 30-40%.
>
> - **Goal**: "I want my estimates to be within 15% of actual effort."
> - **Reality**: "I don't track actuals, and I tend to forget interruptions when estimating."
> - **Options**: "I could use historical velocity data, add a buffer, or pair-estimate with a peer."
> - **Will**: "I'll track actuals for the next two sprints and use three-point estimation starting this sprint."

The PM's role in this dialogue is to ask open-ended questions ("What have you tried before?", "What would success look like?") rather than supplying the answer directly.

### Situational Leadership Integration

Servant leadership is not the absence of directive behavior — it is contextually applied. The Situational Leadership Model (Hersey-Blanchard) complements servant leadership by matching leadership style to team member competence and commitment:

```mermaid
quadrantChart
    title Situational Leadership Applied to Servant Leadership
    x-axis Low Competence --> High Competence
    y-axis Low Commitment --> High Commitment
    quadrant-1 Supporting (Coach)
    quadrant-2 Coaching (Guide)
    quadrant-3 Directing (Instruct)
    quadrant-4 Delegating (Empower)
```

**Key Points**

- New/inexperienced team members may initially require more directive support (S1: Directing)
- As competence grows but confidence wavers, coaching intensifies (S2: Coaching)
- Experienced-but-hesitant contributors benefit from participative support (S3: Supporting)
- Highly skilled, self-motivated team members should be delegated to with minimal oversight (S4: Delegating)

Servant leadership is most visibly expressed in S2–S4, but S1 situations (e.g., safety-critical tasks, compliance requirements) still warrant clear direction — servant leadership does not mean abdicating necessary structure.

### Practical Techniques for PMs

**Key Points**

- **Ask, don't tell**: Replace "Do X" with "What do you think we should do about X?"
- **1:1 coaching cadence**: Regular structured one-on-ones focused on growth, not status updates
- **Retrospective facilitation**: Use techniques like Start-Stop-Continue or the Sailboat Retrospective to surface team-generated improvements
- **Impediment backlogs**: Maintain a visible log of blockers the PM/Scrum Master is actively resolving
- **Delegation with authority**: Assign not just tasks but decision-making authority (RACI clarity)
- **Feedback loops**: Use models like SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact) for constructive feedback delivery

### SBI Feedback Model

- **Situation**: Describe the specific context ("In yesterday's sprint review...")
- **Behavior**: Describe the observable behavior ("...you interrupted the client three times...")
- **Impact**: Describe the effect ("...which made it harder for them to finish their point and may have affected their confidence in us.")

This model keeps feedback factual and non-judgmental, aligning with servant leadership's emphasis on empathy and psychological safety.

### Servant Leadership in Agile Frameworks

- **Scrum**: The Scrum Master is explicitly defined as a servant-leader to the Scrum Team, responsible for removing impediments and coaching the team on self-management (per the Scrum Guide)
- **SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)**: Servant leadership is listed as one of the core Lean-Agile Leader competencies, alongside "leading by example" and "developing people"
- **Kanban**: While less prescriptive on roles, flow managers/service delivery managers apply servant leadership when managing WIP limits and resolving flow-blocking bottlenecks

### Common Pitfalls

**Key Points**

- Conflating servant leadership with permissiveness or lack of accountability — servant leaders still set clear expectations and hold people accountable to commitments
- Over-coaching in situations that require immediate directive action (e.g., safety incidents, hard deadlines with no room for exploration)
- Failing to balance team-member growth with organizational/stakeholder deadlines
- Using coaching questions performatively without genuinely valuing the input received
- Neglecting foresight and conceptualization (the more strategic Spears characteristics) in favor of only interpersonal ones

### Measuring Servant Leadership Effectiveness

[Inference] Because servant leadership outcomes are primarily behavioral and cultural, they are harder to quantify than schedule or cost variance; organizations commonly approximate effectiveness through indirect indicators such as:

- Team psychological safety survey scores (e.g., derived from Google's Project Aristotle research indicators)
- Voluntary attrition/retention rates within the team
- 360-degree feedback specifically targeting facilitation and support behaviors
- Team velocity/throughput stability over time (proxy for reduced friction)
- Frequency and quality of team-raised improvement suggestions in retrospectives
