## Common Points of Negotiation Friction


### Overview

Tax equity transactions involve inherently misaligned incentives between sponsors (who want maximum flexibility, minimum cost of capital, and fast execution) and tax equity investors (who want maximum downside protection, tax risk mitigation, and structural certainty). This structural tension produces a recurring set of negotiation friction points across nearly every deal, regardless of asset class or structure. Understanding these friction points — and market-standard resolutions — allows deal teams to anticipate issues, allocate negotiation capital efficiently, and avoid re-litigating settled market terms.

### Key Points

- Friction points cluster around five categories: economic terms, risk allocation, control/consent rights, tax risk protection, and exit mechanics.
- Most friction points have converged toward market-standard resolutions over the past decade as the tax equity market has matured, though newer technologies (storage, hybrid projects) and post-OBBBA policy uncertainty are reopening some previously settled issues.
- The relative bargaining leverage of sponsor vs. investor shifts with tax equity market supply/demand — in capital-constrained periods, investors extract more favorable terms; in oversupplied periods, sponsors have more leverage.

### 1. Pricing and Yield

**The friction**: Sponsors want to minimize the cost of tax equity capital (measured as dollars raised per dollar of ITC, or as the investor's target after-tax IRR); investors want pricing that compensates for perceived structural, technology, and counterparty risk.

- **ITC pricing**: typically quoted as cents per dollar of ITC (e.g., $0.95-$1.05 per $1.00 of credit), varying with technology maturity, sponsor track record, and structure
- **Target investor IRR**: post-tax IRR targets that have historically ranged in the mid-to-high single digits for high-quality utility-scale solar/wind, with premiums for storage, distributed generation, or first-time sponsors
- **[Inference]** Pricing tension is heightened in periods of tax equity capacity constraints (e.g., following major regulatory shifts that increase demand for tax credit monetization) relative to periods of ample bank and insurance-company tax capacity.

**Market resolution**: Pricing is typically set through competitive process (multiple term sheets) rather than bilateral negotiation, minimizing friction once a counterparty is selected, though pricing can still shift between term sheet and closing if diligence reveals new risks.

### 2. Basis and Valuation for ITC-Eligible Structures

**The friction**: In sale-leaseback and related-party contribution structures, the tax equity investor's ITC is based on the project's fair market value (FMV), not necessarily cost. Sponsors have an incentive to support a higher FMV (maximizing ITC); the IRS scrutinizes inflated FMV claims, and investors bear audit risk if basis is successfully challenged.

- Disputes typically center on the appraisal methodology (cost approach vs. income approach vs. market approach) and comparable transaction selection
- **[Inference]** Post-OBBBA, heightened IRS enforcement focus on ITC basis claims — particularly for solar and storage — has made FMV negotiation more contentious than in prior years, with investors increasingly requiring cost-basis-anchored appraisals or additional indemnity protection.

**Market resolution**: Independent third-party appraisal firms with established IRS-audit track records; investor's tax counsel typically requires "should"-level comfort that the appraisal will withstand IRS scrutiny.

### 3. Risk Allocation: Construction, Completion, and Performance

**The friction**: Investors generally will not accept construction-stage or technology performance risk; sponsors want to avoid over-guaranteeing outcomes they cannot fully control (e.g., weather-dependent resource variance, supply chain delays).

Key negotiated provisions:

- **Completion guaranty**: sponsor or sponsor-parent guarantee that the project will reach mechanical completion and placed-in-service status by a specified date, often with liquidated damages for delay
- **Performance guaranty / true-up mechanisms**: for wind/solar, provisions addressing shortfalls versus P50/P90 energy yield projections, sometimes via true-up capital contribution adjustments
- **EPC contractor risk pass-through**: extent to which sponsor passes EPC contractor liquidated damages and warranty claims through to the investor versus retaining that risk at the sponsor level
- **Curtailment and interconnection risk**: allocation of revenue risk if the interconnecting utility curtails output

**Market resolution**: Sponsor typically retains construction completion risk via guaranty; performance risk allocation varies more by deal and technology, with storage and newer technologies seeing more investor-favorable (sponsor-retained-risk) terms given limited operating history.

### 4. Consent Rights and Investor Control

**The friction**: Investors want approval rights over major decisions (refinancing, material contract amendments, asset sales, additional indebtedness) to protect their tax and economic position; sponsors want operational flexibility and want to avoid investor veto power slowing routine business decisions.

Typical negotiated consent thresholds:

- **Major decisions requiring investor consent**: admission of new members, material amendments to project documents (PPA, EPC, interconnection), incurrence of additional debt above negotiated thresholds, affiliate transactions
- **Budget approval rights**: annual operating budget approval, particularly for capital expenditures above a negotiated threshold
- **Removal rights**: investor's ability to remove the managing member for cause (fraud, gross negligence, insolvency) — heavily negotiated regarding the definition of "cause" and cure periods

**Market resolution**: Consent lists have become relatively standardized in the market, with negotiation focused on dollar thresholds and cure period lengths rather than the categories themselves.

### 5. Tax Risk Allocation

**The friction**: If the IRS successfully challenges the transaction's tax treatment (recharacterization of the investor as a lender rather than equity owner, ITC basis reduction, recapture triggered by a disqualifying event), who bears the economic loss?

Key negotiated provisions:

- **Tax indemnity**: sponsor indemnifies investor for loss of anticipated tax benefits due to sponsor actions (e.g., a disqualifying disposition triggering ITC recapture under the 5-year recapture schedule)
- **Change in law provisions**: allocation of risk if tax law changes after closing but before benefits are realized — increasingly relevant given the phase-down and eligibility changes introduced by OBBBA
- **Indemnity caps and survival periods**: negotiated dollar caps and time limits on sponsor's indemnification obligations

**Market resolution**: Sponsor indemnifies for sponsor-caused disqualifying events (asset sale, change in use); investor generally bears general tax law change risk absent specific carve-outs, though this allocation is increasingly contested given recent legislative volatility.

### 6. Flip Point Mechanics and True-Ups

**The friction**: In partnership flip structures, disputes arise over how and when the flip to majority sponsor ownership is measured and confirmed.

- **Target IRR flip vs. fixed-date flip**: whether the flip occurs upon the investor achieving a target IRR (common) or on a fixed calendar date (PAYGO structures)
- **Flip date confirmation mechanics**: who calculates and certifies achievement of the flip (often an independent accountant), and dispute resolution if sponsor and investor models diverge
- **Minimum gain and capital account true-ups**: adjustments required to maintain compliance with §704(b) substantial economic effect rules

**Market resolution**: Independent accountant certification of flip date achievement is now standard; disputes are typically resolved through pre-agreed model mechanics embedded in the LLC Agreement rather than open renegotiation.

### 7. Exit Mechanics: Purchase Options and ROFR

**The friction**: Sponsors want a clear, affordable path to buy out the investor's residual interest post-flip; investors want to ensure any purchase option price reflects genuine fair market value (to avoid IRS recharacterization risk suggesting a pre-arranged sale) while still receiving adequate compensation for their post-flip residual interest.

Key provisions:

- **Fixed-price call option risk**: IRS guidance and case law (e.g., historical scrutiny of below-FMV purchase options) generally require the sponsor's call option to be priced at fair market value at exercise, not a pre-agreed fixed price, to avoid recharacterization risk
- **Right of first offer/refusal (ROFR)** mechanics if the investor wishes to exit before the sponsor exercises its call
- **FMV determination process** for the exit — typically a third-party appraisal process with dispute resolution (baseball arbitration or similar)

**Market resolution**: FMV-based call options with defined appraisal mechanics are now standard market practice; friction centers on the appraisal process details (timing, appraiser selection, dispute resolution mechanism) rather than the FMV requirement itself.

### 8. Force Majeure and Change-in-Law Risk (Emerging Friction Point)

**The friction**: Post-OBBBA and amid ongoing policy volatility around IRA-era tax credits, sponsors and investors increasingly negotiate protective provisions addressing the risk of further legislative or regulatory change affecting credit value or eligibility after term sheet signing but before funding.

- **[Speculation]** This is an area of active market evolution; provisions such as "MAC-out" rights tied to adverse tax law changes, or repricing mechanisms triggered by eligibility changes, may become more standardized as the market adapts to post-OBBBA conditions, but market practice has not yet fully converged.

### Example: Negotiating a Curtailment Risk Dispute

A wind project's interconnection agreement permits the utility to curtail output during high-supply periods. The IE report flags this as a material revenue risk.

- **Sponsor's opening position**: curtailment is a market risk inherent to the resource, and the investor's IRR model should simply reflect a discount for expected curtailment, with no further protection
- **Investor's opening position**: sponsor should guarantee a minimum revenue floor or provide a true-up capital contribution mechanism if curtailment exceeds modeled assumptions
- **Typical market resolution**: parties agree to model curtailment using a conservative (e.g., P90) assumption rather than P50, reducing the gap between projected and worst-case outcomes, without a full revenue guarantee — splitting the risk through conservative modeling rather than a binary risk transfer
