## Legislative Risk Monitoring Beyond the 2025 Overhaul


### Overview

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law on July 4, 2025, represented the most significant overhaul of clean energy tax policy since the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) of 2022. But OBBBA was not a terminal event — it triggered an active, ongoing legislative environment in which both restorative and further-restrictive bills continue to be introduced, executive branch actions continue to reshape sub-regulatory interpretation, and the 2026 midterm election cycle carries direct implications for the durability of current law. This topic addresses the practice of legislative risk monitoring as a distinct discipline from guidance tracking (covered separately) — focusing specifically on statutory change risk originating from Congress and the executive branch, rather than interpretive guidance from Treasury and the IRS.

### Key Points

- OBBBA is best understood as a baseline, not an endpoint: most of the IRA's energy incentives were originally legislated to expire automatically by 2032, but instead of letting them sunset, the OBBBA terminated most of these programs in 2026 or earlier, generating $496 billion in savings over ten years — a scale of change large enough to invite continued legislative attention from both restorative and further-restrictive factions
- Legislative risk monitoring must track bills at the introduction stage, not just enacted law, since pending legislation signals both political intent and potential future deal risk or opportunity
- Executive branch actions (executive orders directing agency interpretation) have already materially affected deal-relevant definitions once since OBBBA's enactment, and remain an ongoing risk vector independent of formal legislation
- The 2026 midterm election cycle is a structurally important date for legislative risk assessment, since control of Congress directly affects the viability of both restorative and further-restrictive bills

### What OBBBA Actually Changed (Baseline for Monitoring)

Legislative risk monitoring requires a clear baseline understanding of current law before tracking proposed changes against it:

- **Wind and solar (45Y/48E)**: wind and solar facilities must begin construction by July 4, 2026, and be operational by December 31, 2027, to remain eligible, while other technologies like hydropower, nuclear, and geothermal face graduated phase-outs until 2036
- **Clean vehicle credits**: terminated effective September 30, 2025 for new and used EV credits and commercial clean vehicles
- **Section 45X advanced manufacturing**: wind components sold after 2027 would no longer be eligible for the credit, with other component-specific phase-outs
- **Section 45Z clean fuel production credit**: extended two years through 2029 with modifications, including prohibiting feedstocks other than those produced in the U.S, Canada or Mexico
- **Transferability preserved**: the tax credit transferability aspect under Section 6418 remained whole, despite previous versions of the bill proposing either a sunset date or outright repeal of transferability — a critical point since transferability was itself legislatively at risk during OBBBA's drafting process, illustrating that even "settled" provisions can be reopened during active legislative negotiation
- **FEOC restrictions**: introduced stricter foreign ownership and sourcing requirements limiting or disallowing credits for projects with significant ties to China, Russia, North Korea, or Iran

### Post-OBBBA Legislative Activity

**1. Executive branch action affecting interpretation (already realized risk)**

Legislative risk is not limited to Congress. An executive order issued shortly after OBBBA's enactment already triggered a material interpretive shift: in response to the executive order, the Internal Revenue Service issued Notice 2025-42, which removed the long-standing "5 percent safe harbor" for wind and large solar projects that begin construction on or after September 2, 2025. This illustrates that executive branch directives can alter deal-critical definitions (such as beginning-of-construction qualification) without new legislation, and this channel remains active and must be monitored alongside formal bills.

**2. Restorative legislation (Republican-sponsored)**

The American Energy Dominance Act, introduced by a bipartisan-adjacent group of House Republicans, would remove the accelerated deadlines that the One Big Beautiful Bill Act placed on the renewable energy 45Y production tax credit and 48E investment tax credit, and make similar changes to other impacted credits, like the 45V clean hydrogen production credit. Notably, this legislation was developed in direct partnership with the North America's Building Trades Unions, signaling a labor-industry coalition backing restoration of longer credit horizons, and would also fully restore the 179D, or Energy Efficient Commercial Buildings Deduction credit, without a scheduled expiration.

**3. Restorative legislation (Democratic-sponsored)**

Separately, Democratic lawmakers introduced a clean electricity bill in March 2026 that would, among other measures, restore clean energy tax credits that were eliminated by the OBBBA — reflecting continued partisan divergence on energy tax policy even after OBBBA's enactment.

**4. Political viability assessment**

Industry analysts have offered a candid assessment of the restorative bills' prospects: the bill, which would restore clean electricity, hydrogen, and energy efficiency tax credits, may find support if Democrats win the House or Senate in the 2026 elections, directly tying legislative risk monitoring to the electoral calendar. **[Inference]** This assessment implies that under current (pre-midterm) political control, restorative legislation faces a difficult path to enactment, making the 2026 midterm elections a pivotal date for reassessing the durability of current phase-down schedules.

### Market and Investment Impact of Legislative Uncertainty

The scale of legislative risk is reflected in market-wide investment projections. Analysis has estimated that the combined effects of OBBBA rescissions, credit phase-outs, and policy uncertainty put over \$522 billion in announced clean energy investments at risk, with projected new clean power capacity anticipated to decline substantially through 2035 compared to pre-OBBBA projections. At the same time, some analysts note offsetting demand-side pressure: with data centers projected to consume a significant share of all US energy by 2028, large operators have strong incentives to advocate for preserving and expanding renewable tax credits to meet massive energy demands — suggesting that corporate and utility demand-side lobbying may itself become a legislative risk-mitigating factor worth tracking as a countervailing force.

**[Inference]** This dynamic suggests that legislative risk monitoring should track not only bill text but also the coalition of interest groups (labor unions, data center operators, utilities, manufacturers) advocating for or against restoration, since coalition strength is often a better predictor of legislative viability than bill introduction alone.

### Practical Deal Implications

**1. Beginning-of-construction timing as the central legislative risk variable**

Given that wind and solar facilities must begin construction by July 4, 2026, or failing that, be placed in service by December 31, 2027, this single deadline has become the primary structuring variable driven by legislative risk. Sponsors and investors negotiating deals in 2026 must assess:

- Whether a project can realistically achieve a qualifying beginning-of-construction date under the heightened substantiation standard following removal of the 5% safe harbor for projects beginning construction on or after September 2, 2025
- Contingency structuring if legislative relief (such as the American Energy Dominance Act) were to pass, potentially restoring longer timelines — though prudent deal teams generally should not price or structure deals assuming pending legislation will pass, per standard risk-conservative practice

**2. Contract drafting for legislative change risk**

Given the active legislative environment, deal documents increasingly need explicit provisions addressing the possibility of mid-transaction legislative change — both restorative changes that could increase credit value or extend eligibility windows, and further-restrictive changes that could impair previously assumed structuring.

**3. Monitoring cadence**

**[Inference]** Given the pace of legislative activity observed since OBBBA's enactment (including at least two competing restorative bills introduced within the first year), practitioners should treat legislative monitoring as a continuous rather than periodic activity, with particular attention to committee markup activity and any bill gaining bipartisan co-sponsorship, since bipartisan coalitions (as seen in the labor-backed American Energy Dominance Act) have historically higher enactment probability than single-party proposals.

### Distinguishing Legislative Risk from Guidance Risk

| Dimension | Legislative Risk | Guidance/Regulatory Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Congress (statute) or Executive Order | Treasury/IRS (notices, regulations) |
| Change mechanism | Requires bill passage and presidential signature (or veto override) | Agency rulemaking or interpretation, generally faster |
| Typical timeline | Months to years; tied to legislative calendar and elections | Weeks to months; tied to agency rulemaking process |
| Current examples | American Energy Dominance Act; Democratic clean electricity bill | Notice 2026-15 (FEOC); Notice 2025-42 (safe harbor removal) |
| Monitoring approach | Track bill introduction, committee action, floor votes, election outcomes | Track notices, proposed regs, comment periods (see related topic) |

### Example: Assessing Legislative Risk for a Mid-2026 Deal

A sponsor negotiating a wind project's tax equity term sheet in September 2026 faces a beginning-of-construction deadline of July 4, 2026 that has already passed for new projects seeking the standard safe harbor — meaning any project not already qualified under pre-deadline rules must instead pursue the placed-in-service pathway, needing to be operational by December 31, 2027. Legislative risk monitoring would inform the deal team that: (1) the American Energy Dominance Act, if enacted, could retroactively or prospectively ease this deadline, but its enactment prospects depend heavily on the outcome of the 2026 midterm elections; (2) absent legislative change, the December 31, 2027 placed-in-service deadline is a hard structural constraint the deal must be built around; and (3) further-restrictive risk (e.g., additional FEOC tightening) remains an active parallel risk channel requiring separate tracking.
