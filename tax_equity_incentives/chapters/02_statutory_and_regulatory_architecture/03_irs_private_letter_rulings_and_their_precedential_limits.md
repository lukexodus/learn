## IRS Private Letter Rulings and Their Precedential Limits


### Definition

A Private Letter Ruling (PLR) is a written determination issued by the IRS National Office in response to a specific taxpayer's written request, addressing how the tax law applies to that taxpayer's particular, fully-disclosed set of facts, typically in advance of a transaction being completed. In the tax equity context, PLRs have historically played an outsized role in giving market participants comfort on structuring questions not squarely resolved by statute or regulation.

### Statutory and Procedural Basis

PLRs are issued under the IRS's general ruling authority, with procedures governed annually by a Revenue Procedure (e.g., the "Rev. Proc. 20XX-1" series) that sets out the process, required disclosures, user fees, and areas in which the IRS will or will not rule.

**Key Points**

- A taxpayer requests a PLR before undertaking a transaction (or, in limited circumstances, shortly after), describing the relevant facts and proposed tax treatment.
- The IRS may rule favorably, unfavorably, decline to rule, or rule only on certain issues while declining others.
- PLRs are only binding on the IRS with respect to the specific requesting taxpayer and the specific facts described in the request.

### Precedential Limits — IRC §6110(k)(3)

The central limitation on PLRs is codified in IRC §6110(k)(3), which explicitly states that a PLR **may not be used or cited as precedent** by any taxpayer other than the one to whom it was issued.

**Key Points**

- A PLR issued to Taxpayer A provides no direct legal protection or reliance right for Taxpayer B, even if Taxpayer B's facts appear identical.
- Other taxpayers and their advisors nonetheless routinely study published (redacted) PLRs to infer likely IRS positions and reasoning on recurring structuring questions, even though this is technically informal, non-binding guidance-by-analogy rather than citable authority.
- The IRS publishes PLRs in redacted form (identifying details removed) approximately weeks after issuance, making the reasoning — though not the precedential force — publicly available.

$$\text{Binding Effect of PLR} = \begin{cases} \text{Binds IRS} & \text{if taxpayer = original requester AND facts match as represented} \\ \text{No binding effect} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

### Why PLRs Still Matter Heavily in Tax Equity Practice

Despite their narrow technical precedential force, PLRs have historically shaped market practice in tax equity for several practical reasons:

1. **Novel structuring questions** — partnership flip structures, sale-leasebacks, and inverted leases involve complex, fact-intensive applications of partnership tax law (particularly IRC §704(b) substantial economic effect rules) where statutory and regulatory guidance leaves genuine ambiguity.
2. **Reasoning transferability** — while the ruling itself isn't citable, the IRS's articulated reasoning in a published PLR gives practitioners a strong signal of how the IRS is likely to analyze similar future transactions, informing both deal structuring and the confidence level of tax opinions.
3. **Absence of alternative guidance** — in areas where Treasury has not issued regulations or the IRS has not issued a Revenue Ruling, published PLRs may be the only publicly available window into IRS thinking on a specific structuring question.
4. **Investor comfort** — some historically significant tax equity market developments (for example, early confirmation of partnership flip structures' viability) were substantially informed by PLR practice before more general guidance caught up.

[Inference] Market practitioners are widely understood to treat a consistent pattern across multiple published PLRs addressing similar fact patterns as stronger informal evidence of IRS position than any single PLR alone, even though neither individually nor collectively do PLRs constitute binding precedent for other taxpayers.

### PLRs vs. Other Guidance Types — Comparative Table

| Guidance Type | Binding on IRS Generally? | Citable as Precedent by Other Taxpayers? | Typical Use in Tax Equity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Treasury Regulation (final) | Yes, broadly | Yes | Primary interpretive authority |
| Revenue Ruling | Yes, for similar facts | Yes | Addresses recurring fact patterns |
| Revenue Procedure | Yes, procedurally | Yes | Administrative/election mechanics |
| IRS Notice | Yes, as interim guidance | Yes, generally treated as guidance | Fast-moving areas (e.g., BOC rules) |
| Private Letter Ruling | Only for the requesting taxpayer | No — explicitly barred under §6110(k)(3) | Informal signal on novel structuring questions |
| Technical Advice Memorandum | Only for the specific examination | No | IRS field guidance during audit |

### Practical Risk if a PLR Is Relied Upon Improperly

**Example**

A tax equity investor's counsel identifies a published PLR addressing a partnership allocation structure closely resembling a proposed deal and cites it in an internal memo as support. If challenged on audit, the IRS is under no obligation to apply the same reasoning to this different taxpayer, and the taxpayer cannot compel consistent treatment merely by pointing to the earlier PLR. Sound practice is to treat the PLR as informative background supporting the reasonableness of a position — properly documented in a tax opinion grounded independently in the Code, regulations, and citable authority — rather than as a substitute for that independent analysis.

### Revocation and Reliance Risk

**Key Points**

- The IRS can revoke or modify a PLR, generally prospectively, if it later determines the ruling was based on a misstatement or omission of material facts, or if it changes its position on the underlying legal question.
- If facts change after a PLR is issued, or if the actual transaction deviates from what was represented in the request, the IRS is not bound by the original ruling.
- Because of these limits, sophisticated tax equity participants typically pursue a PLR (when they do at all) as one element of a broader risk-mitigation package that also includes a robust independent tax opinion and, increasingly, tax credit insurance to address residual risk that a PLR cannot fully eliminate.

### When Tax Equity Participants Pursue a PLR Today

- Highly novel fact patterns not addressed by existing regulations, notices, or published rulings.
- Large transactions where the cost and time of the PLR process (which can take many months) is justified by the deal's size and the value of additional certainty.
- Situations where the requesting taxpayer itself will be the ongoing party at risk (since only that taxpayer benefits from the ruling's binding effect).

[Unverified] The overall frequency and centrality of PLR requests specifically for tax equity partnership structuring, relative to reliance on published regulations, notices, and tax opinions alone, varies over time and by deal type; current practice should be assessed against up-to-date market commentary rather than assumed to be static.
