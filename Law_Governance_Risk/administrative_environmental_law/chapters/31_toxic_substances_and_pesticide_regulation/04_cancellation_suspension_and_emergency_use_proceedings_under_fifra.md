## Cancellation, Suspension, and Emergency Use Proceedings Under FIFRA

### Overview

FIFRA's registration system is not static — EPA retains ongoing authority to remove a pesticide from the market, in whole or in part, when new information reveals that a registered use no longer satisfies the statutory standard. This authority operates through two distinct procedural tracks with markedly different speed and due-process characteristics: **cancellation**, the standard deliberative process for terminating a registration, and **suspension**, an expedited emergency mechanism for halting sale or use while cancellation proceedings play out. A third, conceptually separate mechanism — **§18 emergency exemptions** — operates in the opposite direction, allowing time-limited *use* of a pesticide that lacks a registration for the specific emergency circumstance at hand. Together these three mechanisms define FIFRA's dynamic, ongoing regulatory oversight beyond initial registration.

### Statutory Basis

- **FIFRA §6(a)** — 7 U.S.C. §136d(a): information reporting and voluntary cancellation
- **FIFRA §6(b)** — 7 U.S.C. §136d(b): cancellation and change in classification
- **FIFRA §6(c)** — 7 U.S.C. §136d(c): suspension
- **FIFRA §18** — 7 U.S.C. §136p: emergency exemptions

---

### Cancellation (§6(b))

#### **Key Points**

- EPA may cancel a pesticide registration when it determines that the pesticide or its labeling **does not comply with FIFRA's requirements**, or that its use **generally causes unreasonable adverse effects on the environment** — the same risk-benefit standard applied at initial registration.
- Cancellation can apply to an **entire registration** or be **limited to specific uses, formulations, or geographic areas**, allowing EPA to remove only the problematic application while preserving uses that remain acceptable.
- The cancellation process is **deliberative and procedurally protective** of registrant due process rights, reflecting Congress's judgment that removing an already-marketed, economically established product from commerce warrants substantial procedural safeguards.

#### Cancellation Procedure

1. EPA issues a **notice of intent to cancel**, published in the Federal Register, along with the scientific basis for the proposed action.
2. **Registrants and other interested persons** may request a **hearing** before an administrative law judge (ALJ) to contest the proposed cancellation.
3. If a hearing is requested, the matter proceeds through **formal adjudication**, including discovery, expert testimony, and briefing — a process that can take substantial time (often measured in years for contested, scientifically complex cancellations).
4. EPA's **Scientific Advisory Panel (SAP)**, an independent panel of outside experts, frequently provides scientific peer review input on the underlying risk assessment supporting a proposed cancellation, particularly for high-profile or scientifically contested actions.
5. The ALJ issues a recommended decision; EPA's Administrator issues the final agency decision, which is subject to **judicial review** in the federal courts of appeals.

#### Voluntary Cancellation (§6(f))

- A registrant may voluntarily request cancellation of its own registration (e.g., due to declining commercial viability, reformulation plans, or a strategic decision to exit a product line ahead of anticipated adverse EPA action).
- EPA must generally provide notice and an opportunity for comment even for voluntary cancellations, since other parties (e.g., other registrants of the same active ingredient, agricultural user groups) may have an interest in continued availability.
- Voluntary cancellation is frequently used to **avoid the more adversarial, evidence-intensive §6(b) process**, particularly where a registrant recognizes that continued defense of a registration is unlikely to succeed or is not commercially worthwhile.

#### Existing Stocks Provisions

- Upon cancellation, EPA typically addresses the disposition of **existing stocks** already in the distribution chain or held by end users — determining whether such stocks may continue to be sold, distributed, or used for some transition period, or must be disposed of.
- Existing stocks determinations balance the goal of promptly removing risk against the practical and economic disruption of immediately rendering lawfully manufactured and purchased product unusable.

### Suspension (§6(c))

#### **Key Points**

- Suspension is an **emergency, expedited mechanism** allowing EPA to immediately halt sale, distribution, or use of a pesticide **while cancellation proceedings are pending**, where EPA determines that continued availability during the (often lengthy) cancellation process would pose an **imminent hazard**.
- Suspension is **not a substitute** for cancellation — it is a temporary bridge measure, and full cancellation proceedings must still run their course (or reach voluntary resolution) to permanently terminate the registration.
- FIFRA provides for two distinct types of suspension, differentiated by urgency:

#### Ordinary Suspension

- Requires EPA to determine that an imminent hazard exists and that suspension is warranted **pending completion of cancellation proceedings**.
- The registrant is entitled to an **expedited hearing** (typically far faster than a full cancellation hearing) before the suspension takes effect, though this hearing is significantly abbreviated compared to full cancellation adjudication.

#### Emergency Suspension

- Reserved for situations of such **extreme urgency** that EPA determines an emergency exists that does not allow sufficient time even for the expedited ordinary-suspension hearing process.
- EPA may order an **emergency suspension effective immediately upon issuance**, with the registrant's hearing rights preserved but occurring **after** the suspension has already taken effect, rather than as a precondition to it.
- This is the most extraordinary FIFRA enforcement tool, reserved for the clearest and most time-sensitive risk scenarios, given that it deprives the registrant of any pre-deprivation hearing.

#### **[Inference]** Due Process Calibration Across the Three Mechanisms

The cancellation/ordinary suspension/emergency suspension trio reflects a deliberate statutory calibration: as the urgency of the risk increases, the pre-deprivation procedural protections afforded to the registrant correspondingly decrease, with emergency suspension representing the point at which Congress judged that immediate risk avoidance outweighs the value of a pre-action hearing, subject to post-deprivation review.

### Emergency Use Exemptions (§18)

#### **Key Points**

- Operates in the **opposite direction** from cancellation/suspension: §18 allows the **use** of a pesticide product **without the otherwise-required registration** for a specific use, in response to an urgent, non-routine pest situation for which no adequately effective registered alternative is reasonably available.
- Available to **federal or state agencies** (states typically apply on behalf of growers or other affected parties) rather than directly to individual pesticide users.
- Four recognized categories of emergency conditions historically include:
  1. **Specific exemptions** — a specific, non-emergency pest problem for which time did not permit a normal registration
  2. **Quarantine exemptions** — addressing an isolated pest infestation posing quarantine significance
  3. **Public health exemptions** — significant risk to human health
  4. **Crisis exemptions** — the most urgent category, allowing use without prior EPA approval where the emergency situation does not allow time for the normal review process, subject to immediate post-use notification to EPA

#### **[Inference]** Practical Use Pattern

Section 18 exemptions are commonly sought by state agricultural agencies facing an unexpected pest outbreak (e.g., an invasive species or a resistant pest strain) for which no currently registered pesticide provides adequate control, and are typically time-limited (often tied to a single growing season) and geographically bounded to the specific emergency circumstances justifying the exemption, rather than functioning as a general registration substitute.

---

### **Example**

A widely used agricultural insecticide, registered for decades, becomes the subject of new epidemiological and ecological data suggesting substantial, previously unrecognized risk to aquatic invertebrates and elevated cancer risk among agricultural workers with high occupational exposure.

1. EPA reviews the new data and determines the registered uses likely **generally cause unreasonable adverse effects on the environment**, issuing a **notice of intent to cancel** the relevant agricultural uses under §6(b).
2. The registrant requests a **hearing**, triggering formal adjudicatory proceedings; EPA also refers the underlying risk assessment to its **Scientific Advisory Panel** for independent peer review given the scientific complexity and stakes involved.
3. While the cancellation hearing is pending — a process expected to take a significant period — new incident reports reveal acute, severe worker poisonings occurring with alarming frequency during the current growing season, and EPA concludes the risk is sufficiently urgent that continued sale during the pending cancellation process would constitute an **imminent hazard**.
4. Given the acute, ongoing nature of the poisoning incidents, EPA determines that even an expedited ordinary suspension hearing process would not occur quickly enough to prevent further harm, and issues an **emergency suspension** effective immediately, halting further sale and use pending the registrant's post-suspension hearing.
5. Separately, a state agricultural department, facing an unrelated and unexpected invasive pest outbreak threatening a specialty crop with no adequately effective registered pesticide available, applies for and receives a **§18 crisis exemption**, allowing emergency use of a different (unregistered-for-that-use) pesticide for the remainder of the growing season, with immediate post-use notification to EPA.

### Comparative Table: Cancellation, Suspension, and Emergency Exemption

| Mechanism | Direction of Action | Standard | Pre-Action Hearing | Typical Duration/Scope |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cancellation (§6(b)) | Terminates a registration | Generally causes unreasonable adverse effects; non-compliance with FIFRA | Full adjudicatory hearing available before final agency action | Can take years for contested cases |
| Ordinary suspension (§6(c)) | Halts sale/use pending cancellation | Imminent hazard exists | Expedited hearing before suspension takes effect | Bridges the gap until cancellation resolves |
| Emergency suspension (§6(c)) | Halts sale/use immediately | Emergency precludes even expedited hearing | Hearing occurs after suspension takes effect | Immediate; most extraordinary tool |
| Section 18 emergency exemption | Permits otherwise-unregistered use | Urgent, non-routine pest situation with no adequate registered alternative | N/A (permits use rather than restricting it) | Time-limited, often single season |
