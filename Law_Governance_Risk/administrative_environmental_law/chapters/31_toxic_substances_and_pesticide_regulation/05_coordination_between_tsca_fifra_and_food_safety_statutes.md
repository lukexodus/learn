## Coordination Between TSCA, FIFRA, and Food Safety Statutes


### Overview

Chemical and pesticide regulation in the United States is distributed across several statutes that were enacted at different times, with different regulatory philosophies, and administered under overlapping but distinct EPA offices — yet chemical substances frequently move across these statutory boundaries as they progress from raw chemical to formulated product to residue in food. Understanding how TSCA, FIFRA, and the food safety statutes (principally the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, as amended) interlock — and, critically, where TSCA's general jurisdiction is expressly **excluded** in favor of these more specific regimes — is essential to correctly identifying which statute governs a given chemical or use.

### Statutory Basis

- **TSCA §3(2)(B)** — 15 U.S.C. §2602(2)(B): TSCA's exclusion of substances regulated under other statutes, including pesticides and certain food/drug/cosmetic-related uses
- **FIFRA §2(u)** — 7 U.S.C. §136(u): definition of "pesticide"
- **Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FFDCA) §408** — 21 U.S.C. §346a: pesticide tolerances for residues in food
- **Food Quality Protection Act of 1996 (FQPA)** — Pub. L. 104-170: amended both FIFRA and FFDCA to harmonize pesticide residue and registration standards

---

### The TSCA Exclusion for Pesticides and Other Regulated Substances

#### **Key Points**

- TSCA's definition of "chemical substance" under §3(2)(B) expressly **excludes** certain categories of substances that are separately regulated under other federal statutes, including:
  - **Pesticides**, as defined under FIFRA, when manufactured, processed, or distributed for use as a pesticide
  - **Tobacco and tobacco products**
  - Certain nuclear source, byproduct, and special nuclear material
  - **Foods, food additives, drugs, cosmetics, and devices**, as those terms are defined under the FFDCA, when manufactured, processed, or distributed for those specific uses
- This exclusion means TSCA does **not** duplicate regulation already occurring under FIFRA or the FFDCA — the exclusion is jurisdictional, not merely a matter of regulatory preference, and prevents a chemical from being simultaneously subject to full TSCA review and FIFRA/FFDCA review for the **same regulated use**.
- **A single chemical substance can nonetheless fall under multiple statutes for different uses.** A chemical manufactured for use as an industrial solvent is subject to TSCA; if the same chemical (or a closely related compound) is also formulated and sold as a pesticide active ingredient, that pesticide use falls under FIFRA, not TSCA — because the exclusion turns on the specific manufactured/processed/distributed **use**, not on some inherent property of the molecule itself.

#### **[Inference]** Practical Classification Question

Because the TSCA/FIFRA boundary depends on **use** rather than chemical identity, practitioners evaluating a new chemical's regulatory pathway must first determine whether the intended use falls within FIFRA's "pesticide" definition (§2(u): substances intended for preventing, destroying, repelling, or mitigating pests, or intended for use as a plant regulator, defoliant, or desiccant) — if so, TSCA's PMN/§5 process does not apply to that use, and FIFRA registration governs instead.

### The Pesticide-Chemical/Food Safety Interface

#### **Key Points**

When a pesticide is intended for use on food or feed crops, its regulation requires **coordinated action under both FIFRA and the FFDCA**, since two distinct regulatory questions arise:

1. **May the pesticide be sold and used at all?** — Governed by FIFRA §3 registration (the risk-benefit "unreasonable adverse effects" standard).
2. **What level of pesticide residue, if any, may lawfully remain on food when it reaches consumers?** — Governed by FFDCA §408, which requires EPA to establish a **tolerance** (the maximum legally permissible pesticide residue level) or grant an **exemption from the tolerance requirement** for any pesticide chemical residue in or on food.

#### The Food Quality Protection Act of 1996 (FQPA)

The FQPA substantially harmonized the FIFRA registration standard and the FFDCA tolerance standard, addressing inconsistencies that had existed between the two statutes since their original enactment:

- **Unified safety standard**: FQPA established that a tolerance may be set only if EPA determines the tolerance is **"safe,"** meaning there is a **reasonable certainty that no harm will result** from aggregate exposure to the pesticide chemical residue, including all anticipated dietary exposure and other exposure for which there is reliable information.
- **Aggregate exposure requirement**: EPA must consider **aggregate exposure** from all sources (dietary, drinking water, and residential/non-occupational exposure), not merely dietary residue in isolation.
- **Cumulative risk assessment**: EPA must consider **cumulative effects** of pesticides and other substances that share a **common mechanism of toxicity**, rather than evaluating each pesticide's residue in isolation from chemically or toxicologically related substances.
- **Special protections for children**: FQPA requires EPA to apply an additional **tenfold (10x) margin of safety** for pesticide residue tolerances to account for potential increased susceptibility of infants and children, unless reliable data supports a different margin.
- **Endocrine disruptor screening**: FQPA directed EPA to develop a screening program for substances that may have an effect on the human endocrine system, implemented through the **Endocrine Disruptor Screening Program (EDSP)**.

#### Coordinated Registration and Tolerance Process

- A pesticide manufacturer seeking registration for a **food-use pesticide** must submit residue chemistry data supporting a proposed tolerance **simultaneously** with (or closely coordinated with) the FIFRA §3 registration application, since a food-use registration generally cannot be granted without a corresponding tolerance (or applicable exemption) being in place.
- EPA's **Office of Pesticide Programs (OPP)** administers both the FIFRA registration process and the FFDCA tolerance-setting process, providing a degree of internal coordination since a single office handles both determinations, even though they arise under different statutes.
- Import tolerances must also be established for pesticide residues on imported food commodities, ensuring imported food is held to the same residue standards as domestically produced food.

### **Example**

A manufacturer develops a new fungicide active ingredient intended for use on multiple food crops (e.g., grapes, tomatoes) as well as a non-food ornamental plant application.

1. Because the substance is intended to **prevent or mitigate a pest** (fungal pathogens), it falls within FIFRA's "pesticide" definition — **TSCA's new chemical review under §5 does not apply** to this use, per the §3(2)(B) exclusion, even though the manufacturer might separately need TSCA review if the same chemical were also used as, say, an industrial preservative unrelated to pest control.
2. The manufacturer submits a **FIFRA §3 registration application** including toxicology, environmental fate, and ecological effects data.
3. Because the intended uses include food crops, the manufacturer **simultaneously petitions EPA for a tolerance** under **FFDCA §408**, submitting residue trial data showing expected residue levels on grapes and tomatoes at harvest under labeled application rates.
4. EPA's **Office of Pesticide Programs** evaluates the tolerance petition under the FQPA's "reasonable certainty of no harm" standard, considering:
   - **Aggregate exposure** from the fungicide across all approved food uses plus any residential exposure pathways
   - **Cumulative risk** if the fungicide shares a common mechanism of toxicity with other registered fungicides
   - The **FQPA 10x children's safety factor**, applied unless reliable data justifies a different margin
5. EPA establishes the tolerance concurrently with finalizing the FIFRA registration for the food-use crops, while the ornamental (non-food) use may proceed under the FIFRA registration alone without implicating the FFDCA tolerance process, since no food residue concern arises for that particular use.
6. If the manufacturer later develops an unrelated, non-pesticidal industrial application for a structurally related chemical (e.g., as a fabric treatment with no pest-control function), **that** separate use would fall under TSCA's §5 new chemical review, since it does not meet FIFRA's pesticide-use definition.

### Comparative Table: Statutory Roles in Pesticide-Food Chemical Regulation

| Question | Governing Statute | Standard Applied | Administering Office |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| May the pesticide be sold/distributed at all? | FIFRA §3 | Unreasonable adverse effects (risk-benefit) | EPA Office of Pesticide Programs |
| What residue level is permitted on food? | FFDCA §408 | Reasonable certainty of no harm (FQPA) | EPA Office of Pesticide Programs |
| Does the chemical require TSCA review? | TSCA §5/§6 | N/A for pesticide uses — TSCA excludes pesticide uses under §3(2)(B) | EPA Office of Pollution Prevention and Toxics |
| Non-food, non-pesticide use of a related chemical | TSCA | Risk-only "unreasonable risk" standard | EPA Office of Pollution Prevention and Toxics |
