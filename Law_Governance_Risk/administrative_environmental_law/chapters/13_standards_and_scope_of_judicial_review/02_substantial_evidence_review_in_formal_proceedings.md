## Substantial Evidence Review in Formal Proceedings

### Overview

Substantial evidence review, codified at 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(E), is the standard of review applicable to factual findings made in **formal** agency proceedings — formal rulemaking under 5 U.S.C. §§ 556-557 and formal adjudication — as well as certain informal proceedings where a specific organic statute expressly mandates substantial evidence review. It is generally understood to be a somewhat more searching standard of factual review than arbitrary-and-capricious review, though the two standards converge substantially in practice, particularly as applied to factual components of informal rulemaking review.

### Statutory Basis

**5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(E)** directs a reviewing court to set aside agency findings "unsupported by substantial evidence" in a case subject to §§ 556-557 of the APA (formal rulemaking/adjudication) or otherwise reviewed on the record of an agency hearing provided by statute.

**Sections 556-557** govern formal adjudications and formal rulemakings — proceedings that must be conducted "on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing," triggering the APA's formal procedural requirements (presiding officer, evidentiary hearing, exclusive record, etc.).

### When Does Substantial Evidence Review Apply?

Substantial evidence review under § 706(2)(E) applies to:

1. **Formal adjudications** — required by statute to be determined "on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing" under APA §§ 556-557, triggering full trial-type procedures (e.g., many licensing revocations, certain benefits determinations, and specific environmental permit denials/revocations where the organic statute mandates formal hearing procedures).
2. **Formal rulemaking** — comparatively rare in modern practice, since the Supreme Court in *United States v. Florida East Coast Railway Co.*, 410 U.S. 224 (1973), construed statutory "on the record" triggering language narrowly, meaning formal (as opposed to informal, notice-and-comment) rulemaking is now infrequent — most agencies, including EPA, conduct rulemaking through informal notice-and-comment procedures under APA § 553, reviewed instead under the arbitrary-and-capricious standard.
3. **Statute-specific mandates** — some organic statutes independently require substantial evidence review even for proceedings that would not otherwise trigger APA formal-proceeding requirements (e.g., certain Clean Air Act provisions historically specified substantial evidence review for particular EPA actions, a legacy of pre-APA drafting conventions in some environmental statutes).

Because most environmental rulemaking (NAAQS-setting, NSPS, effluent limitations guidelines) proceeds through informal notice-and-comment rulemaking rather than formal on-the-record proceedings, arbitrary-and-capricious review under § 706(2)(A) — not substantial evidence review — is the dominant standard for the bulk of environmental regulatory challenges. Substantial evidence review is comparatively more significant in formal adjudicatory contexts, such as certain permit revocation hearings, enforcement adjudications before an agency's own administrative law judges, and specific licensing proceedings.

### The Substantial Evidence Standard Defined

**Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB**, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938), supplies the classic definition, later widely adopted: substantial evidence is "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This is:

- **More than a mere scintilla** of evidence.
- **Less than a preponderance** in the sense that a reviewing court need not conclude the agency's finding is more likely true than not by independently reweighing the evidence; the court asks only whether a reasonable factfinder *could* have reached the agency's conclusion on the record as a whole.
- **Whole-record review**: *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474 (1951), clarified that substantial evidence review requires the court to examine the *entire* record, including evidence that detracts from the agency's conclusion, not merely the evidence supporting it in isolation — a court cannot uphold a finding by looking only at the evidence favorable to the agency while ignoring significant contrary evidence in the record.

### Comparing Substantial Evidence and Arbitrary-and-Capricious Review

| Dimension | Substantial Evidence (§ 706(2)(E)) | Arbitrary and Capricious (§ 706(2)(A)) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Applies to | Formal adjudication/rulemaking; statute-specific formal-hearing mandates | Default standard for informal rulemaking and most other agency action |
| Focus | Whether factual findings are supported by the hearing record | Whether the agency's overall decisionmaking process was reasoned (facts, alternatives, explanation) |
| Record basis | Exclusive, closed hearing record generated through formal trial-type procedures | Broader administrative record, including comments, data, and materials considered during informal rulemaking |
| Practical rigor | Traditionally considered somewhat more searching for factual findings specifically | *State Farm*'s "hard look" doctrine has substantially narrowed the practical gap, particularly for factual/technical findings underlying informal rules |

**Practical convergence**: Although the two standards derive from different statutory text and historically different procedural contexts, courts and commentators have widely observed that in practice, the "hard look" version of arbitrary-and-capricious review (post-*State Farm*) applied to factual findings in informal rulemaking often functions similarly in rigor to substantial evidence review — both require the agency's factual conclusions to be reasonably supported by record evidence, considered as a whole, including evidence that cuts against the agency's position. [Inference: the degree of practical convergence between these standards is a matter of scholarly and judicial discussion rather than settled black-letter law, and some courts and commentators continue to treat substantial evidence review as measurably more searching in specific factual-sufficiency contexts.]

### Whole-Record Review in Detail

The *Universal Camera* whole-record principle requires courts to consider:

- Evidence supporting the agency's conclusion.
- Evidence detracting from or contradicting the agency's conclusion, including testimony the agency's factfinder found less credible, contrary expert opinions in the record, and any internal inconsistencies in the agency's supporting evidence.
- The reasoning of any subordinate factfinder (e.g., an administrative law judge) whose findings may differ from the ultimate agency decisionmaker's conclusions — where an ALJ's factual findings are reversed or modified by the agency head, courts give some weight to the ALJ's contrary assessment, particularly regarding witness credibility, though the agency head's ultimate factual determination remains the operative finding under review.

### Application in Environmental Law

Because most environmental *rulemaking* proceeds informally (and is thus reviewed under arbitrary-and-capricious standards), substantial evidence review's environmental-law footprint is concentrated in specific formal proceedings:

- **Formal enforcement adjudications**: Certain EPA and state environmental agency enforcement proceedings — particularly civil penalty assessments following an evidentiary hearing before an agency ALJ — are reviewed for substantial evidence support of the factual findings underlying liability and penalty determinations.
- **Permit revocation/suspension hearings conducted as formal adjudications**: Where a specific environmental statute or regulation requires formal, trial-type procedures for revoking or suspending a permit (as opposed to the more common informal permitting process), the resulting factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence.
- **Historical Clean Air Act provisions with statute-specific substantial evidence language**: Some pre-1990s environmental statutory provisions specify substantial evidence review for particular EPA actions as a matter of the specific organic statute's text, independent of whether the underlying proceeding would otherwise trigger APA formal-proceeding requirements — practitioners must check the specific statutory provision at issue rather than assuming arbitrary-and-capricious review applies by default. [Inference: the precise set of environmental statutory provisions retaining statute-specific substantial evidence review language, as opposed to general arbitrary-and-capricious review, requires checking the specific current text of the relevant provision, since this can vary and has been the subject of periodic statutory amendment.]
- **NRC licensing adjudications**: Nuclear Regulatory Commission licensing proceedings (relevant to environmental and safety review of nuclear facilities) are frequently conducted as formal, trial-type adjudications, with resulting factual findings reviewed for substantial evidence.

### Practical Example

A state environmental agency conducts a formal, trial-type administrative hearing (mandated by the relevant state environmental statute) to determine whether a facility violated its discharge permit, resulting in a civil penalty. The facility appeals, arguing the agency's factual finding that a specific discharge event occurred is not supported by the record.

1. The reviewing court examines the **entire hearing record**, not merely the agency's cited exhibits — including the facility's contrary monitoring data, testimony from the facility's expert disputing the agency's sampling methodology, and any credibility determinations the presiding ALJ made regarding the agency's inspector versus the facility's expert witness.
2. If the agency's finding rests on some credible testimony and documentary evidence (the inspector's contemporaneous sampling records, chain-of-custody documentation, and no significant procedural irregularities in evidence-gathering), a reviewing court would likely uphold the finding under substantial evidence review even if the facility's contrary evidence, viewed alone, might also have supported a different conclusion — the standard asks whether the agency's conclusion is one a reasonable factfinder *could* reach, not whether the court would have reached the same conclusion de novo.
3. If, however, the agency's finding rests entirely on speculative inference unsupported by any direct record evidence, or if the agency's reasoning simply fails to grapple with substantial, credible contrary evidence in the record (per *Universal Camera*'s whole-record principle), the finding would be vulnerable to reversal as unsupported by substantial evidence.

### Key Case Summary Table

| Case | Holding | Doctrinal Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB* (1938) | Defines substantial evidence as what a reasonable mind might accept as adequate | Foundational definition of the standard |
| *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB* (1951) | Requires whole-record review, including evidence detracting from the agency's conclusion | Establishes the whole-record principle |
| *United States v. Florida East Coast Railway Co.* (1973) | Narrowly construes statutory "on the record" triggers for formal rulemaking | Explains why formal rulemaking (and thus substantial evidence review of rules) is now rare |

### Practice Pointers

- Before assuming substantial evidence review applies, confirm the specific proceeding actually qualifies as formal adjudication/rulemaking under APA §§ 556-557, or that a specific organic statute independently mandates substantial evidence review — most modern environmental rulemaking is informal and reviewed instead under arbitrary-and-capricious standards.
- When challenging a formal adjudicatory finding, marshal all record evidence detracting from the agency's conclusion, not merely argue the agency's own cited evidence is weak — *Universal Camera*'s whole-record principle requires the court (and thus counsel's briefing) to grapple with the complete evidentiary picture.
- Pay particular attention to any ALJ credibility determinations that differ from the ultimate agency decisionmaker's findings, since courts give some independent weight to a subordinate factfinder's contrary credibility assessment even though the agency head's finding remains the operative decision under review.
- Recognize that, despite formal doctrinal distinctions, the practical rigor of substantial evidence review and modern "hard look" arbitrary-and-capricious review for factual findings has substantially converged — arguments framed around record support and reasoned engagement with contrary evidence are often effective under either standard.
