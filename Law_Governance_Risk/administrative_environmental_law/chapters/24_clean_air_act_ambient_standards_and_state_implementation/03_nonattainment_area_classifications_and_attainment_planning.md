## Nonattainment Area Classifications and Attainment Planning

### Overview

When an area fails to meet a National Ambient Air Quality Standard, the Clean Air Act's Part D nonattainment provisions impose a graduated, increasingly stringent set of planning and control obligations designed to bring the area back into compliance by a statutorily fixed deadline. The severity of these obligations scales with how far an area's air quality departs from the standard, using pollutant-specific classification systems (most elaborately developed for ozone) that link classification level to control technology requirements, offset ratios, and attainment deadlines. This structure creates strong incentives for states to act early and aggressively, since delay and worsening air quality trigger progressively harsher regulatory consequences.

### Statutory Basis

**Key Points**

- CAA Part D, 42 U.S.C. §§ 7501–7515, governs nonattainment area planning requirements, supplementing the general SIP obligations of § 110.
- Subpart 1 (§§ 7501–7509a) sets general nonattainment requirements applicable to all criteria pollutants.
- Subparts 2 through 5 impose pollutant-specific classification schemes and requirements for ozone, carbon monoxide, particulate matter (PM), and (historically) other pollutants, with **ozone (Subpart 2, §§ 7511–7511f)** representing the most elaborately tiered classification system.
- § 172 establishes general nonattainment SIP requirements applicable across pollutants: reasonably available control technology (RACT), reasonable further progress (RFP) demonstrations, attainment demonstrations, and contingency measures.
- § 173 governs permit requirements for new and modified major stationary sources in nonattainment areas, including the **emissions offset** requirement central to Nonattainment New Source Review (NNSR).

### Designation Process

**Key Points**

- Following promulgation or revision of a NAAQS, EPA designates every area of the country under § 107(d) as:
  - **Attainment**: meets the NAAQS based on monitored air quality data
  - **Nonattainment**: violates the NAAQS
  - **Unclassifiable**: insufficient data to make a determination (generally treated similarly to attainment for regulatory purposes pending further data)
  - **Unclassifiable/Attainment**: a hybrid category sometimes used where limited data suggests likely attainment
- Designations are typically based on air quality monitoring data over a specified averaging period (e.g., a three-year design value for ozone and PM$_{2.5}$), and states may petition EPA regarding boundary determinations, since nonattainment status is assigned to defined geographic areas (often metropolitan statistical areas or specific counties) rather than uniformly statewide.
- Designation triggers the clock for a state's nonattainment SIP submission deadline, generally set by statute or EPA regulation following the effective date of designation.

### Ozone Classification System (Subpart 2)

**Key Points**

The ozone program uses the most granular classification structure, tying classification level directly to the severity of the area's design value relative to the NAAQS:

| Classification | Approximate Design Value Threshold (illustrative, standard-dependent) | Attainment Deadline (years from designation) | Key Additional Requirements |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marginal | Just above the NAAQS | 3 years | Basic emissions inventory, NNSR at higher major source thresholds |
| Moderate | Moderately above the NAAQS | 6 years | RACT for existing major sources, reasonable further progress (RFP) plan |
| Serious | Substantially above the NAAQS | 9 years | Enhanced monitoring, lower major source thresholds, transportation control measures |
| Severe | Well above the NAAQS | 15–17 years | Further reduced major source thresholds, higher offset ratios |
| Extreme | Far above the NAAQS | 20 years | Most stringent controls; lowest major source thresholds (10 tons/year); highest offset ratios |

- A defining feature of the ozone classification system is the **"bump-up" mechanism**: if an area fails to attain the NAAQS by its classification-specific deadline, EPA generally reclassifies it to the next higher (more stringent) classification, triggering additional control requirements and a new, later deadline.
- As classification increases, the **major source threshold** (the emissions level triggering "major source" status for NNSR permitting purposes) decreases — meaning progressively smaller sources become subject to the most stringent nonattainment permitting requirements as an area's air quality problem is classified as more severe.

### Particulate Matter and Carbon Monoxide Nonattainment (Subparts 3–4)

**Key Points**

- **PM nonattainment** (Subpart 4) uses a less granular structure than ozone, generally distinguishing **Moderate** and **Serious** classifications, with corresponding attainment deadlines and control requirements (e.g., Best Available Control Measures (BACM) for Moderate areas, Best Available Control Technology-equivalent stringency for Serious areas).
- **CO nonattainment** (Subpart 3) similarly uses a simpler classification structure, historically significant but with many former CO nonattainment areas having achieved and maintained attainment status over time, in part due to vehicle fleet turnover and cleaner fuel/engine technology.
- Both programs share the general Part D architecture: RACT/RACM requirements, attainment demonstrations, reasonable further progress, and contingency measures, scaled to classification severity.

### Core Nonattainment SIP Elements

**Key Points**

Regardless of pollutant, nonattainment SIPs under § 172 generally must include:

1. **Attainment demonstration** — modeling showing the area will attain the NAAQS by the applicable deadline through the proposed control measures
2. **Reasonably Available Control Technology (RACT)** — control technology requirements for existing major stationary sources
3. **Reasonable Further Progress (RFP)** — a demonstration of incremental, measurable emission reductions leading to attainment, not merely a "back-loaded" plan reliant on late compliance
4. **Emissions inventory** — a comprehensive accounting of sources and emissions within the nonattainment area
5. **Contingency measures** — additional control measures automatically triggered if the area fails to make reasonable further progress or fails to attain by the deadline, without requiring a new SIP revision process
6. **Nonattainment New Source Review (NNSR) program** — permitting requirements for new and modified major sources

### Nonattainment New Source Review and Emissions Offsets

**Key Points**

- Under § 173, a new or modified major stationary source in a nonattainment area must, among other requirements:
  - Install controls meeting the **Lowest Achievable Emission Rate (LAER)** — a more stringent standard than PSD's Best Available Control Technology (BACT), reflecting the more urgent air quality need in nonattainment areas
  - Obtain **emissions offsets**: emission reductions from existing sources sufficient to more than compensate for the new source's emissions increase, ensuring a net air quality benefit despite new source additions
  - Demonstrate compliance with all other applicable federal and state emission limitations
- **Offset ratios** generally increase with classification severity (e.g., ozone offset ratios can range from approximately 1.1:1 in Marginal areas up to 1.5:1 or higher in Extreme areas), reflecting the greater emission reduction burden needed to accommodate growth in more severely polluted areas. [Unverified — specific offset ratios vary by pollutant, classification, and specific EPA regulations in effect; verify current applicable ratios.]
- This LAER-plus-offset framework contrasts with the PSD program's BACT-only requirement in attainment areas (see *National Ambient Air Quality Standards and the criteria pollutants*), reflecting the different regulatory purposes: PSD prevents degradation of already-acceptable air quality, while NNSR affirmatively drives improvement in already-degraded air quality while still permitting economic growth.

### Comparison: PSD vs. NNSR

| Feature | PSD (Attainment/Unclassifiable Areas) | NNSR (Nonattainment Areas) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Governing provisions | CAA Part C | CAA Part D |
| Control technology standard | Best Available Control Technology (BACT) | Lowest Achievable Emission Rate (LAER) — more stringent |
| Cost consideration in tech standard | Yes, BACT considers cost | No, LAER does not consider cost |
| Emissions offsets required | No | Yes, at ratios exceeding 1:1 |
| Purpose | Prevent degradation of acceptable air quality | Improve/attain currently substandard air quality |
| Major source threshold | Generally fixed statutory thresholds | Decreases as classification severity increases (ozone) |

### Reclassification, Extensions, and Attainment Determinations

**Key Points**

- If an area attains the NAAQS by its deadline and maintains that attainment, EPA may issue a **redesignation to attainment**, accompanied by an approved **maintenance plan** under § 175A demonstrating the area will maintain compliance for at least ten years following redesignation.
- If an area fails to attain by its deadline, EPA generally must reclassify it to a higher (more stringent) classification (the "bump-up" mechanism described above for ozone), though certain limited extension provisions exist under specific statutory criteria (e.g., demonstrating attainment was not reasonably achievable despite implementation of all required measures).
- **Clean Data Determinations**: EPA may determine that an area's monitored air quality data shows attainment of the NAAQS, which can suspend certain otherwise-applicable nonattainment planning obligations (e.g., attainment demonstration and RFP requirements) pending a formal redesignation, though this is distinct from formal redesignation itself and does not remove the area from nonattainment status outright.

### Practice Pointers

**Key Points**

- When advising a source seeking to construct or modify in a nonattainment area, confirm the area's specific classification and current major source threshold before assuming PSD-style review applies; NNSR's LAER and offset requirements are typically more burdensome and offer less cost-based flexibility than PSD's BACT standard.
- Monitor an area's design value trend relative to its attainment deadline, since a likely "bump-up" to a more stringent classification can be anticipated and factored into long-term facility planning, particularly given the significantly lower major source thresholds and higher offset ratios at higher classification levels.
- For clients considering redesignation to attainment, ensure the required maintenance plan and contingency provisions are robust, since redesignation is contingent on EPA's approval of an adequate ten-year maintenance demonstration, not merely on current monitoring data showing attainment.
- [Inference] Because specific numeric thresholds, deadlines, and offset ratios vary by pollutant, by which NAAQS iteration applies, and are subject to periodic revision alongside the five-year NAAQS review cycle, confirm current classification-specific requirements against EPA's current regulations and the specific NAAQS version applicable to the area and pollutant at issue.

### Conclusion

The nonattainment classification and attainment planning framework operationalizes the Clean Air Act's core structural insight that areas with worse air quality problems need proportionally more stringent regulatory tools — reflected in the graduated classification systems (most elaborately for ozone), progressively lower major source thresholds, higher offset ratios, and longer-but-firm attainment deadlines. The "bump-up" mechanism for missed deadlines creates a self-reinforcing incentive structure pushing states toward early, aggressive action, while the redesignation and maintenance plan process provides a durable off-ramp once an area demonstrates sustained compliance.
