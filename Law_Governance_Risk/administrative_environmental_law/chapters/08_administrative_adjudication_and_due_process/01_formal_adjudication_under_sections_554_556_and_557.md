## Formal Adjudication Under Sections 554, 556, and 557


### Overview

Formal adjudication is the trial-type administrative process the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) prescribes when an agency's organic statute requires that an adjudication be determined "on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing." The three interlocking provisions — 5 U.S.C. § 554 (adjudications generally), § 556 (hearing conduct, burden of proof, evidence), and § 557 (decisionmaking, initial and recommended decisions, ex parte restrictions) — together constitute the formal adjudication framework, structurally parallel to (and in some respects modeled on) formal rulemaking under §§ 556–557, but applied to individualized agency determinations rather than rules of general applicability.

### Triggering Formal Adjudication: The "On the Record" Requirement

Section 554(a) applies formal adjudication procedures "in every case of adjudication required by statute to be determined on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing." The critical interpretive question is whether a given organic statute's hearing requirement triggers formal (§§ 556–557) versus informal adjudication procedures.

- ***United States v. Florida East Coast Railway Co.***, 410 U.S. 224 (1973): held that a statute's mere reference to a "hearing" does not automatically trigger formal, on-the-record procedures; the statute must be read to require a determination "on the record" for §§ 556–557 to apply. This case is more frequently cited in the rulemaking context but its interpretive approach extends to adjudication as well.
- Courts examine statutory language, legislative history, and the nature of the proceeding to determine whether Congress intended formal, trial-type procedures or a more flexible informal process.
- ***Dominion Energy Brayton Point, LLC v. Johnson***, 443 F.3d 12 (1st Cir. 2006), and similar circuit decisions illustrate the continued judicial reluctance to infer formal adjudication requirements absent clear statutory "on the record" language. ### Section 554: Adjudications Generally

**Applicability and Exemptions (§ 554(a))**

Section 554 applies to formal adjudications except to the extent they involve:

1. A matter subject to a subsequent trial of the law and facts de novo in a court
2. The selection or tenure of an employee, except administrative law judges
3. Proceedings in which decisions rest solely on inspections, tests, or elections
4. The conduct of military or foreign affairs functions
5. Cases in which an agency is acting as an agent for a court
6. The certification of worker representatives

**Notice Requirements (§ 554(b))**

Persons entitled to notice of a hearing must be timely informed of:

1. The time, place, and nature of the hearing
2. The legal authority and jurisdiction under which the hearing is held
3. The matters of fact and law asserted

**Settlement and Informal Disposition (§ 554(c))**

The agency must give interested parties opportunity to submit and consider facts, arguments, offers of settlement, or proposals of adjustment, where time, the nature of the proceeding, and the public interest permit. This encourages settlement even within the formal adjudication track.

**Separation of Functions (§ 554(d))**

This is one of formal adjudication's most structurally significant protections: an employee or agent engaged in investigative or prosecuting functions for a case may not, in that or a factually related case, participate or advise in the decision, recommended decision, or agency review (with limited exceptions, e.g., for witnesses or parties, or in ratemaking/licensing initial-decision contexts under specific statutory carve-outs). This separation-of-functions requirement is designed to prevent the institutional combination of prosecutorial and adjudicative roles from compromising the fairness of the decision.

### Section 556: Hearings, Presiding Officers, Evidence, and Burden of Proof

**Presiding Officer (§ 556(b))**

Formal hearings must be presided over by one of the following, in descending order of typical use:

1. The agency itself
2. One or more members of the body that comprises the agency
3. One or more **administrative law judges (ALJs)** appointed under 5 U.S.C. § 3105

ALJs are subject to distinctive employment protections designed to insulate them from agency pressure: they are appointed through a competitive examination process, cannot be removed except for good cause established by the Merit Systems Protection Board after a hearing (5 U.S.C. § 7521), and their performance ratings and compensation are structurally separated from the agency's direct control to preserve decisional independence.

**Powers of Presiding Officers (§ 556(c))**

Presiding officers have authority to administer oaths, issue subpoenas authorized by law, rule on offers of proof and receive relevant evidence, take depositions, regulate the course of the hearing, hold settlement conferences, and make or recommend decisions.

**Burden of Proof and Evidence Standards (§ 556(d))**

- The proponent of a rule or order generally bears the **burden of proof**.
- A party is entitled to present oral or documentary evidence, submit rebuttal evidence, and conduct **cross-examination** as may be required for a full and true disclosure of the facts.
- Agencies may adopt procedures for submission of evidence in written form when a party will not be prejudiced.
- **Substantial evidence standard**: a sanction may not be imposed, and a rule or order may not be issued, except on consideration of the whole record and as supported by and in accordance with reliable, probative, and substantial evidence.

**Official Notice (§ 556(e))**

The transcript of testimony and exhibits, together with all papers filed in the proceeding, constitutes the exclusive record for decision. When an agency decision rests on official notice of a material fact not appearing in the evidence, a party must be given adequate opportunity to show the contrary.

### Section 557: Initial Decisions, Review, and Ex Parte Communications

**Initial and Recommended Decisions (§ 557(b))**

When the presiding officer (e.g., an ALJ) makes the initial decision, that decision becomes the agency's decision unless a party appeals to the agency or the agency reviews the decision on its own initiative — a structure often described as "appeal by exception." The agency retains authority, in reviewing, to exercise all the powers it would have had in making the initial decision, meaning agency review of an ALJ's decision is generally de novo as to both fact and law, not deferential.

**Contents of Decisions (§ 557(c))**

All decisions must include findings and conclusions, and the reasons or basis for them, on all material issues of fact, law, or discretion presented on the record, plus the appropriate rule, order, sanction, relief, or denial.

**Ex Parte Communications (§ 557(d))**

This provision imposes strict restrictions designed to preserve the integrity of the on-the-record decision:

- No interested person outside the agency may make, and no agency decisionmaker may knowingly entertain, an **ex parte communication relevant to the merits** of the proceeding.
- If such a communication occurs, it must be placed on the public record, and the agency may require the offending party to show cause why the claim or interest should not be dismissed, denied, disregarded, or otherwise adversely affected.
- This restriction applies from the time a proceeding is noticed for hearing (or, if earlier, from when the person responsible for the communication has knowledge that it will be noticed) and continues until the proceeding's final disposition.

### Judicial Review of Formal Adjudications

- **Standard of review**: substantial evidence, per APA § 706(2)(E) — "unsupported by substantial evidence in a case subject to sections 556 and 557 of this title or otherwise reviewed on the record of an agency hearing provided by statute."
- ***Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB***, 340 U.S. 474 (1951): established that substantial evidence review requires courts to consider the record as a whole, including evidence that detracts from the agency's conclusion, not merely evidence supporting it — a more searching standard than simply asking whether any evidence supports the decision.
- The substantial evidence standard is generally understood as somewhat more rigorous than the "arbitrary and capricious" standard applied to informal agency action under § 706(2)(A), reflecting the more developed evidentiary record formal adjudication procedures produce.

### Constitutional Due Process Overlay

Formal adjudication under §§ 554, 556, 557 operationalizes, but is analytically distinct from, constitutional procedural due process requirements under the Fifth Amendment (federal) or Fourteenth Amendment (state):

- ***Goldberg v. Kelly***, 397 U.S. 254 (1970): established that certain individualized deprivations (there, welfare benefits) require a hearing prior to termination, incorporating due process principles resembling formal adjudication's protections (notice, opportunity to be heard, right to confront adverse evidence).
- ***Mathews v. Eldridge***, 424 U.S. 319 (1976): established the now-canonical three-factor balancing test for determining what process is constitutionally due — (1) the private interest affected, (2) the risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used and the probable value of additional safeguards, and (3) the government's interest, including administrative burden.

$$Process\ Due \propto f(Private\ Interest,\ Risk\ of\ Error,\ Government\ Burden)$$

[Inference] The relationship between APA formal adjudication and constitutional due process is best understood as overlapping but non-coextensive: Congress can statutorily require formal APA procedures even where the Constitution might not independently demand a trial-type hearing, and conversely, constitutional due process can require certain protections (such as pre-deprivation hearings in specific contexts) independent of whether the APA's formal adjudication provisions are triggered by the particular statute at issue.

### Comparative Table: Formal vs. Informal Adjudication

| Dimension | Formal Adjudication (§§ 554, 556, 557) | Informal Adjudication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Triggering language | Statute requires determination "on the record after opportunity for agency hearing" | Statute requires a hearing without "on the record" language, or no hearing requirement at all |
| Presiding officer | Agency, agency members, or ALJ | Varies; no ALJ requirement |
| Evidentiary record | Exclusive record: transcript, exhibits, filings | No formal exclusive record requirement |
| Cross-examination | Available as required for full and true disclosure | Not guaranteed |
| Separation of functions | Mandatory under § 554(d) | Not required |

| Typical examples | Social Security disability hearings, some licensing revocations, certain NLRB proceedings, formal FERC ratemaking adjudications | Most immigration proceedings (though these have their own specialized procedures), many benefit determinations, informal enforcement actions |

### Illustrative Application in Administrative & Environmental Law Contexts

Formal adjudication under §§ 554/556/557 arises in environmental law principally in:

- **Permit revocation or modification proceedings** where the governing environmental statute (e.g., certain provisions under the Clean Water Act NPDES permit program, or Clean Air Act Title V operating permit contested case provisions) requires an on-the-record hearing
- **Civil penalty assessment proceedings** before agency ALJs, such as EPA administrative penalty actions where the underlying statute mandates formal hearing procedures
- **License or certification proceedings** for regulated environmental activities, where formal hearing rights are statutorily conferred

[Inference] Whether a specific environmental enforcement or permitting proceeding triggers full §§ 556–557 formal procedures, as opposed to an agency's own informal or quasi-formal hearing rules, depends entirely on the particular statute's language and judicial interpretation of that language under the *Florida East Coast Railway* framework; this should be verified statute-by-statute rather than assumed uniformly across environmental programs.

**Key Points**

- Formal adjudication is triggered only when an organic statute requires a determination "on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing" — the mere presence of a "hearing" requirement is insufficient without on-the-record language, per *Florida East Coast Railway*.
- Section 554(d)'s separation-of-functions requirement is a structural due-process-adjacent protection distinct from, but complementary to, § 557(d)'s ex parte communication restrictions — together they aim to prevent institutional bias and improper influence on the decisionmaker.
- ALJ decisions under § 557(b) become final agency decisions absent appeal or agency-initiated review, but when the agency does review, that review is generally de novo, not deferential to the ALJ.
- Judicial review of formal adjudications applies the substantial evidence standard (§ 706(2)(E)), which *Universal Camera* clarified requires whole-record review, not merely a search for supporting evidence.
- Formal APA adjudication and constitutional due process (*Mathews v. Eldridge* balancing) are related but distinct bodies of doctrine — one is a statutory interpretation question, the other a constitutional floor.

**Example**

Suppose an agency's organic statute authorizes revocation of a hazardous waste treatment facility's operating permit "after an agency hearing on the record" for repeated violations.

1. This "on the record" language triggers formal adjudication under § 554(a).
2. The agency issues § 554(b) notice specifying the alleged violations, the statutory and regulatory authority for revocation, and the hearing's time and place.
3. Before the hearing, the facility operator and agency counsel explore settlement under § 554(c); if unsuccessful, the case proceeds to hearing before an ALJ.
4. Under § 554(d), the agency investigators who conducted the compliance inspection cannot also advise the ALJ on the merits of the revocation decision.
5. At the § 556 hearing, the agency (as proponent of the revocation) bears the burden of proof, presents inspection evidence and witness testimony, and the facility operator cross-examines the agency's inspectors and presents rebuttal evidence.
6. The ALJ issues an initial decision under § 557(b); if neither party appeals and the agency does not review on its own initiative, this becomes the final agency decision.
7. If the facility operator seeks judicial review, the reviewing court applies the substantial evidence standard, examining the whole record per *Universal Camera* to determine whether the revocation decision is adequately supported.
