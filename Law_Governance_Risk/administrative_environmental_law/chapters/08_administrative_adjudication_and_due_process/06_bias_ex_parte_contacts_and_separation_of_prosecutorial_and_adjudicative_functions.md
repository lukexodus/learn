## Bias, Ex Parte Contacts, and Separation of Prosecutorial and Adjudicative Functions


### Overview

Administrative law imposes a cluster of related but doctrinally distinct protections designed to preserve the impartiality and integrity of agency decisionmaking: constitutional and statutory bias/disqualification standards for decisionmakers, restrictions on ex parte communications that would introduce off-the-record influence into a pending matter, and structural separation-of-functions requirements that prevent the same agency personnel from simultaneously investigating/prosecuting and adjudicating a case. These protections operate at different levels — some are constitutional due process floors, others are APA statutory requirements specific to formal adjudication, and others are agency-specific ethical or regulatory rules — and understanding their distinct sources and scope is essential to assessing any claim of unfair agency process.

### Bias and Disqualification of Decisionmakers

**Constitutional Due Process Bias Standard**

The Due Process Clause requires that adjudicators — including administrative decisionmakers — be free from bias that would deprive a party of a fair hearing before a neutral decisionmaker. The constitutional standard is comparatively demanding to invoke successfully:

- ***Withrow v. Larkin***, 421 U.S. 35 (1975): rejected the argument that combining investigative and adjudicative functions within the same agency body is inherently unconstitutional; the Court held that a "fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process," but that the combination of investigative and adjudicative functions does not, without more, constitute a due process violation — a presumption of honesty and integrity applies to administrative decisionmakers absent a specific showing of actual bias or an unacceptable risk of bias.
- ***Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.***, 556 U.S. 868 (2009): while decided in the judicial recusal context (elected state judge who received substantial campaign support from a party appearing before him), articulated a due process standard asking whether, under a realistic appraisal of psychological tendencies and human weakness, the interest at stake poses "such a risk of actual bias or prejudgment that the practice must be forbidden if the guarantee of due process is to be adequately implemented" — a framework with clear relevance to, though not squarely addressed to, administrative adjudicator bias claims.
- **Pecuniary interest**: a decisionmaker with a direct, substantial pecuniary interest in the outcome of a matter is constitutionally disqualified, per the foundational principle traced to ***Tumey v. Ohio***, 273 U.S. 510 (1927) (invalidating a mayor's compensation structure that gave him a direct financial stake in conviction outcomes).
- **Prejudgment**: a decisionmaker who has prejudged the specific facts at issue in a particular case (as opposed to holding a general policy view relevant to the subject matter) may be disqualified; the Supreme Court has distinguished mere prior exposure to or expression of views about general policy questions (not disqualifying) from prejudgment of specific adjudicative facts in the case at hand (potentially disqualifying).

**Statutory and Agency-Specific Disqualification Standards**

- Many agencies maintain internal regulations governing ALJ or decisionmaker recusal for financial conflicts, prior involvement in the matter, or personal relationships with parties, often modeled on but not identical to the judicial recusal standards in 28 U.S.C. § 455 (applicable to federal judges).
- The APA itself does not contain a freestanding, general disqualification provision comparable to § 455; disqualification standards for agency adjudicators are typically a blend of due process doctrine, agency-specific regulation, and (for ALJs specifically) professional norms reinforced by their § 7521 independence protections.

### Separation of Prosecutorial and Adjudicative Functions

**Statutory Basis: 5 U.S.C. § 554(d)**

For formal adjudications under §§ 554, 556, 557, Congress addressed the institutional-combination concern *Withrow* found constitutionally tolerable by imposing a specific statutory separation-of-functions requirement:

- An agency employee or agent engaged in the **performance of investigative or prosecuting functions** for an agency in a case may not, in that or a **factually related case**, participate or advise in the decision, recommended decision, or agency review under § 557, except as a witness or counsel in public proceedings.
- This applies notwithstanding *Withrow*'s holding that such combination is not constitutionally mandatory — § 554(d) is a statutory choice to impose a stricter separation than the Constitution independently requires, illustrating that Congress can and did provide more protection than the due process floor.
- **Exceptions**: § 554(d) contains carve-outs, including for the agency itself or a member of the body comprising the agency (reflecting that multi-member commissions often necessarily combine some policy and adjudicative functions at the commissioner level), and for certain ratemaking or licensing functions where specific statutory schemes permit different arrangements.

**Scope: "Factually Related Case"**

The separation-of-functions bar extends beyond the specific case at hand to any "factually related case," preventing an investigator/prosecutor from circumventing the rule merely by formally advising on a technically separate but substantively overlapping matter.

**Distinction from *Withrow*'s Constitutional Baseline**

[Inference] The relationship between § 554(d) and *Withrow* is best understood as Congress imposing a categorical, bright-line statutory rule for formal adjudication specifically, in an area where the Constitution itself (per *Withrow*) would only require a case-by-case showing of actual bias or unacceptable risk of bias from institutional combination — meaning agencies conducting informal adjudication (where § 554(d) does not apply) remain subject only to the more permissive *Withrow* constitutional floor absent an agency's own voluntary adoption of separation-of-functions practices.

### Ex Parte Communications: Section 557(d)

**Statutory Text and Structure**

5 U.S.C. § 557(d), applicable specifically to formal adjudications (and formal rulemaking) subject to §§ 556–557, provides:

1. No interested person outside the agency shall make or knowingly cause to be made to any agency member or decisionmaking employee an ex parte communication relevant to the merits of the proceeding.
2. No agency member or decisionmaking employee shall make or knowingly cause to be made to any interested person outside the agency an ex parte communication relevant to the merits.
3. A decisionmaker who receives, or who makes or knowingly causes to be made, a prohibited ex parte communication shall place on the public record: the communication, if written; a memorandum stating the substance, if oral; and any written response and a memorandum of the substance of any oral response.
4. Upon receipt of a prohibited ex parte communication knowingly made or caused to be made by a party, the agency, ALJ, or other presiding employee may, to the extent consistent with the interests of justice and the policy of the underlying statutes, require the party to show cause why its claim or interest in the proceeding should not be dismissed, denied, disregarded, or otherwise adversely affected on account of the violation.

**Temporal Scope**

The ex parte prohibition attaches from the time a proceeding is **noticed for hearing** (or earlier, if the person responsible for the communication has knowledge that a proceeding will be noticed), and continues until the proceeding's final disposition — meaning communications occurring before formal notice generally fall outside § 557(d)'s reach, a point of frequent practical significance in determining whether a particular contact is even covered.

**"Relevant to the Merits" Limitation**

Not all communications with agency personnel are prohibited — only those relevant to the merits of the proceeding. Purely procedural inquiries (e.g., questions about hearing scheduling, docket status) generally fall outside the prohibition, while substantive discussion of the facts, legal arguments, or likely outcome of the case falls within it.

**Distinction from Separation of Functions**

Ex parte restrictions and separation-of-functions requirements address related but distinct risks: separation of functions (§ 554(d)) is a structural, role-based rule preventing certain categories of agency personnel from participating in decisionmaking at all in a given case; ex parte restrictions (§ 557(d)) are a communications-channel rule preventing off-the-record influence on whoever the decisionmaker is, regardless of that person's institutional role, once the proceeding has reached the hearing-noticed stage.

### Ex Parte Rules Outside Formal Adjudication

- The APA's § 557(d) ex parte bar applies by its terms only to formal adjudication (and formal rulemaking). **Informal adjudication and informal (notice-and-comment) rulemaking are not subject to a general statutory ex parte prohibition under the APA itself.**
- However, courts have sometimes found that ex parte contacts in informal rulemaking can compromise the adequacy of the public comment record or raise due process concerns in adjudicatory-like informal proceedings, particularly where competing private parties are involved in a essentially adjudicatory dispute resolved through nominally informal procedures (sometimes termed "quasi-adjudicatory" informal proceedings in case law and commentary).
- [Inference] This gap — the absence of a general ex parte bar for informal adjudication — is a recurring point of administrative law scholarly discussion, since informal adjudication constitutes the large majority of agency individualized decisionmaking, meaning the strongest ex parte protections (§ 557(d)) apply only to the numerically smaller category of formal proceedings; agencies frequently address this gap voluntarily through their own internal ethics rules or procedural regulations rather than through APA-mandated requirements.

### The Federal Advisory Committee Act and Sunshine Act as Related Transparency Frameworks

While analytically distinct, two related statutes reinforce the broader transparency ecosystem within which bias and ex parte doctrine operates:

- **Government in the Sunshine Act** (5 U.S.C. § 552b): requires multi-member federal agencies to conduct their business in meetings open to public observation, subject to specific exemptions, addressing a related but distinct concern (collective deliberative transparency) from § 557(d)'s individual-communication focus.
- **Federal Advisory Committee Act (FACA)** (5 U.S.C. §§ 1001–1014, recodified from the original 5 U.S.C. App.): governs the formation and operation of federal advisory committees, requiring balanced membership and public meeting access, relevant where an agency's use of outside advisory bodies in a matter under adjudication could raise ex parte or bias-adjacent concerns if not properly structured.

### Judicial Remedies for Ex Parte and Separation-of-Functions Violations

**Ex Parte Violations**

Beyond § 557(d)'s own show-cause remedy, courts reviewing agency decisions tainted by ex parte contacts have, in some cases, vacated or remanded agency action where the violation was found to have had a material, unrebutted effect on the outcome, particularly in licensing and comparative-hearing contexts (e.g., broadcast licensing disputes, historically a fertile source of ex parte case law given the comparative, competitive nature of the proceedings).

**Separation-of-Functions Violations**

A § 554(d) violation, if established, can result in vacatur of the resulting decision and remand for a properly insulated decisionmaking process, analogous to the remedy structure for Appointments Clause defects discussed in ALJ appointment doctrine — the remedy focuses on curing the structural defect through a new, properly constituted decision process rather than simply reviewing the tainted decision on its existing merits.

### Application in Environmental and Administrative Enforcement Contexts

- **EPA enforcement adjudications**: where EPA pursues formal administrative penalty proceedings, § 554(d) requires that EPA enforcement staff who investigated the alleged violation be separated from those advising the presiding ALJ on the ultimate penalty decision.
- **Permit comparative proceedings**: historically, comparative licensing/permitting proceedings (where multiple applicants compete for a limited resource, such as certain natural resource extraction permits) have generated significant ex parte litigation, given the heightened incentive for competing applicants to seek off-the-record influence with agency decisionmakers.
- **Multi-member commission structures**: agencies structured as multi-member commissions (e.g., certain independent regulatory commissions with both enforcement and adjudicative authority) rely on *Withrow*'s tolerance of institutional combination at the commissioner level, while still being subject to § 554(d)'s stricter separation as to staff-level investigative/prosecutorial personnel advising on formal adjudications.

**Key Points**

- *Withrow v. Larkin* establishes that combining investigative and adjudicative functions within a single agency is not, by itself, unconstitutional — a presumption of administrative integrity applies absent a specific showing of actual bias or unacceptable risk of bias.
- Section 554(d) is a statutory choice to impose stricter separation than *Withrow*'s constitutional floor requires, but only for formal adjudication — informal adjudication remains governed by the more permissive constitutional standard absent agency-specific rules.
- Section 557(d)'s ex parte restrictions are communications-focused and temporally bounded (attaching once a matter is noticed for hearing), distinct from § 554(d)'s structural, role-based separation requirement — a party could violate one without violating the other.
- Both § 554(d) and § 557(d) apply only to formal adjudication under the APA; the large majority of agency informal adjudication lacks a comparable statutory bar on institutional combination or ex parte contact, leaving constitutional due process and agency-specific rules as the primary safeguards in that context.
- Remedies for both separation-of-functions and ex parte violations generally focus on curing the structural or procedural defect (vacatur and remand, or show-cause sanctions) rather than simply reviewing the tainted decision's substance on the existing record.

**Example**

Suppose an EPA regional office investigates a facility for Clean Water Act permit violations and initiates a formal administrative penalty proceeding under §§ 554/556/557.

1. The EPA inspector who conducted the site investigation and gathered evidence of the violation cannot, under § 554(d), also advise the presiding ALJ on the appropriate penalty amount in that case or in a factually related enforcement matter against the same facility.
2. Once the matter is noticed for hearing, if a facility representative attempts to contact the ALJ directly (bypassing formal proceedings) to argue the merits of the case informally, this constitutes a prohibited ex parte communication under § 557(d); the ALJ must place the communication on the public record and may require the facility to show cause why its position should not be adversely affected by the violation.
3. If the facility instead argues that the ALJ personally harbors a pecuniary interest in the outcome (for instance, an undisclosed financial relationship with a party) or has publicly prejudged the specific facts of this case (as opposed to having previously expressed general views about water quality policy), this raises a separate constitutional bias/disqualification claim, analytically distinct from both the § 554(d) and § 557(d) issues, and would be evaluated under due process bias doctrine rather than either APA-specific provision.
