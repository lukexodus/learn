## Strict, Joint and Several, and Retroactive Liability under CERCLA


### Overview

CERCLA §107(a) (42 U.S.C. §9607(a)) imposes liability on potentially responsible parties (PRPs) using a framework courts have consistently described as **strict, and (presumptively) joint and several, and retroactive**. This tripartite characterization is not itself written verbatim into the statutory text — Congress deleted an express joint-and-several liability standard from the bill before passage — but courts, led by the seminal *United States v. Chem-Dyne Corp.*, 572 F. Supp. 802 (S.D. Ohio 1983), read this standard into the statute by incorporating common-law principles from the Restatement (Second) of Torts. Understanding these three doctrines together is essential because they compound: strict liability removes the need to prove fault, joint and several liability removes the need to apportion harm precisely, and retroactivity removes any temporal limit tied to when the waste was disposed.

### Statutory Basis

- **CERCLA §107(a)** — 42 U.S.C. §9607(a): establishes liability for four classes of PRPs
- **CERCLA §101(32)** — 42 U.S.C. §9601(32): defines "liable" or "liability" by reference to the standard of liability under §311 of the Clean Water Act (which is strict liability)
- **CERCLA §113(f)** — 42 U.S.C. §9613(f): contribution actions among jointly and severally liable PRPs

### Strict Liability

#### **Key Points**

- CERCLA liability attaches **without regard to fault, intent, or negligence**. A PRP need not have violated any law, acted carelessly, or even known the substance was hazardous at the time of disposal.
- The plaintiff (EPA or a private cost-recovery plaintiff) need only establish the four statutory elements of §107(a):
  1. The site is a **"facility"** (defined broadly in §101(9))
  2. There has been a **"release" or threatened release"** of a **"hazardous substance"** (§101(14), §101(22))
  3. The release has caused the plaintiff to incur **"response costs"** consistent with the National Contingency Plan (NCP)
  4. The defendant falls into one of **four PRP categories** under §107(a)(1)–(4)

#### The Four PRP Categories (§107(a)(1)–(4))

1. **Current owners and operators** of the facility (§107(a)(1)) — liable regardless of whether they had any role in the contamination.
2. **Owners and operators at the time of disposal** (§107(a)(2)) — past owners/operators when hazardous substances were disposed of.
3. **Arrangers** (§107(a)(3)) — persons who arranged for disposal or treatment of hazardous substances they owned or possessed, at a facility owned/operated by another party.
4. **Transporters** (§107(a)(4)) — persons who accepted hazardous substances for transport to disposal or treatment facilities selected by the transporter.

#### **[Inference]** Why "Strict" Rather Than Absolute

Courts uniformly characterize CERCLA liability as strict rather than "absolute" because a narrow set of statutory affirmative defenses remain available under §107(b): (1) act of God, (2) act of war, (3) act or omission of an unrelated third party (subject to due-care and precaution requirements), or a combination of these. These defenses are notoriously difficult to satisfy in practice, but their existence is why courts avoid the term "absolute liability."

#### Arranger Liability Nuance: *Burlington Northern v. United States*

*Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599 (2009), clarified that arranger liability under §107(a)(3) requires that the defendant have **intended** to dispose of a hazardous substance — mere knowledge that some spillage or leakage would occur during a useful product sale (in that case, agricultural chemical sales that resulted in incidental spillage) is insufficient without an intent to arrange for disposal. This introduced a narrow fault-like element specifically into arranger liability, distinguishing it somewhat from the otherwise pure strict-liability character of the other PRP categories.

### Joint and Several Liability

#### **Key Points**

- Where the harm caused by multiple PRPs is **indivisible**, each PRP can be held liable for the **entire cost** of cleanup, not merely its proportional share.
- This standard derives from *Chem-Dyne*, which rejected a rule of automatic joint and several liability but held that courts should apply **traditional common-law rules of joint and several liability**, as reflected in the Restatement (Second) of Torts §433A.
- The plaintiff bears no burden to apportion harm among defendants; instead, **the burden shifts to defendants** to prove that the harm is **divisible** and can be reasonably apportioned.

#### The Divisibility Defense

A defendant can escape joint and several liability (avoiding liability for the full cleanup cost) by proving there is a **reasonable basis for apportionment** of harm — i.e., that its own contribution is separate and distinguishable from that of other PRPs.

**Burlington Northern's Apportionment Analysis**: The Supreme Court in *Burlington Northern* upheld a district court's apportionment of liability where the court used a formula based on:

- The percentage of the total contaminated site area owned by the defendant
- The proportion of time the defendant owned that land relative to the total period of contamination
- A further discount reflecting that only a portion of the contamination was attributable to the defendant's activistes

This case is significant because it confirmed that **apportionment based on reasonable, even if imprecise, evidence is permissible**, and it is not necessary for a defendant to achieve mathematical precision — courts may accept a "reasonable basis" supported by circumstantial and volumetric evidence.

#### **[Inference]** Practical Effect

Because divisibility is difficult to prove (most contamination involves commingled wastes with no clear volumetric or toxicity-based attribution), joint and several liability functions as the practical default in most multi-party Superfund sites. This is a major driver of CERCLA litigation strategy: a PRP found liable often faces exposure for the entire cleanup, then must pursue **contribution** from other PRPs under §113(f) to recover its equitable share.

#### Contribution Actions (§113(f))

- A PRP that has been sued under §107 or has settled with the government may seek **contribution** from other PRPs to recoup a proportionate share of what it paid.
- Courts allocate contribution shares using **equitable factors** — often informally called the "Gore factors" (drawn from a rejected amendment proposed by then-Senator Al Gore), which commonly include:
  - Ability of the parties to demonstrate that their contribution can be distinguished
  - Amount and toxicity/degree of hazard of the waste involved
  - Degree of involvement in generation, transport, treatment, or disposal
  - Degree of care exercised, considering the hazardous nature of the substances
  - Degree of cooperation with government authorities
- **Statute of limitations**: 3 years for contribution actions under §113(f), generally running from judgment or settlement.
- ***Cooper Industries v. Aviall Services***, 543 U.S. 157 (2004), held that a PRP may only bring a §113(f)(1) contribution claim if it has been sued under §106 or §107, or has resolved liability through an administrative or judicially approved settlement — a PRP that voluntarily cleans up without such a trigger cannot use §113(f)(1).
- ***United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.***, 551 U.S. 128 (2007), clarified that a PRP who incurs voluntary cleanup costs (not triggered by suit or settlement) may instead pursue a **direct cost-recovery action under §107(a)**, filling the gap left by *Aviall*.

### Retroactive Liability

#### **Key Points**

- CERCLA liability applies to disposal activities that occurred **before CERCLA's enactment in 1980**, even though such conduct was lawful at the time it occurred.
- This retroactive application has been **repeatedly upheld** against due process and takings challenges.

#### Leading Case: *United States v. Monsanto Co.*

*United States v. Monsanto Co.*, 858 F.2d 160 (4th Cir. 1988), is a leading circuit-level decision holding that CERCLA's retroactive imposition of liability does not violate substantive due process, reasoning that Congress has broad power to impose retroactive liability for economic regulation where there is a rational legislative purpose — here, ensuring that the parties responsible for hazardous waste, rather than the general taxpayer, bear cleanup costs.

#### Constitutional Framework

Courts applying rational basis review to retroactivity challenges generally ask whether the retroactive application is a **rational means of furthering a legitimate legislative purpose**. Courts have found this satisfied because:

1. Congress intended to address a nationwide backlog of contaminated sites resulting from historical disposal practices that predated CERCLA.
2. Making the law retroactive was necessary to accomplish this purpose — a purely prospective statute would not reach existing contamination.
3. The financial burden is placed on those who profited from or caused the waste disposal, rather than on the public through general tax revenue (the "polluter pays" principle).

#### **[Inference]** Circuit Consensus

Every circuit that has addressed the question has upheld CERCLA's retroactivity, making this one of the most settled issues in CERCLA jurisprudence despite the doctrinal tension with typical presumptions against retroactive legislation (compare *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), which articulates the general federal presumption against retroactivity for statutes silent on the question — CERCLA's retroactive reach is treated as an established exception given its remedial purpose and extensive judicial history).

### Interaction of the Three Doctrines: Illustrative Scenario

#### **Example**

A multi-party industrial site received hazardous waste from 1965 to 1978 (pre-CERCLA) from Company A (a past owner/operator) and Company B (an arranger who sent drums of solvent for disposal). The site is now owned by Company C, which purchased it in 2015 with no involvement in the original contamination.

- **Retroactivity**: Companies A and B remain liable for disposal that occurred entirely before CERCLA's 1980 enactment — the pre-CERCLA timing of the conduct is not a defense.
- **Strict liability**: Neither A nor B needs to have violated any law in effect at the time; the mere act of disposal/arranging for disposal of a hazardous substance triggers liability.
- **Current owner liability**: Company C is liable under §107(a)(1) as the **current owner**, even though it had no role in causing the contamination and purchased the property decades later (absent a valid defense such as the **bona fide prospective purchaser** defense under §101(40), added by the 2002 Brownfields Amendments).
- **Joint and several liability**: If the contamination from A's and B's waste has commingled such that individual contributions cannot be distinguished, EPA may sue any one of A, B, or C for the **full cost** of cleanup. Company C, despite having caused none of the contamination, could bear the entire initial cost burden and would need to pursue contribution from A and B under §113(f).
- **Divisibility defense attempt**: If A can show through historical records that its waste was deposited only in a specific, distinct area of the site with no commingling, it might succeed in limiting its liability to that portion under the *Burlington Northern* apportionment approach.

### Comparative Table: The Three Doctrines

| Doctrine | Core Rule | Key Case | Defense/Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strict liability | No fault/negligence required | *Chem-Dyne* (fault standard); *Burlington Northern* (arranger intent) | §107(b) defenses (act of God, act of war, third-party act with due care) |
| Joint and several liability | Full liability for indivisible harm | *Chem-Dyne*; *Burlington Northern* (apportionment upheld) | Divisibility defense (burden on defendant) |
| Retroactive liability | Applies to pre-1980 conduct | *Monsanto* | None recognized; rational basis review satisfied |
