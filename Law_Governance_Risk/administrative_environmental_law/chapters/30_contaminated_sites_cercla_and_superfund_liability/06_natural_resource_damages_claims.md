## Natural Resource Damages Claims

### Overview

Natural Resource Damages (NRD) liability is a distinct category of CERCLA recovery separate from response cost recovery, addressing compensation for **injury to, destruction of, or loss of natural resources** themselves — such as fisheries, wetlands, groundwater, and wildlife habitat — rather than the cost of remediating contamination. NRD claims are brought by designated government trustees on behalf of the public, and the recovered funds must be used to restore, replace, or acquire equivalent natural resources rather than being treated as general compensatory damages. This makes NRD a fundamentally different remedy from the cleanup-cost recovery mechanisms under §107(a) and §113(f), even though both arise from the same underlying release of hazardous substances.

### Statutory and Regulatory Basis

- **CERCLA §107(a)(4)(C)** — 42 U.S.C. §9607(a)(4)(C): establishes PRP liability for damages for injury to, destruction of, or loss of natural resources
- **CERCLA §107(f)** — 42 U.S.C. §9607(f): designates trustees and establishes rebuttable presumption for trustee assessments
- **CERCLA §111(i)** — 42 U.S.C. §9611(i): use restrictions on recovered NRD funds
- **NRD Assessment Regulations** — 43 C.F.R. Part 11 (Department of the Interior) and 15 C.F.R. Part 990 (NOAA, for coastal/marine resources)

---

### **Key Points**

- NRD liability attaches to the same PRP categories as response cost liability under §107(a)(1)–(4), but is assessed separately under §107(a)(4)(C).
- Only designated **federal, state, and tribal trustees** — not private parties or ordinary citizens — may bring NRD claims. Federal trustees are designated by the President (commonly agencies such as the Department of the Interior, NOAA, and the Department of Agriculture, depending on resource type); states designate their own trustees (often a state environmental or natural resources agency); tribes may serve as trustees for tribal resources.
- Recovered NRD funds are subject to a statutory **use restriction** under §111(i) — they must be used **only** to restore, replace, or acquire the equivalent of the injured natural resources, and may not be diverted to general government revenue or unrelated purposes.
- NRD claims are generally **not barred** by a PRP's completion of remedial action addressing the same release — cleanup of contamination and restoration of injured natural resources are treated as distinct obligations, meaning a PRP that has fully remediated a site for response-cost purposes can still face separate NRD liability for the resource injury that occurred before or during the response.

---

### Definition of "Natural Resources"

Under CERCLA §101(16), "natural resources" broadly includes land, fish, wildlife, biota, air, water, groundwater, drinking water supplies, and other such resources belonging to, managed by, held in trust by, appertaining to, or otherwise controlled by the United States, a state, a local government, a tribe, or a foreign government. Key characteristics:

- Resources must generally be held in some form of **public trust** or governmental control — purely private property interests in a resource (e.g., a privately owned pond with no public trust dimension) typically fall outside NRD's scope, though the line can be fact-dependent.
- The definition covers both **use values** (e.g., commercial fishing revenue, recreational access) and **non-use/existence values** (e.g., the public's interest in the mere existence of a healthy ecosystem, independent of direct use).

### The Assessment Process

#### **1. Pre-Assessment Screening**

Trustees conduct an initial screening to determine whether a detailed NRD assessment is warranted, considering factors such as the nature and extent of contamination, likely resource injury, and feasibility of restoration.

#### **2. Injury Determination**

Trustees establish that natural resources have been injured, destroyed, or lost as a result of a release of hazardous substances, distinguishing this injury from any injury attributable to unrelated causes.

#### **3. Quantification of Damages**

Damages are quantified using recognized valuation methodologies, which commonly include:

- **Restoration-based (or "restoration cost") approach**: measuring damages as the cost of restoring the injured resource to baseline (pre-injury) condition, or providing equivalent replacement resources — this is the predominant modern approach under both DOI and NOAA regulations.
- **Resource equivalency analysis (REA) / habitat equivalency analysis (HEA)**: scaling the amount of replacement/restoration project needed to make the public whole for both the injury and the interim lost use during the period between injury and full recovery.
- **Contingent valuation and other use-value methodologies**: economic techniques for estimating public willingness-to-pay for non-market resource values, more commonly associated with older assessment approaches and higher-profile cases (e.g., large marine spill litigation) than routine Superfund NRD claims.

#### **4. Restoration Planning**

Trustees develop a **Restoration Plan** identifying specific projects to restore, rehabilitate, replace, or acquire equivalent resources, often subject to public comment.

#### **5. Settlement or Litigation**

Most NRD claims are resolved through **settlement** (often incorporated into a broader CERCLA consent decree alongside response-cost provisions), though litigated NRD claims occur, particularly in complex or high-value cases.

---

### The Rebuttable Presumption (§107(f)(2)(C))

#### **Key Points**

- Assessments of damages performed by a federal or state trustee **in accordance with the applicable NRD assessment regulations** (43 C.F.R. Part 11 or 15 C.F.R. Part 990) are afforded a **rebuttable presumption** of correctness in any judicial or administrative proceeding.
- This shifts the practical burden onto a defendant PRP to demonstrate that the trustee's assessment methodology or resulting damage figure is flawed, arbitrary, or inconsistent with the regulations — a significant procedural advantage for trustees who follow the prescribed assessment protocols.
- PRPs frequently challenge NRD assessments on grounds such as: failure to establish adequate baseline conditions, methodological flaws in equivalency analysis, or failure to account for natural recovery processes.

---

### Interim Lost Use and Restoration Timeframes

#### **[Inference]** Why Interim Losses Matter

Because ecological restoration and natural recovery take time, trustees typically calculate damages to cover not only the ultimate restoration cost but also the **value lost to the public during the interim period** between the injury and full recovery of the resource. This interim-loss component is a standard feature of modern HEA/REA methodologies and is generally treated as well-established assessment practice, though the specific scaling calculations in any given case can be genuinely contested by PRPs.

---

### **Example**

A chemical manufacturing facility releases contaminants into an adjacent river over several decades, killing fish populations and degrading riparian wetland habitat used by migratory waterfowl.

1. The **state environmental agency** and the **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service** (as federal trustee) jointly conduct a **pre-assessment screening**, concluding that significant fishery and wetland injury likely occurred.
2. Trustees perform an **injury determination**, using historical fish population data, sediment sampling, and habitat surveys to establish causation between the release and observed ecological decline.
3. Using **habitat equivalency analysis**, trustees calculate that restoring an equivalent acreage of wetland habitat elsewhere in the watershed, scaled to compensate for both the permanent injury and decades of interim lost ecological services, would make the public whole.
4. Trustees issue a **Restoration Plan** proposing wetland creation and fish passage improvements at a nearby degraded tributary, subject to public comment.
5. The facility's PRPs, having separately entered a consent decree for response-cost cleanup of the contaminated soil and groundwater, now negotiate a **separate NRD settlement** — often incorporated into the same or a companion consent decree — funding the restoration projects identified in the plan.
6. The **rebuttable presumption** applies to the trustees' HEA-based assessment because it followed DOI's Part 11 regulations, meaning the PRPs bear the burden of showing the assessment was flawed if they wish to contest the damage figure in litigation rather than settling.

### Distinguishing NRD from Response Cost Recovery

| Feature | Response Cost Recovery (§107(a)(4)(A)/(B)) | Natural Resource Damages (§107(a)(4)(C)) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who may claim | Government or private party incurring costs | Only designated federal, state, or tribal trustees |
| What is compensated | Cost of investigating and cleaning up contamination | Injury to, destruction of, or loss of natural resources |
| Use of recovered funds | Reimburses actual cleanup expenditures | Restricted to restoration, replacement, or acquisition of equivalent resources (§111(i)) |
| Assessment presumption | No comparable rebuttable presumption mechanism | Rebuttable presumption for regulation-compliant trustee assessments |
| Relationship to remedial action | Directly tied to NCP-consistent cleanup costs | Independent — can proceed even after remediation is complete |
