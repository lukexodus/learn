## Settlement Tools: Consent Decrees and De Minimis Settlements

### Overview

CERCLA strongly favors settlement over prolonged litigation, and Congress built an extensive settlement toolkit into the statute — primarily through §122 — to encourage PRPs to resolve liability efficiently while preserving EPA's ability to secure prompt, adequately funded cleanups. **Consent decrees** are the primary vehicle for judicially enforceable settlements involving significant response action or cost recovery, while **de minimis settlements** provide an expedited, simplified mechanism for resolving the liability of parties whose contribution to a site is minor. Both tools are structured to provide settling parties with **contribution protection**, which is often the single most valuable incentive driving early settlement in multi-party Superfund litigation.

### Statutory Basis

- **CERCLA §122** — 42 U.S.C. §9622: settlement authority generally
- **CERCLA §122(g)** — 42 U.S.C. §9622(g): de minimis settlements
- **CERCLA §122(h)** — 42 U.S.C. §9622(h): cost recovery settlements (administrative)
- **CERCLA §113(f)(2)** — 42 U.S.C. §9613(f)(2): contribution protection for settling parties
- **CERCLA §113(f)(3)(B)** — 42 U.S.C. §9613(f)(3)(B): contribution rights for settling parties against non-settlors

### Consent Decrees

#### **Key Points**

- A **consent decree** is a judicially entered and enforceable settlement agreement, typically resolving a PRP's liability for performing or funding remedial action (RD/RA), reimbursing EPA's past response costs, or both.
- Consent decrees are lodged with the federal district court and subject to a **public comment period** (typically 30 days) before final entry, consistent with CERCLA's and DOJ's general settlement transparency practices.
- Because consent decrees require judicial approval, courts review them under a **"fair, reasonable, and consistent with CERCLA's objectives"** standard, though judicial review is generally deferential to the negotiated terms reached by EPA and PRPs.
- A consent decree typically includes:
  - A description of the remedial work to be performed (referencing the Record of Decision)
  - Reimbursement of EPA's past and future oversight costs
  - **Stipulated penalties** for noncompliance with decree obligations
  - **Contribution protection** under §113(f)(2) for matters addressed in the settlement
  - **Reopener provisions** allowing EPA to pursue additional relief if new information reveals the remedy is not protective or previously unknown contamination is discovered
  - Force majeure and dispute resolution provisions

#### Types of Consent Decrees

1. **RD/RA Consent Decrees** — PRPs agree to design and construct the remedy selected in the ROD, typically the most resource-intensive settlement category.
2. **Cost Recovery Consent Decrees** — PRPs agree to reimburse EPA for past response costs where EPA (rather than the PRPs) performed the cleanup.
3. **Cash-Out Settlements** — a PRP pays a lump sum to resolve its liability without performing work directly, often used for parties with limited involvement who prefer certainty over performing remedial work themselves.

#### Contribution Protection Mechanics

- A PRP that enters a consent decree resolving its liability to the United States or a state receives **protection from contribution claims** by other PRPs regarding matters addressed in the settlement (§113(f)(2)).
- This protection is a powerful settlement incentive: settling early **caps** a PRP's exposure at the negotiated amount for the covered matters, insulating it from later claims by non-settling PRPs who might otherwise seek a larger contribution share.
- Correspondingly, the settling PRP itself gains the right to pursue **contribution from non-settlors** under §113(f)(3)(B) for amounts paid in excess of its equitable share.

#### **[Inference]** The "Race to Settle" Dynamic

Because contribution protection operates on a first-mover advantage basis — the earliest, most cooperative PRPs typically negotiate more favorable terms and lock in protection before allocation disputes intensify — multi-party Superfund litigation often exhibits pressure for early PRP settlement, with holdout parties bearing increased relative exposure as the pool of contribution targets shrinks. The precise dynamics vary considerably by site and PRP group composition.

### De Minimis Settlements (§122(g))

#### **Key Points**

- Designed for PRPs whose contribution to site contamination is both **minimal in amount and minimal in toxic effect**, allowing EPA to resolve their liability quickly and administratively rather than drawing them into protracted, resource-intensive multi-party litigation.
- Congress's stated goal was to allow EPA to **"cash out"** minor contributors early, reducing transaction costs and administrative burden for parties who contributed relatively little waste, while preserving resources for pursuing major PRPs.
- EPA generally exercises considerable discretion in identifying appropriate de minimis candidates and settlement terms.

#### Eligibility Criteria

A PRP is generally eligible for de minimis treatment where EPA determines both:

1. **Minimal volume/toxicity** — the party's contribution of hazardous substances to the site is minimal in comparison to other hazardous substances at the facility, in terms of both **amount** and **toxic or other hazardous effects**; and
2. **No aggravating factors** — there is no reason to believe the wastes contributed pose unusual risks (e.g., disproportionate toxicity relative to volume) that would make de minimis treatment inappropriate.

#### **De Micromis** Exemption

Distinct from, but related to, de minimis settlements, the 2002 Brownfields Amendments added a **de micromis exemption** (§107(o)) for parties whose contribution is exceptionally small — generally very small quantities of municipal solid waste or hazardous substances — providing an even more streamlined liability exemption (rather than merely an expedited settlement) for the smallest contributors, subject to certain conditions and exceptions (e.g., where the party's conduct was found to have contributed significantly to costs of response).

#### Premium Payment and Streamlined Terms

- De minimis settlements typically require the settling party to pay a **premium** above its strict pro-rata share of costs, in exchange for the certainty, finality, and administrative simplicity of an early cash-out settlement.
- Settlement terms are generally **non-negotiable "model" agreements** applied uniformly across similarly situated de minimis parties at a given site, reducing transaction costs for both EPA and the settling parties.
- De minimis settlements are typically achieved through **administrative settlement agreements** (rather than judicial consent decrees) under §122(g), though EPA retains discretion to use a consent decree where circumstances warrant.

#### Contribution Protection for De Minimis Settlors

- Like consent decree settlors, PRPs entering de minimis settlements receive **contribution protection** under §113(f)(2), shielding them from later claims by other PRPs regarding the settled matters — a critical incentive given that de minimis parties, by definition, have limited resources or interest in prolonged litigation.
- De minimis settlements also typically include a **reopener** limited to circumstances such as fraud, material misrepresentation, or (in some agreements) a significant unforeseen increase in the volume/toxicity of the settling party's actual contribution.

### **Example**

At a multi-party Superfund site, EPA identifies 40 PRPs, ranging from a large chemical manufacturer responsible for roughly 60% of the waste volume and toxicity, to dozens of small businesses (e.g., local dry cleaners, auto repair shops) that each contributed trace quantities of hazardous substances through routine, lawful disposal practices decades earlier.

1. EPA negotiates a comprehensive **RD/RA consent decree** with the major chemical manufacturer and several other significant contributors, requiring them to design and construct the selected remedy and reimburse EPA's past oversight costs. This decree is lodged in federal court, subject to public comment, and includes contribution protection and a reopener for newly discovered contamination.
2. For the numerous small-volume contributors, EPA instead offers **de minimis settlements** under §122(g), using a standardized model agreement requiring each small contributor to pay a modest premium-inclusive lump sum reflecting its estimated (minor) share of costs, without requiring them to participate in the complex allocation litigation involving the major PRPs.
3. A handful of contributors whose waste contribution was so minimal it falls below even the de minimis threshold (e.g., a small quantity of ordinary municipal solid waste generator) may instead qualify for the **de micromis exemption** under §107(o), avoiding liability altogether rather than needing even an expedited settlement.
4. All settling parties — whether through the consent decree or the de minimis agreements — receive **contribution protection**, meaning non-settling PRPs (if any remain) cannot later sue them to recover a larger contribution share for the settled matters.

### Comparative Table: Consent Decrees vs. De Minimis Settlements

| Feature | Consent Decree | De Minimis Settlement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical parties | Major PRPs performing significant remedial work | Minor contributors (small volume/toxicity) |
| Statutory basis | General §122 authority; judicial approval | §122(g) |
| Approval mechanism | Judicial (lodged in federal court, public comment) | Typically administrative |
| Negotiation posture | Individually negotiated, often extensive | Standardized "model" agreement, largely non-negotiable |
| Financial structure | Performance of RD/RA work and/or cost reimbursement | Lump-sum cash-out, often including a premium |
| Contribution protection | Yes (§113(f)(2)) | Yes (§113(f)(2)) |
| Typical reopener scope | Broad (new contamination, remedy failure) | Narrow (fraud, misrepresentation, significant volume/toxicity understatement) |
