## Cost Recovery and Contribution Actions Among Responsible Parties


### Overview

CERCLA provides two distinct, though related, private causes of action for recovering the costs of hazardous substance cleanup: **cost recovery** under §107(a) and **contribution** under §113(f). These provisions serve different functions and have distinct procedural requirements, statutes of limitations, and available defenses. Confusing the two — or pleading the wrong one — has repeatedly proven fatal to CERCLA claims, making the §107/§113 distinction one of the most litigated procedural issues in Superfund practice.

### Statutory Basis

- **CERCLA §107(a)** — 42 U.S.C. §9607(a): cost recovery for response costs incurred
- **CERCLA §113(f)** — 42 U.S.C. §9613(f): contribution among liable parties
- **CERCLA §113(g)** — 42 U.S.C. §9613(g): statutes of limitations for both action types
- **CERCLA §122(g)/(h)** — 42 U.S.C. §9622(g)/(h): settlement mechanisms triggering contribution rights and protection

---

### Cost Recovery Under §107(a)

#### **Key Points**

- A party that has **itself incurred response costs** consistent with the National Contingency Plan (NCP) may sue any PRP under §107(a) to recover those costs.
- Available to: (1) the **United States** (or a state) recovering costs of government-led cleanup, and (2) **private parties** — including PRPs themselves — who have voluntarily incurred their own cleanup costs **without** having been sued under §106/§107 or having entered a qualifying settlement.
- Because §107(a) liability is **joint and several** for indivisible harm, a plaintiff may recover its **entire** cost outlay from any single PRP defendant, leaving that defendant to seek contribution from other PRPs.
- The recoverable cost standard requires costs be **"consistent with the national contingency plan"** — a plaintiff bears the burden of demonstrating substantial compliance with NCP procedural requirements (e.g., public comment for remedial actions, proper documentation).

#### Who May Bring a §107 Claim: *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*

*United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128 (2007), resolved a critical gap left by *Cooper Industries v. Aviall Services* (below):

- A PRP that **voluntarily** incurs cleanup costs — meaning without having been sued under §106/§107 or having entered a settlement resolving its liability — may bring a **direct cost-recovery action under §107(a)** against other PRPs.
- This is true even though the plaintiff is itself a PRP; §107(a)'s text does not exclude PRPs from suing other PRPs for recovery of the plaintiff's own incurred costs.
- The Court reasoned that §107(a)(4)(B) allows "any other person" (including a PRP) who incurs "necessary costs of response" to recover, distinct from a contribution claim which seeks reallocation of costs already imposed via judgment or settlement.

#### **[Inference]** Practical Significance

*Atlantic Research* effectively created a two-track system: a PRP that acts first and cleans up voluntarily gets access to the more favorable §107(a) joint-and-several framework as a plaintiff, while a PRP that waits to be sued (or settles) is relegated to the more limited §113(f) contribution framework as a plaintiff. This has been described as creating incentives for PRPs to undertake voluntary cleanup rather than await enforcement, though the precise strategic calculus depends heavily on case-specific factors including the availability of divisibility defenses against the PRP itself.

### Contribution Under §113(f)

#### **Key Points**

- Available to a person who **has been sued** under §106 or §107(a), or who has **resolved its liability** to the United States or a state through an administrative or judicially approved settlement.
- Contribution actions seek to **reallocate** costs already paid or judgment already entered, apportioning the burden among jointly and severally liable PRPs according to **equitable factors**.
- Courts commonly apply the so-called **"Gore factors"** (drawn from a proposed but rejected 1980 Senate floor amendment by then-Senator Al Gore) as a non-exhaustive guide, including:
  - Ability to distinguish each party's contribution to the contamination
  - Amount and toxicity of hazardous waste involved
  - Degree of involvement in generation, transport, treatment, or disposal
  - Degree of care exercised, given the hazardous nature of the substances
  - Degree of cooperation with government agencies in preventing harm

#### Trigger Requirement: *Cooper Industries v. Aviall Services*

*Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157 (2004), held:

- A party may bring a §113(f)(1) contribution claim **only if** it has been sued under §106 or §107(a), or has resolved its liability through a qualifying settlement.
- A PRP that has **not** been sued and has **not** settled — but simply undertook voluntary cleanup on its own initiative — **cannot** use §113(f)(1) as its vehicle. (This gap was later filled by *Atlantic Research*'s recognition of a direct §107(a) action for such voluntary actors.)

#### Settlement-Triggered Contribution: §113(f)(3)(B)

- A party that has resolved its liability to the United States or a state in an **administrative or judicially approved settlement** may seek contribution from non-settling PRPs under §113(f)(3)(B), separate from the "sued" trigger of §113(f)(1).
- This is the mechanism by which settling PRPs recover a proportionate share from PRPs who did not participate in the settlement.

#### Contribution Protection (§113(f)(2))

- A person who has **resolved liability** to the United States or a state in an administrative or judicially approved settlement is **protected from contribution claims** by other PRPs regarding matters addressed in that settlement.
- This protection is a central incentive EPA uses to encourage early settlement — settling PRPs gain certainty against being dragged back into contribution litigation by non-settlors, while non-settlors bear a greater relative share of remaining costs (often adjusted downward only by the settling parties' equitable share, not dollar-for-dollar, depending on the jurisdiction's approach to the settlement credit).

### Comparative Table: §107(a) Cost Recovery vs. §113(f) Contribution

| Feature | §107(a) Cost Recovery | §113(f) Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who may sue | Government; private party (including a PRP) who **voluntarily** incurred costs | PRP who has been **sued** under §106/§107, or has **settled** liability |
| Liability standard applied | Joint and several (for indivisible harm) | Equitable allocation among liable parties |
| Governing case | *Atlantic Research* | *Cooper Industries v. Aviall* |
| Statute of limitations | 3 years (removal actions) / 6 years (remedial actions) — §113(g)(2) | 3 years from judgment or settlement — §113(g)(3) |
| Recoverable amount | Full response costs (subject to defendant's divisibility defense) | Proportionate equitable share only |
| Settlement effect | N/A directly, though settlement may resolve liability and shift future claims to §113(f) posture | Triggers contribution protection under §113(f)(2) for settling party |

### **Example**

Company A, a past owner/operator, is named in an EPA unilateral administrative order (UAO) under §106 to perform remedial action at a contaminated site also linked to Company B (an arranger) and Company C (a transporter).

- **Scenario 1 — Company A complies with the UAO and performs the cleanup.** Because Company A was compelled to act via a §106 order, it may seek **contribution** from B and C under §113(f)(1) (the "sued" trigger extends to parties subject to a §106 order, which is treated as equivalent to being sued for these purposes), allocating costs according to equitable factors — perhaps reflecting that B contributed the more toxic waste stream.
- **Scenario 2 — Company A voluntarily investigates and remediates the site before EPA ever issues an order or files suit.** Under *Atlantic Research*, Company A may instead bring a **direct §107(a) cost-recovery action** against B and C, potentially recovering its full costs from either defendant under joint-and-several principles, subject to B or C's ability to raise a divisibility defense.
- **Scenario 3 — Company B settles with EPA via a consent decree covering its liability for the site.** Company B gains **contribution protection** under §113(f)(2) against claims by A or C regarding matters resolved in the settlement, and may itself pursue **contribution** from A and C under §113(f)(3)(B) for amounts paid under the settlement exceeding B's equitable share.

### Statute of Limitations Nuances (§113(g))

| Action Type | Limitations Period | Trigger Date |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost recovery — removal action | 3 years | Completion of removal action |
| Cost recovery — remedial action | 6 years | Initiation of physical on-site remedial construction |
| Contribution | 3 years | Date of judgment (for cost recovery judgments) or date of settlement (administrative or judicial) |

#### **[Inference]** Strategic Timing Considerations

Because the two limitations regimes run from different triggering events, PRPs contemplating voluntary action versus awaiting a government order must weigh not only the *Atlantic Research*/*Aviall* procedural distinction but also how each path affects the applicable limitations clock — voluntary cost-recovery actions under §107(a) tied to remedial action generally afford a longer 6-year window, whereas contribution claims under §113(f) carry the shorter 3-year period running from a more clearly defined triggering event (judgment or settlement date).
