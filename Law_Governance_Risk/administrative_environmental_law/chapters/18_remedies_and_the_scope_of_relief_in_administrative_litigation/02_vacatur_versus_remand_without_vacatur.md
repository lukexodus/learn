## Vacatur versus Remand without Vacatur

### Overview

**Key Points**

- When a reviewing court determines that agency action is unlawful under 5 U.S.C. § 706(2), it must select a remedy. The two dominant options are **vacatur** (setting the rule or order aside entirely, voiding its legal effect) and **remand without vacatur** (returning the matter to the agency for correction while allowing the flawed action to remain operative in the interim).
- This choice is doctrinally significant and practically consequential: vacatur immediately eliminates the challenged rule's legal force, while remand without vacatur preserves the regulatory status quo pending the agency's cure of the identified defect — producing materially different real-world outcomes for regulated parties, beneficiaries of the rule, and third parties.
- The choice between these remedies is one of the most consequential, and doctrinally underdeveloped, areas of administrative remedies law, with significant circuit variation and an absence of definitive Supreme Court resolution of the governing standard.

### The Default Rule: Vacatur as Ordinary Remedy

**Key Points**

- Text of § 706(2) instructs that a reviewing court "shall... hold unlawful **and set aside**" agency action found deficient under the enumerated standards — the plain statutory language uses mandatory "shall" language paired with "set aside," which many courts and commentators read as establishing vacatur as the **presumptive default remedy** once a § 706(2) violation is found.
- Historically, and particularly in the D.C. Circuit (which reviews a large share of nationwide agency rules), vacatur has been the ordinary consequence of a successful arbitrary-and-capricious or other § 706(2) challenge, with remand-without-vacatur treated as the **exception** requiring specific justification.

### The Allied-Signal Framework

#### Origin and Two-Factor Test

**Key Points**

***Allied-Signal, Inc. v. NRC***, 988 F.2d 146 (D.C. Cir. 1993), is the seminal case establishing the operative balancing framework used by the D.C. Circuit (and widely adopted or referenced by other circuits) to decide whether vacatur or remand-without-vacatur is the appropriate remedy. The test weighs two factors:

1. **The seriousness of the agency's deficiencies** — i.e., how likely it is that the agency will be able to justify its decision, or reach the same result through adequate reasoning, on remand. The more fundamental or unfixable the flaw, the stronger the case for vacatur.
2. **The disruptive consequences of vacatur** — i.e., the practical costs of nullifying the rule, including reliance interests of regulated parties, administrative burden of restarting the prior regime, and the risk of a regulatory gap with public health, safety, or environmental consequences.

Courts balance these factors holistically; neither factor is dispositive standing alone, and the framework does not specify precise weighting, leaving courts considerable discretion in application.

#### Applying Factor One: Curability of the Defect

**Key Points**

- **Procedural defects** (e.g., inadequate notice-and-comment, failure to respond to significant comments) are often viewed as more readily curable on remand — the agency may simply need to provide better process or explanation, making remand-without-vacatur more attractive since the underlying substantive judgment may well survive a corrected process.
- **Substantive/reasoning defects going to the heart of the agency's judgment** (e.g., reliance on a factor Congress forbade, a conclusion unsupported by any evidence in the record) are often viewed as less curable, weighing toward vacatur, since it is unclear the agency can reach a lawful result through any additional explanation.
- [Inference] Courts applying this factor engage in a kind of predictive judgment about the likely outcome on remand, which necessarily involves some speculation about how the agency would resolve the defect — a feature of the doctrine that has drawn academic criticism for asking courts to forecast agency behavior rather than adjudicate based on the record before them.

#### Applying Factor Two: Disruptive Consequences

**Key Points**

Courts consider a range of practical harms from vacatur, including:

- Loss of a regulatory framework that regulated parties and third parties have relied upon (e.g., a permitting scheme, compliance deadlines already built into business planning);
- Reversion to a prior regulatory regime that may itself be outdated, inadequate, or difficult to administer;
- Creation of a **regulatory gap** — particularly salient in environmental and public health rulemaking, where vacating a protective standard removes the regulatory floor entirely until a replacement rule is promulgated (a process that, under full notice-and-comment procedures, can take years);
- Effects on ongoing agency proceedings, permits already issued in reliance on the rule, or pending enforcement actions.

### Remand without Vacatur: Mechanics

**Key Points**

- Under remand-without-vacatur, the court's order typically: (1) holds the challenged action unlawful under the applicable § 706(2) ground; (2) remands to the agency, often with a specified or suggested timeline; and (3) explicitly declines to vacate, leaving the rule in legal effect during the remand period.
- Some courts retain jurisdiction over the remand, requiring the agency to report back or seek further court involvement if the defect is not adequately cured within a reasonable period — functioning as an ongoing supervisory mechanism rather than a one-time disposition.
- [Inference] Remand without vacatur effectively shifts the practical burden of the litigation's outcome: the challenged rule continues to bind regulated parties (and, in reverse-challenge cases, to under-protect beneficiaries) while the agency completes further process, meaning the remedy's real-world effect depends heavily on how quickly and faithfully the agency addresses the identified defect.

### Circuit Variation and Doctrinal Uncertainty

**Key Points**

- The D.C. Circuit has been the primary architect and most frequent user of the *Allied-Signal* framework, given its outsized role reviewing nationwide agency rules under statutes that channel review there (e.g., certain Clean Air Act provisions).
- Other circuits have applied remand-without-vacatur with varying degrees of consistency and, in some instances, have expressed skepticism about whether § 706's "shall... set aside" language actually permits courts to leave an unlawful rule in effect at all — an important **textualist critique** of remand-without-vacatur, arguing that the statute's mandatory language leaves no room for a remedy that stops short of setting the rule aside.
- [Unverified] Whether the Supreme Court will ultimately resolve the tension between § 706's "shall... set aside" text and the practice of remand-without-vacatur remains an open question; as of general knowledge available, the Court has not issued a definitive ruling squarely addressing whether remand-without-vacatur is a permissible reading of § 706(2), and commentary on this question continues to evolve.
- Practitioners should confirm the current circuit-specific posture and any recent Supreme Court developments when this issue affects case strategy, given the doctrine's continued flux.

### The Separate but Related Nationwide-Scope-of-Vacatur Debate

**Key Points**

- Distinct from the vacatur-versus-remand choice is the question of vacatur's **geographic/party scope** once a court decides vacatur is appropriate: does "set aside" nullify the rule nationwide (as to all regulated parties), or only as to the parties before the court?
- This scope question has drawn increasing academic and judicial attention, partly by analogy to the parallel debate over "universal" or "nationwide" injunctions in constitutional litigation, with critics arguing that party-specific vacatur better respects the traditional limits of judicial power to redress the injury of the parties actually before the court, while defenders of nationwide vacatur argue that a "set aside" rule, unlike an injunction against a party, operates on the legal existence of the rule itself and is therefore inherently universal in effect.
- [Unverified] This scope question, like the vacatur-versus-remand choice itself, remains unsettled at the Supreme Court level and is treated differently across circuits and even across panels within the same circuit; treat any claimed uniform rule with caution.

### Environmental Law Illustrations

**Key Points**

- **Vacatur risk in protective-standard cases**: If a court vacates an EPA air toxics or water quality standard found procedurally or substantively deficient, the practical effect may be to eliminate regulatory protection entirely until EPA completes a new notice-and-comment rulemaking — a process that can take years, creating an extended period without any operative standard on the relevant pollutant or activity.
- **Remand-without-vacatur as a protective-standard preservation tool**: Courts have used remand-without-vacatur specifically to avoid this gap, allowing an imperfect but operative environmental standard to remain in force while the agency addresses the identified legal defect — effectively treating regulatory continuity as an independent value distinct from the substantive correctness of the specific rule under review.
- **Reverse dynamic in deregulatory rule challenges**: Where a challenged rule is itself a *deregulatory* action (e.g., a rule weakening a prior emissions standard), vacating the deregulatory rule has the effect of reinstating the prior, more protective standard — meaning the disruption calculus under *Allied-Signal* can cut in favor of vacatur in this context, since voiding the challenged rule restores rather than removes regulatory protection.

### Practical Litigation Considerations

**Key Points**

- Parties litigating § 706(2) claims frequently brief the *Allied-Signal* factors explicitly as part of the remedy phase, separate from the merits of the underlying legal challenge — meaning a party may prevail on the merits (establishing a violation) and still need to separately persuade the court on the appropriate remedy.
- [Inference] Strategic considerations for litigants differ depending on posture: a petitioner challenging a rule as *insufficiently protective* often prefers remand-without-vacatur if vacating the flawed-but-protective rule would leave *no* standard in place, whereas a petitioner challenging a rule as *unlawfully burdensome* typically prefers full vacatur to eliminate the burden immediately.
- Courts sometimes combine remedies in complex cases — vacating discrete, severable portions of a rule while remanding other portions without vacatur — requiring careful severability analysis of which parts of a challenged action can be disaggregated from the rest.

### Common Exam/Analysis Traps

**Key Points**

- Do not assume remand-without-vacatur is available as a matter of course — it remains a genuinely contested remedy whose statutory basis is disputed by some courts and commentators; a careful answer should note this uncertainty rather than treating the *Allied-Signal* framework as uniformly settled law across all circuits.
- Distinguish the vacatur-versus-remand choice (this topic) from the nationwide-versus-party-specific scope-of-vacatur question (a related but analytically separate issue) — both are unsettled, but they address different dimensions of the remedy.
- Remember that remand-without-vacatur does not mean the litigation is over — courts often retain some supervisory role and the agency remains under an obligation to actually cure the identified defect, not merely to leave the rule in place indefinitely.
- In environmental cases, do not assume vacatur is always the "pro-environment" outcome or remand-without-vacatur is always the "pro-industry" outcome — the practical effect depends entirely on whether the challenged rule was protective or deregulatory in the first place.
