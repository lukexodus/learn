## Reparation, Restitution, and Compensation in State Responsibility Claims


### The Legal Problem Reparation Solves

When a state commits an internationally wrongful act, international law does not stop at declaring the act unlawful. It attaches **new legal obligations** to the responsible state. The most important of these is the duty to **repair** the consequences of the wrong. This body of rules is the law of **reparation**.

The governing codification is Part Two of the **Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (ARSIWA)**, adopted by the International Law Commission (ILC) in 2001 and commended to states by General Assembly Resolution 56/83. ARSIWA is not a treaty, but the International Court of Justice (ICJ) has repeatedly treated its reparation provisions as reflecting customary international law.

Recall that an **internationally wrongful act** exists under ARSIWA Article 2 when conduct attributable to a state constitutes a breach of an international obligation of that state. Reparation is the primary legal consequence that follows once that two-element test is satisfied and no circumstance precluding wrongfulness applies.

**Key Points**

- Reparation is an **automatic legal consequence** of an internationally wrongful act; it does not depend on a judicial order to come into existence (ARSIWA Article 28).
- Reparation is distinct from **cessation** (stopping a continuing wrong) and **assurances of non-repetition**, though the three are often pleaded together.
- Reparation has three recognised **forms**: restitution, compensation, and satisfaction (ARSIWA Article 34).
- The applicable standard is **full reparation**, but that standard is qualified by causation, remoteness, and proportionality limits.
- Reparation is owed to the **injured state**, and in some cases to the international community or to non-state beneficiaries, depending on the character of the obligation breached.

### Foundational Authority: The *Chorzów Factory* Principle

The modern law of reparation rests on two decisions of the Permanent Court of International Justice (PCIJ) in the *Case Concerning the Factory at Chorzów (Germany v. Poland)*.

- **Jurisdiction (1927):** The Court stated that it is a principle of international law, and even a general conception of law, that any breach of an engagement involves an obligation to make reparation.
- **Merits (1928):** The Court set out the standard that has become the reference point for the entire field:

> Reparation must, as far as possible, wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act and reestablish the situation which would, in all probability, have existed if that act had not been committed. Restitution in kind, or, if this is not possible, payment of a sum corresponding to the value which a restitution in kind would bear; the award, if need be, of damages for loss sustained which would not be covered by restitution in kind or payment in place of it.

Three ideas in this passage structure the whole doctrine:

1. **Wipe out all the consequences** (the full-reparation objective).
2. **Reestablish the situation which would, in all probability, have existed** (a counterfactual or "but for" baseline, not merely a return to the status quo ante).
3. **A hierarchy of forms**: restitution in kind first, then a money payment equivalent to restitution, then damages for residual loss.

The ICJ has consistently applied *Chorzów Factory*, for example in *Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project (Hungary/Slovakia)* (1997), *Avena and Other Mexican Nationals (Mexico v. United States)* (2004), *Legal Consequences of the Construction of a Wall in the Occupied Palestinian Territory* (Advisory Opinion, 2004), and *Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo (Democratic Republic of the Congo v. Uganda)* (2005).

### Article 31: The General Obligation of Full Reparation

Article 31 provides:

1. The responsible State is under an obligation to make **full reparation** for the injury caused by the internationally wrongful act.
2. Injury includes **any damage, whether material or moral**, caused by the internationally wrongful act of a State.

#### The Concept of "Injury"

The ILC's use of "injury" (rather than "damage") is deliberate. **Injury** is the broader legal concept and includes material damage (loss of property, lost earnings, costs) and **moral damage** (for example, loss of dignity, mental suffering, and injury to reputation or to the honour of the state). Where an obligation protects a state's sovereignty, the wrong itself is the injury, even if no financially quantifiable loss follows. This explains why a mere declaration of wrongfulness by the ICJ may, in the *Corfu Channel* case, constitute appropriate reparation.

#### Causation

Article 31 requires that the injury be **"caused by"** the wrongful act. The ILC commentary explains that the causal link must exist, but that not every consequence, however remote, is compensable. The wrongful act must be a sufficiently direct and certain source of the injury. The commentary uses language such as "directness," "foreseeability," and "proximity," and states that questions of remoteness are governed by the applicable primary rule and the specific circumstances, so international law lacks a single mechanical test.

The ICJ articulated its practical test in *Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Bosnia and Herzegovina v. Serbia and Montenegro)* (Merits, 2007): compensation requires a **"sufficiently direct and certain causal nexus"** between the wrongful act and the injury. The Court found Serbia in breach of its obligation to **prevent** genocide at Srebrenica, but declined to award compensation because it could not establish with the necessary degree of certainty that the genocide would in fact have been averted had Serbia acted in conformity with its obligation. It held that satisfaction, by way of a declaration of the breach, was appropriate.

**Example: Causal reasoning in a due diligence case**

State A breaches a due diligence obligation to prevent private armed groups on its territory from attacking State B. An attack kills civilians in State B.

- If State A's failure to act is established, the breach is the failure to prevent.
- Compensation depends on whether the failure was a sufficiently direct and certain cause of the deaths. If evidence shows that a reasonable exercise of due diligence would in all probability have prevented the attack, causation may be satisfied.
- If the evidence shows only a possibility that prevention would have succeeded, the *Bosnian Genocide* standard suggests that compensation may be refused, though satisfaction remains available.

This is a **legal-causation** question and is not the same as pure "but for" causation in fact.

#### The Injured Party and the Beneficiary

Article 33 clarifies that obligations of reparation may be owed to **another state, to several states, or to the international community as a whole**, depending on the character of the obligation breached. Article 33(2) adds that Part Two is **without prejudice to any right, arising from the international responsibility of a State, which may accrue directly to any person or entity other than a State**. This preserves the rights of individuals, such as human rights victims and foreign investors, who may pursue their own claims. In diplomatic protection, the state of nationality claims reparation in its own name, and the *Chorzów Factory* formulation was applied to a state claim on behalf of nationals.

#### Irrelevance of Internal Law (Article 32)

The responsible state may **not rely on its internal law** as justification for failure to comply with its obligations of cessation and reparation. This parallels Article 27 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) and Article 3 of ARSIWA.

### Forms of Reparation (Article 34)

Article 34 states that full reparation for the injury caused takes the form of **restitution, compensation and satisfaction, either singly or in combination**.

| Form | Function | Article | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Restitution** | Restore the situation that existed before the wrong (or, per *Chorzów*, the one that would have existed but for the wrong) | 35 | Primary in principle, subject to limits |
| **Compensation** | Provide a financial equivalent for financially assessable damage not made good by restitution | 36 | Residual, but most frequent in practice |
| **Satisfaction** | Remedy injury not capable of financial assessment, especially moral or symbolic injury | 37 | Residual, and often used for non-material injury |

The three forms are **cumulative and complementary**. A tribunal may combine them, for example ordering restitution of seized property, compensation for lost use, and satisfaction by way of an apology.

### Restitution (Article 35)

#### Definition and Scope

**Restitution** (sometimes *restitutio in integrum* or "restitution in kind") is the obligation to **re-establish the situation which existed before the wrongful act was committed**. Article 35 imposes this obligation **provided and to the extent that** restitution:

- **(a) Is not materially impossible; and**
- **(b) Does not involve a burden out of all proportion to the benefit deriving from restitution instead of compensation.**

#### Forms of Restitution

The ILC commentary recognises that restitution takes several forms:

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Material restitution** | Return of seized territory, vessels, property, cultural objects, archives, or persons unlawfully detained |
| **Juridical restitution** | Revocation, annulment, or amendment of a legislative or administrative measure; reversal of a judicial decision; the setting aside of an unlawful act |

**Juridical restitution** is illustrated by *Arrest Warrant of 11 April 2000 (Democratic Republic of the Congo v. Belgium)* (Judgment, 2002). The ICJ found that Belgium's issue and international circulation of an arrest warrant against the incumbent Foreign Minister of the DRC violated the immunity of a serving foreign minister. The Court held that Belgium must, **by means of its own choosing, cancel the warrant** and so inform the authorities to whom it had been circulated. This is restitution by legal act.

In *Avena* (2004), the ICJ held that the appropriate reparation for the failure to comply with consular notification obligations under Article 36 of the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (VCCR) was **review and reconsideration** of the convictions and sentences of the affected Mexican nationals, by means of the United States' own choosing, taking account of the violation. The Court thus ordered a remedy that is closer to restitution than to monetary compensation, but which did not require annulment of the convictions as such.

#### Limits on Restitution

**Material impossibility (Article 35(a)).** Restitution cannot be required where the object has been destroyed, the situation has irreversibly changed, or the injured state has lost interest through legal developments. The ILC commentary distinguishes material impossibility from **legal impossibility** arising from the responsible state's domestic law, which is not a valid ground because of Article 32. If the responsible state's own law or institutions prevent restitution, that does not excuse the obligation.

**Disproportionate burden (Article 35(b)).** The limitation applies only where there is a **grave disproportion** between the burden on the responsible state and the benefit to the injured state from restitution as compared with compensation. The commentary explains that the exception is meant to apply only where the balance is **clearly** against restitution, and that it should be applied with a presumption in favour of restitution, though it is narrower in the context of the protection of individuals from the consequences of a wrongful act.

**Example**

In *Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay (Argentina v. Uruguay)* (Judgment, 2010), the ICJ found that Uruguay breached its **procedural** obligations under the 1975 Statute of the River Uruguay (obligations to inform and consult) in authorising the construction of a pulp mill. Argentina asked the Court to order the dismantling of the mill as restitution. The Court declined, holding that Uruguay had **not** breached the substantive obligations concerning environmental protection, and that a declaration of the procedural breach constituted appropriate satisfaction. It reasoned that dismantling would be disproportionate given that the substantive obligations were not violated.

**Key Points**

- Restitution is the **primary form** in principle, but courts and tribunals often move to compensation in practice because of material impossibility or proportionality.
- Juridical restitution (annulling laws, decisions, or warrants) is available and has been ordered, though the ICJ typically leaves the **means** to the responsible state.
- The doctrine of restitution links to the *Chorzów* baseline: it aims at the situation that would have existed but for the wrong.

### Compensation (Article 36)

#### The Rule

Article 36 provides:

1. The State responsible for an internationally wrongful act is under an obligation to **compensate for the damage caused thereby, insofar as such damage is not made good by restitution**.
2. The compensation shall **cover any financially assessable damage including loss of profits insofar as it is established**.

Compensation is thus the **residual monetary remedy** that fills the gap left by restitution. In practice it is the most frequently awarded remedy in state responsibility claims and in investment arbitration.

#### Heads of Compensable Damage

| Head | Content |
| --- | --- |
| ***Damnum emergens*** (actual loss) | Value of property destroyed, seized, or damaged; costs incurred |
| ***Lucrum cessans*** (lost profits) | Profits that would have been earned but for the wrong, insofar as established with sufficient certainty |
| **Moral damage to individuals** | Non-material harm to individuals (pain and suffering, loss of reputation, loss of loved ones), which the ILC commentary places within compensable damage insofar as it is financially assessable |
| **Costs and expenses** | Legal fees and costs incurred in redressing the wrong, though the position on legal costs is inconsistent across fora |
| **Environmental damage** | Costs of clean-up, restoration, and (in recent ICJ practice) impairment or loss of ecosystem goods and services |

#### Valuation Standards

Valuation is the technical heart of compensation. Key principles:

- **Fair market value** is the standard approach to valuing property or an enterprise: the price a willing buyer would pay a willing seller. The *Chorzów Factory* formulation ("a sum corresponding to the value which a restitution in kind would bear") supports this approach.
- **Going-concern value** and the **discounted cash flow (DCF)** method are used for income-producing assets with an established record of profitability. Where the enterprise lacks a sufficient track record, tribunals have been reluctant to rely on DCF because of its speculative character.
- **Lost profits** are awarded where established with **reasonable certainty**. The ILC commentary states that they are compensable for a productive enterprise with a history of profitability, but that the claim becomes speculative where based on future earnings that are uncertain.
- **Date of valuation:** For **lawful** expropriation, the valuation date is usually the date of the taking. For **unlawful** acts, following *Chorzów Factory*, the valuation may be made at the date of the award if that yields a higher value, so that the injured party is placed in the position it would have been in but for the wrong. This difference between lawful and unlawful takings is a significant feature of the *Chorzów* jurisprudence, applied in the *ADC Affiliate Ltd v. Hungary* (ICSID, 2006) award.

**Key distinction: lawful versus unlawful expropriation.** Recall that **expropriation** is a state's deprivation of an investor's property or its equivalent. International law permits lawful expropriation subject to conditions (public purpose, non-discrimination, due process, and prompt, adequate and effective compensation, known as the **Hull formula**). Compensation for a **lawful** expropriation is a condition of its lawfulness and is measured by fair market value at the time of the taking. Compensation for **unlawful** expropriation is a remedy for breach and follows the more generous full-reparation standard of *Chorzów Factory*. [Inference] The distinction is deeply embedded in the *Chorzów* dicta and in investment-treaty case law, though some tribunals have questioned whether it should continue to make a difference in practice where treaty provisions specify the standard of compensation.

#### ICJ Practice on Compensation

For many decades the ICJ rarely awarded monetary compensation, but recent practice shows a shift.

| Case | Outcome |
| --- | --- |
| ***Corfu Channel (United Kingdom v. Albania)*** (Assessment of Compensation, 1949) | First ICJ compensation award. Albania was ordered to pay compensation for the destruction of British warships and loss of life caused by mines, calculated on the basis of replacement cost of the destroyed ship, with adjustments |
| ***Ahmadou Sadio Diallo (Republic of Guinea v. Democratic Republic of the Congo)*** (Compensation, 2012) | The Court awarded USD 95,000 for non-material injury and USD 10,000 for material injury (lost personal property), refusing compensation for the loss of profits from companies owned by the individual, as the Court considered the claim of lost remuneration and other items insufficiently proven. The judgment addressed the application of equitable considerations to non-material injury |
| ***Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo (DRC v. Uganda)*** (Reparations, 2022) | The Court, after finding in 2005 that Uganda had violated the prohibition on the use of force, international humanitarian law, and human rights law, awarded the DRC a **lump sum of USD 325 million**, payable in five annual instalments, in respect of damage to persons (USD 225 million), damage to property (USD 40 million) and damage related to natural resources (USD 60 million). The Court used a **global sum** approach, reasoning that, given the scale and the impossibility of proving every individual loss, a lump-sum award was appropriate. |
| ***Certain Activities Carried Out by Nicaragua in the Border Area (Costa Rica v. Nicaragua)*** (Compensation, 2018) | First ICJ award of compensation for **environmental damage**. The Court awarded compensation for both the costs of restoration and for the impairment or loss of environmental goods and services, using an **ecosystem services** approach |

#### Environmental Damage: The 2018 Costa Rica v. Nicaragua Approach

Nicaragua's unlawful excavation of canals in a Costa Rican wetland led to the ICJ's 2018 compensation judgment. The Court held that:

- Environmental damage is **compensable in principle** under international law, as an injury caused by an internationally wrongful act.
- Compensation may cover both **(a)** the impairment or loss of environmental goods and services in the period before recovery, and **(b)** the payment for restoration of the damaged environment (including the costs of natural recovery, where restoration is not feasible or is best achieved by allowing natural regeneration).
- Where precise quantification is not possible, the Court may apply an **overall valuation** based on the evidence, and need not reject the claim entirely.

The Court used an "ecosystem services" approach: the assessment of the goods and services provided by the affected environment (such as timber, gas regulation, air quality regulation, and biodiversity), to value the losses. The judgment is widely regarded as a landmark for the treatment of environmental harm in state responsibility.

#### Compensation and Mass Claims

In situations of mass injury, individual adjudication is not feasible. States have resorted to **claims commissions** and **lump-sum settlements**:

- **United Nations Compensation Commission (UNCC)**, established by Security Council Resolution 687 (1991) and Resolution 692 (1991), to process claims arising from Iraq's invasion and occupation of Kuwait. It processed some 2.7 million claims and awarded approximately USD 52 billion, paid from a percentage of Iraqi oil export revenues. It is the leading example of a mass claims mechanism.
- **Eritrea-Ethiopia Claims Commission (EECC)**, established under the 2000 Algiers Agreement, awarded damages to each state for violations of international humanitarian law and *jus ad bellum* in the 1998-2000 war, with final awards in 2009.
- **Iran-United States Claims Tribunal** (established 1981 under the Algiers Accords), which decided several thousand claims arising from the Iranian Revolution and its aftermath, and whose jurisprudence has shaped the law of valuation and expropriation.

**Key Points**

- Compensation must cover **financially assessable damage**, including lost profits where established.
- Valuation standards differ between lawful and unlawful takings, and the *Chorzów* principle supports a more generous measure for unlawful acts.
- Recent ICJ practice shows a willingness to award compensation, including **lump-sum** and **environmental** awards, using equitable estimation where precise proof is not possible.
- The law of compensation does not include punitive damages.

### Satisfaction (Article 37)

#### The Rule

Article 37 provides that a state responsible for an internationally wrongful act must give **satisfaction for the injury caused insofar as it cannot be made good by restitution or compensation**. Satisfaction may consist of:

- An **acknowledgement of the breach**;
- An **expression of regret**;
- A **formal apology**; or
- **Another appropriate modality**.

Satisfaction must **not be out of proportion to the injury** and may **not take a form humiliating to the responsible State** (Article 37(3)).

#### Function

Satisfaction addresses injuries that are **not financially assessable**, notably the **moral or symbolic injury** to the state itself, arising from an infringement of its rights, such as a violation of its sovereignty, territorial integrity, or diplomatic dignity, or from insults to its symbols (its flag or its representatives).

Unlike compensation, satisfaction is **not intended to be punitive** and does not include punitive or exemplary damages. The ILC commentary stresses that international law does not recognise punitive damages in state responsibility.

#### Forms and Practice

| Form | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Declaration of wrongfulness** by a court or tribunal | *Corfu Channel* (Merits, 1949): the ICJ held that its own declaration that British minesweeping (Operation Retail) violated Albanian sovereignty constituted appropriate satisfaction |
| **Apology or expression of regret** | *Rainbow Warrior* (1986 UN Secretary-General ruling and 1990 arbitration): France was required to apologise to New Zealand for the sinking of the vessel in Auckland harbour and to pay compensation (USD 7 million), and later the tribunal recommended the establishment of a fund to promote friendly relations between the two states' citizens |
| **Punishment or disciplinary action against responsible individuals** | Recognised in practice as an appropriate modality where wrongdoing was committed by state officials, for example in cases arising from attacks on foreign diplomatic missions |
| **Assurances or guarantees** | Sometimes overlapping with non-repetition under Article 30 |
| **Symbolic measures** | Commemoration, memorials, and public acknowledgement |

#### The Declaratory Judgment

A finding of wrongfulness by a competent court is itself a form of satisfaction. The ICJ frequently treats its own declaration as sufficient where the injury is essentially legal or moral, as in *Corfu Channel* (Merits) and in *Pulp Mills* (2010), and in *Bosnian Genocide* (2007), where the Court declared that Serbia had failed to prevent genocide and held that this declaration was itself appropriate satisfaction. This approach recognises that vindication of the legal right is often what the injured state principally seeks.

**Key Points**

- Satisfaction is a **residual** form, used for non-material injury.
- It **may not be humiliating** to the responsible state, and it is not punitive.
- A **declaration of breach** by a tribunal is a widely used form of satisfaction.

### Interest (Article 38) and Contribution to the Injury (Article 39)

#### Interest

Article 38 provides that **interest on any principal sum due under this chapter shall be payable when necessary in order to ensure full reparation**. The rate of interest and mode of calculation are to be set so as to achieve that result. Interest runs from the date when the principal sum should have been paid until the date the obligation to pay is fulfilled.

Interest is thus an **ancillary** element of compensation; it is not automatic, but it is available whenever needed to achieve full reparation. Tribunals have differing approaches: some award **simple interest**, others **compound interest**, and the latter has gained ground in investment arbitration on the theory that a claimant deprived of capital would ordinarily have reinvested it. The ILC commentary acknowledges that the practice is not uniform but supports the flexible approach. [Unverified] The trend in investment awards is towards compound interest at commercial rates, but courts of general jurisdiction such as the ICJ have historically awarded simple interest where they have awarded interest at all, and the position varies by forum.

#### Contribution to the Injury

Article 39 provides that in determining reparation, account shall be taken of the **contribution to the injury by wilful or negligent action or omission of the injured State or any person or entity in relation to whom reparation is sought**.

This is the international law analogue of **contributory negligence** or comparative fault. The ILC commentary emphasises that the relevant conduct must be **wilful or negligent**, and that it must have contributed **materially** to the injury.

The rule applies to both the injured state and to private persons on whose behalf a claim is brought. In *LaGrand (Germany v. United States)* (2001), the United States had raised the argument that Germany's delay in claiming contributed to the injury, but the Court was not persuaded on the facts. In *Certain Activities Carried Out by Nicaragua*, the Court considered but did not apply a reduction. [Inference] In practice, courts apply Article 39 sparingly and require clear evidence of causal contribution.

### Cessation, Assurances, and Guarantees of Non-Repetition (Article 30)

Although distinct from reparation, cessation and non-repetition are often pleaded alongside it and warrant treatment for completeness.

Article 30 requires the responsible state:

- **(a)** To **cease** the act, **if it is continuing**; and
- **(b)** To **offer appropriate assurances and guarantees of non-repetition, if circumstances so require**.

**Cessation** applies only to **continuing** wrongful acts. It is a consequence of the continuing duty of performance (Article 29): the primary obligation remains in force despite the breach. In the *Arrest Warrant* case, cessation was effected by cancelling the warrant. In *Legal Consequences of the Construction of a Wall* (2004), the ICJ found that Israel was under an obligation to cease the construction of the wall in the occupied territory and to dismantle the parts already built.

**Assurances and guarantees of non-repetition** are forward-looking. In *LaGrand* (2001), the ICJ held that the United States' commitment to ensure implementation of specific measures to comply with its consular notification obligations, including a compliance programme, was sufficient as a general assurance. The Court also held that if the United States failed in its obligation again, it must allow review and reconsideration of the conviction and sentence in the case of the affected persons. The Court declined, in general, to order **specific** guarantees, stating that a state's commitment is to be taken at face value unless circumstances require more. Assurances are ordered only where there is a **real risk of repetition** (a factor referred to in the ILC commentary). In *Bosnian Genocide*, the Court did not order assurances against Serbia, finding that the circumstances did not require them.

**Comparison**

| Concept | Temporal orientation | Trigger | Article |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cessation | Present (stop ongoing breach) | Continuing wrongful act | 30(a) |
| Assurances and guarantees of non-repetition | Future (prevent recurrence) | Circumstances so require | 30(b) |
| Reparation | Past (repair consequences) | Injury caused | 31 |

### Reparation for Serious Breaches of Peremptory Norms (Articles 40 and 41)

Chapter III of Part Two applies where a state commits a **serious breach** of an obligation arising under a **peremptory norm** (*jus cogens*) of general international law. Recall that a peremptory norm is a norm accepted and recognised by the international community of states as a whole as one from which no derogation is permitted (VCLT Article 53). A breach is **serious** if it involves a **gross or systematic failure** by the responsible state to fulfil the obligation (Article 40(2)).

Article 41 imposes consequences on **all states** (in addition to those under Part Two for the responsible state):

1. States must **cooperate to bring to an end**, through lawful means, any serious breach.
2. No state shall **recognise as lawful** a situation created by the serious breach, nor **render aid or assistance** in maintaining that situation.

The ICJ applied similar reasoning in *Legal Consequences for States of the Continued Presence of South Africa in Namibia* (Advisory Opinion, 1971) and *Wall* (2004). In the *Wall* opinion, the Court held that all states are under an obligation not to recognise the illegal situation resulting from the construction of the wall and not to render aid or assistance in maintaining it, and that Israel was obliged to make reparation for damage caused to all natural or legal persons affected, including restitution of property seized and compensation where restitution was materially impossible.

**Debate on the customary status of Article 41.** The duty of non-recognition is widely regarded as established in customary law. The **duty to cooperate** in Article 41(1) is more contested, and the ILC itself described it as, at least in part, progressive development. Sceptics argue that no general duty of positive cooperation has crystallised in state practice, and that the reference to "lawful means" is vague. Supporters respond that the ICJ's *Wall* opinion treated a duty of cooperation as flowing from the *erga omnes* character of the norms breached.

### Who Can Claim Reparation: Injured States, Other States, and Individuals

#### Injured State

Recall that under ARSIWA Article 42, an **injured state** is one to which the obligation breached is owed individually, or which is specially affected by the breach of an obligation owed to a group or to the international community. It is entitled to claim cessation, assurances, and full reparation.

#### Non-Injured States (Article 48)

Recall that **erga omnes obligations** are obligations owed to the international community as a whole, in whose protection all states have a legal interest (*Barcelona Traction, Light and Power Company, Limited (Belgium v. Spain)*, Second Phase, 1970), and that **erga omnes partes obligations** are owed to all parties to a treaty regime protecting a collective interest. Under Article 48(2), a state other than an injured state that is entitled to invoke responsibility may claim:

- **(a)** Cessation and assurances or guarantees of non-repetition; and
- **(b)** **Performance of the obligation of reparation in the interest of the injured State or of the beneficiaries of the obligation breached.**

The second limb means that a non-injured state claims reparation **not for itself** but for the injured state or for the actual beneficiaries, such as victims of human rights violations. This structure has been applied in the ICJ's *Belgium v. Senegal* (2012) and *The Gambia v. Myanmar* (Preliminary Objections, 2022) proceedings on standing, though neither has yet reached a stage of reparation on the merits. [Inference] The design of Article 48(2)(b) leaves open questions about how reparation is to be delivered to victims where the injured state is unable or unwilling to receive it.

#### Individuals and Private Claimants

Article 33(2) preserves rights accruing directly to persons or entities other than states. In practice, individuals obtain reparation through:

- **Diplomatic protection**, where the state of nationality espouses the claim. Recall that under the *Mavrommatis* principle (*Mavrommatis Palestine Concessions*, PCIJ, 1924), a state asserts its own right when it takes up the case of one of its nationals. The ILC's 2006 Articles on Diplomatic Protection elaborate the nationality-of-claims and exhaustion-of-local-remedies rules. The compensation is, in principle, the state's, though in *Ahmadou Sadio Diallo* the ICJ emphasised that compensation was to be assessed by reference to the injury to the individual and awarded a sum specific to his losses.
- **Direct treaty rights**, for example, in human rights treaty bodies and courts (such as the European Court of Human Rights' just satisfaction under Article 41 of the European Convention on Human Rights, and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights' reparations regime under Article 63(1) of the American Convention on Human Rights), and in investor-state arbitration under bilateral investment treaties.

The **Inter-American Court of Human Rights** has developed a notably broad conception of reparation, including restitution, rehabilitation, satisfaction, guarantees of non-repetition, and compensation, drawing on the 2005 UN Basic Principles and Guidelines on the Right to a Remedy and Reparation (General Assembly Resolution 60/147). This framework, which is non-binding, identifies **restitution, compensation, rehabilitation, satisfaction, and guarantees of non-repetition** as the five forms of reparation for gross violations of human rights and serious violations of international humanitarian law. It adds **rehabilitation** (medical, psychological, legal, and social services) to the ARSIWA list.

### Reparation Before the ICJ: Procedure and Practice

- **Bifurcation of proceedings:** The ICJ commonly finds liability in one judgment and reserves the question of reparation for a later phase if the parties fail to agree. In *Armed Activities* (DRC v. Uganda), the 2005 merits judgment found responsibility and ordered that reparation be settled by negotiation, failing which the Court would settle the question on the request of either party, leading to the 2022 reparations judgment.
- **Standard of proof:** Claimants bear the burden of proving injury and causation. Where full proof is impossible due to the nature of the conflict, the Court has used estimation and equitable considerations (*Armed Activities*, 2022; *Diallo*, 2012; *Costa Rica v. Nicaragua*, 2018).
- **Form:** The Court usually leaves the means of implementing restitution or non-repetition to the responsible state's own choosing, while specifying the result to be achieved.
- **Execution:** Judgments of the ICJ are binding under Article 94(1) of the UN Charter, and Article 94(2) permits recourse to the Security Council in case of non-compliance. In practice, the Security Council's power under Article 94(2) is constrained by the veto of permanent members, and the absence of a general enforcement mechanism means that compliance rests largely on state consent, reputational costs, and countermeasures.

### Worked Example

**Example**

State R's naval forces unlawfully seize a commercial vessel owned by a company from State I on the high seas, detaining the crew for six weeks, and later sell the vessel. The seizure breaches the freedom of navigation under UNCLOS. State I brings an Annex VII arbitration and the tribunal finds R responsible.

1. **Cessation (Art. 30(a)):** The detention of crew has ended; the seizure was instantaneous, so cessation is largely moot. If any crew were still held, release would be required.
2. **Assurances (Art. 30(b)):** State I asks for assurances of non-repetition. The tribunal orders them only if it finds a real risk of recurrence.
3. **Restitution (Art. 35):** The vessel has been sold to a third-party purchaser abroad. Return is **materially impossible** or would impose a disproportionate burden on innocent third parties. Restitution is therefore not ordered, and the claim moves to compensation.
4. **Compensation (Art. 36):**
   - *Damnum emergens:* fair market value of the vessel at the time of the seizure (or at the award date for an unlawful taking, if higher), plus the value of cargo lost.
   - *Lucrum cessans:* lost charter income if the company shows established, non-speculative charter arrangements.
   - *Crew:* compensation for the detention of the crew. Under the reasoning of *M/V "Saiga" (No. 2)* (ITLOS, 1999), the flag state may claim for injury to crew of any nationality.
   - *Moral damage:* the crew's detention may support compensation for non-material injury.
5. **Satisfaction (Art. 37):** A declaration of unlawfulness and, if warranted, a formal apology for the violation of State I's flag-state rights.
6. **Interest (Art. 38):** Interest from the date of seizure to the date of payment, at a rate set to ensure full reparation.
7. **Contribution (Art. 39):** If the vessel's master ignored a lawful warning, R may argue that this contributed to the injury and seek a reduction in compensation. A tribunal would assess whether the master's conduct was wilful or negligent and materially contributory.

### Application to Philippine Interests: The South China Sea Arbitration

In the **South China Sea Arbitration (Philippines v. China)** (PCA Case No. 2013-19, Award of 12 July 2016), the Annex VII tribunal, constituted under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), found that China had violated multiple provisions, including breaches of the Philippines' sovereign rights in its exclusive economic zone (EEZ), obligations concerning the protection and preservation of the marine environment (through large-scale island-building and harm to coral reef ecosystems), and navigation-safety obligations.

Several points about reparation are worth noting:

- **Declaratory character of the Award.** The Philippines' submissions in the arbitration sought principally **declarations** on the entitlements of maritime features and the lawfulness of China's conduct, together with a request that China cease unlawful activities. The Tribunal did not award monetary compensation, and the Philippines did not seek it in the proceedings. The Award thus operates primarily through **declaration** and the legal consequences of cessation.
- **Cessation and non-repetition as the operative consequences.** Under ARSIWA Article 30 and as reflected in the Tribunal's findings, China's continuing conduct in areas the Tribunal found to fall within the Philippines' EEZ engages the obligation to **cease** continuing wrongful acts. China's rejection of the Award as "null and void" does not alter its binding character under UNCLOS Annex VII Article 11 and Article 296, and, under ARSIWA Article 32, China may not rely on its internal law or its rejection of the Award as a justification for non-compliance with obligations of cessation and reparation.
- **Environmental damage and reparation.** The Tribunal's findings that China's activities caused severe, irreparable harm to the coral reef ecosystem raise, in principle, questions of restitution (materially impossible for destroyed reefs) and compensation for environmental damage. [Inference] The approach of the ICJ in *Costa Rica v. Nicaragua* (2018), which valued lost ecosystem goods and services, offers a doctrinal template for any future claim, though no such claim has been pursued.
- **Standing of other UNCLOS parties.** [Inference] Because obligations concerning the marine environment under UNCLOS may be characterised as *erga omnes partes*, other UNCLOS parties may have standing under ARSIWA Article 48 to invoke China's responsibility and to claim reparation in the interest of the injured state or beneficiaries; no state has pursued such a claim.
- **Enforcement gap.** The practical limits on enforcing arbitral awards without a central enforcement authority mean that the reparation regime in this dispute depends on diplomatic pressure, reputational costs, and, potentially, lawful countermeasures under ARSIWA Articles 49 to 54 that respect the limits on proportionality, reversibility, and the exclusion of force.

### Areas of Continuing Debate

1. **Full reparation versus practical limits.** The *Chorzów* formula demands full reparation, but limits on causation, remoteness, proof, and the responsible state's capacity to pay mean that awards are often partial. Proponents of strict full reparation argue that departures from it reward wrongdoing and undermine deterrence. Others argue that awards must be realistic to be complied with, and that lump-sum settlements, as in the UNCC and *DRC v. Uganda*, achieve rough justice where individualised proof is unattainable.
2. **Lawful versus unlawful takings.** Whether the distinction in the standard of compensation remains meaningful in the era of investment treaties that specify the compensation standard.
3. **Punitive or exemplary damages.** ARSIWA excludes punitive damages, but scholars and some decisions (particularly in relation to gross human rights violations) have questioned whether the exclusion holds in cases of grave breaches. [Speculation] Reparation regimes in human rights bodies may in practice function with a deterrent element, without formally adopting the label of punitive damages.
4. **Compound interest.** Whether compound interest should be the norm in general international law, given the greater acceptance in investment arbitration than in inter-state litigation.
5. **Reparation for collective or environmental harm.** How to value damage to global commons and how to allocate reparation where the injured party is the international community or where no identifiable state has suffered specific injury.
6. **Reparation to non-state victims where the injured state is unwilling to forward it.** Article 48(2)(b) leaves unresolved how reparation reaches beneficiaries in practice.
7. **Reparation and state immunity from enforcement.** A finding of responsibility and an award of compensation may face obstacles to enforcement against state assets due to sovereign immunity from execution, a topic addressed by the law of jurisdictional immunities.

### Common Misconceptions

- **"Reparation means paying money."** Incorrect. Compensation is only one of three forms, and it is residual to restitution. A declaration alone may constitute adequate reparation.
- **"The responsible state can choose not to repair if its domestic law prevents restitution."** Incorrect. Article 32 bars reliance on internal law, though material impossibility can still limit restitution.
- **"International law awards punitive damages against wrongdoing states."** Generally incorrect. Reparation is compensatory, and satisfaction is not punitive.
- **"Any consequence of a wrongful act is compensable."** Incorrect. There must be a sufficiently direct and certain causal link.
- **"Reparation depends on a court order."** Incorrect. The obligation arises by operation of law upon the commission of the wrongful act; a judgment declares and quantifies it.
- **"Only the injured state can claim."** Incorrect. Under Article 48, other states may invoke responsibility and claim reparation in the interest of the injured state or the beneficiaries of the obligation.

### Conclusion

The law of reparation translates the abstract finding of state responsibility into concrete obligations. Anchored in the *Chorzów Factory* principle that reparation should eliminate the consequences of the wrong, ARSIWA structures the remedy through three cumulative forms (restitution, compensation, and satisfaction), supplemented by interest and moderated by contributory fault. The doctrine is characterised by a preference for restitution in principle and a practical predominance of compensation; by strict requirements of causation and proof; by a refusal to permit punitive damages; and by increasing judicial willingness, evident in recent ICJ practice, to award compensation for mass harm and environmental damage using lump-sum and ecosystem-services approaches. The main challenges remain enforcement in a decentralised legal order, valuation of non-market and collective harms, and the delivery of reparation to individual victims and the international community as beneficiaries.
