## Condemnation of Easement Interests

### Overview

Condemnation of easement interests addresses the eminent domain power as applied to non-possessory property interests rather than fee simple ownership — both the government's authority to condemn (take) an easement interest that already exists on private land, and the more common scenario of government condemning a *new* easement over private property for a public purpose, taking less than the full bundle of ownership rights. This topic examines how just compensation principles and public use doctrine apply differently when the property interest at stake is an easement rather than a fee, and how condemned easements interact with existing private easements and servitudes on the same land.

---

### Two Distinct Scenarios

**Scenario One: Government Condemns a New Easement Over Private Land**

- The government takes a partial, defined interest in privately owned land — a right to use the land for a specific purpose (utility lines, drainage, road right-of-way, avigation/overflight, conservation restriction, pipeline corridor) — while the underlying fee owner retains title and all rights not encumbered by the easement.
- This is the more common scenario in modern practice: government frequently prefers to condemn only the specific interest actually needed for its project (an easement) rather than the full fee, since this minimizes both the scope of the taking and the resulting compensation obligation, while still securing the necessary rights.

**Scenario Two: Government Condemns an Existing Private Easement**

- Where private property is already burdened or benefited by an existing easement (e.g., a private right-of-way, a utility easement, a conservation easement held by a land trust), a government project may require extinguishing, relocating, or otherwise taking that existing easement interest itself — requiring separate compensation to the easement holder (dominant estate owner) independent of any compensation owed to the servient estate's fee owner.
- This scenario frequently arises when government projects (highway construction, public facility siting) affect land already subject to private easements, requiring condemnation proceedings that must account for and compensate multiple distinct property interests simultaneously.

---

### Valuation of a Condemned Easement (New Easement Scenario)

**The Core Valuation Principle: Diminution in Servient Estate Value**

- Just compensation for a newly condemned easement is measured not by some abstract, freestanding value of the easement itself, but by the **diminution in value to the servient estate** caused by imposing the easement — the difference between the property's fair market value unencumbered by the easement and its fair market value subject to the easement's restrictions.
- This "before and after" methodology parallels severance damages analysis in partial fee takings (addressed in the Just Compensation Principles topic), and for good reason: an easement condemnation is functionally a partial taking, leaving the fee owner with a diminished, but not eliminated, bundle of property rights.

**Factors Affecting the Diminution Calculation**

- **Scope and exclusivity of the easement**: An exclusive easement effectively excluding the fee owner from using the burdened area (e.g., a permanent below-ground pipeline corridor prohibiting any surface construction) will generally produce a greater diminution than a non-exclusive easement permitting continued, compatible use by the fee owner (e.g., a utility easement allowing continued agricultural use of the surface).
- **Duration**: A permanent easement produces a correspondingly larger diminution than a temporary construction easement (common for utility or infrastructure projects, granting the condemning authority temporary access rights during construction before the land reverts to unencumbered fee ownership).
- **Severance-type effects**: Where the easement bisects a larger parcel or otherwise impairs the usability of the remaining, unencumbered portions (e.g., a pipeline easement preventing efficient farming operations across the full tract, or fragmenting a parcel's development potential), severance-damages-style compensation for the remainder may be added to the direct diminution attributable to the easement area itself.
- **Highest and best use of the underlying fee**: Consistent with general just compensation principles, the diminution calculation accounts for the servient estate's highest and best use — an easement restricting development on land with significant development potential produces a substantially greater diminution than the same easement imposed on land whose highest and best use was already incompatible with development (e.g., agricultural land in an area with no realistic development pressure).

### Avigation Easements: A Prominent Applied Example

- Avigation easements — condemned by airport authorities and municipalities to secure the legal right for aircraft to overfly private property at specified altitudes — are among the most litigated examples of easement condemnation, tracing to *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946), which held that frequent, low-altitude military aircraft overflights so severely interfered with a chicken farmer's use of his land (destroying his poultry business due to the noise and disturbance) as to constitute a taking of an easement in the airspace above the property, even without any physical entry onto the land's surface.
- *Causby*'s recognition that overflight interference could constitute a taking prompted the now-standard practice of airport authorities proactively acquiring avigation easements over land near runways and under flight paths, compensating landowners in advance for the diminished value their property suffers due to anticipated aircraft noise and overflight — a preemptive alternative to defending against individual inverse condemnation or nuisance claims after the fact.
- Compensation for avigation easements is typically measured by the diminution in the servient property's value attributable to the noise, vibration, and psychological impact of regular low-altitude overflight, often requiring specialized appraisal methodology (noise contour mapping, sound level measurements) distinct from more conventional easement valuation.

### Compensation When Condemning an Existing Private Easement

**Dominant Estate Holder's Compensable Interest**

- Where a government project requires extinguishing or relocating an existing private easement, the dominant estate holder (the easement's beneficiary) holds a compensable property interest independent of the servient estate owner's fee interest — both parties may be entitled to separate compensation determinations in the same condemnation proceeding, since both hold distinct, condemnable property rights in the same physical land.
- Valuation of the dominant estate holder's loss typically focuses on the value the easement provided to the dominant estate (e.g., the increased value a right-of-way easement contributed to an otherwise landlocked parcel, or the utility value of an access or utility easement now being extinguished), which may be measured by the diminution in the dominant parcel's value caused by losing the easement, analogous to how the servient owner's compensation is measured by the burden the easement imposed.

**Conservation Easements Held by Land Trusts**

- Where government condemnation affects land already burdened by a conservation easement (addressed in the Conservation and Environmental Easements chapter), the land trust holding the easement is generally entitled to a share of the condemnation award reflecting the value of its conservation interest — this connects directly to the extinguishment/proceeds framework under Treasury Regulation § 1.170A-14(g)(6), which requires that a conservation easement holder receive a share of extinguishment proceeds proportionate to the easement's value relative to the property's total value at the time of the original donation, specifically to preserve the tax-deductibility rationale underlying the original gift.
- This proceeds-sharing requirement means a government condemnation affecting a conservation-easement-encumbered parcel typically requires a bifurcated (or at least multi-party) compensation determination: the fee owner is compensated for their (easement-diminished) fee interest, and the land trust is separately compensated for the loss or partial loss of its conservation easement interest, with the land trust's stewardship obligations (discussed in that chapter) generally requiring the organization to actively participate in the condemnation proceeding to protect and properly value its interest.

### Public Use and Necessity for Easement Condemnations

- The public use requirement (addressed in its own topic) applies identically to easement condemnations as to fee condemnations — the government must establish that acquiring the easement interest serves a valid public use or purpose, subject to the same deferential *Berman*/*Midkiff*/*Kelo* framework (or the relevant state's more protective standard, where applicable).
- A separate and distinct requirement in many condemnation statutes is a finding of **necessity** — not merely that some public use is served, but that condemning an easement (as opposed to acquiring the full fee, or not acquiring any interest at all) is a reasonably necessary and appropriately scoped means of achieving that public use; courts generally defer substantially to the condemning authority's determination of the scope of interest needed, though excessively broad or duration-mismatched easement condemnations (e.g., seeking a permanent easement where the underlying public need is genuinely temporary) can be challenged as exceeding the necessary scope.

### Comparison Table: Fee Condemnation vs. Easement Condemnation

| Feature | Fee Simple Condemnation | Easement Condemnation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interest transferred | Full title | Defined, limited use right; fee owner retains residual title |
| Valuation method | Fair market value of entire parcel | Diminution in servient estate value (before/after methodology) |
| Owner's continued use | None (full transfer) | Continues, subject to easement's restrictions |
| Multiple compensable interests | Generally single fee owner (absent existing liens/leases) | Servient fee owner AND any existing dominant estate holders may each hold separate compensable interests |
| </br> | Severance damages | Applies to any uncondemned remainder in partial fee takings |
| Typical government preference | Where permanent, exclusive use is genuinely needed | Preferred where only a specific, limited use right is needed — minimizes compensation and preserves owner's residual use |

### Practical Example

A regional airport authority needs to secure overflight rights and construction clearance over a corridor of privately owned farmland approaching a runway expansion. Part of the corridor includes a parcel already burdened by a conservation easement held by a local land trust, protecting the farmland from development, and by a separate private drainage easement benefiting a neighboring downstream parcel.

- **Avigation easement over the farmland**: The authority condemns a permanent avigation easement establishing overflight rights and height restrictions on structures/vegetation. Compensation to the fee owner is measured by the diminution in the farmland's value caused by anticipated aircraft noise and the height restrictions, likely requiring noise-contour-based appraisal methodology, following the *Causby*-derived framework.
- **Conservation easement interaction**: Because the parcel is already burdened by a conservation easement, the land trust holding that easement has an independent compensable interest — if the avigation easement and associated height restrictions are compatible with the conservation easement's existing restrictions (both, for instance, prohibiting new structures), the practical diminution in the trust's specific interest may be minimal; but if the airport authority's height/clearance requirements require actually extinguishing or substantially modifying the conservation easement's terms, the land trust would be entitled to a share of any condemnation proceeds under the extinguishment/proceeds framework, and — consistent with its stewardship obligations — would need to actively participate in the proceeding to protect its interest and ensure any proceeds are redirected to other qualifying conservation purposes.
- **Drainage easement interaction**: The existing private drainage easement benefiting the downstream neighbor is a separate dominant estate interest; if the airport authority's construction or the new avigation easement's restrictions interfere with the drainage easement's function, the downstream neighbor (as dominant estate holder) may be entitled to separate compensation reflecting the impairment to their drainage rights, independent of and in addition to whatever compensation the farmland's fee owner receives.
- **Necessity scope check**: If the authority sought a permanent easement extending well beyond the area genuinely affected by the runway expansion's flight path, the farmland owner could challenge the condemnation's scope as exceeding what is reasonably necessary for the stated public purpose, even while conceding that some avigation easement over the genuinely affected portion is validly needed.

---

### Key Points

- Condemnation of an easement interest is valued by the diminution it causes to the servient estate's fair market value (a "before and after" methodology), not by any freestanding valuation of the easement in isolation — reflecting that the fee owner retains residual, if diminished, ownership rights.
- *United States v. Causby* established that severe overflight interference can constitute a taking of an easement in airspace, prompting the now-standard practice of airport authorities proactively condemning avigation easements and compensating landowners for the resulting diminution in value.
- Where an existing private easement (including a conservation easement) already burdens land subject to a new government condemnation, the dominant estate holder (including a land trust) generally holds an independent compensable interest requiring separate valuation and, in the conservation easement context, adherence to the extinguishment/proceeds-sharing framework under Treasury Regulation § 1.170A-14(g)(6).
- Public use and necessity requirements apply to easement condemnations on the same basis as fee condemnations, with an additional practical focus on whether the scope and duration of the specific easement interest sought is reasonably necessary for the stated public purpose.
- [Inference] Because the precise valuation methodology for avigation and other specialized easements, the procedural mechanics for compensating multiple simultaneous interest-holders in a single condemnation proceeding, and the necessity-review standard for easement scope all vary by state condemnation statute and case law, the general framework described here should be confirmed against the specific jurisdiction's eminent domain code and procedural rules for any actual condemnation matter.
