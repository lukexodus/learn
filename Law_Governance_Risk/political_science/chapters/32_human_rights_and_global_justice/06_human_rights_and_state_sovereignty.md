## Human Rights and State Sovereignty


### Definition and Conceptual Scope

The relationship between human rights and state sovereignty concerns a foundational and persistent tension within international relations and international law: the traditional Westphalian principle that states possess supreme, exclusive authority over matters within their own territory versus the post-1945 development of international human rights law, which asserts that how a state treats individuals within its jurisdiction is a legitimate matter of international concern rather than purely domestic prerogative.

**Key Points**

- This tension is often framed as a conflict between two competing organizing principles of the international order: sovereignty as **non-interference** and human rights as **universal entitlement** warranting external scrutiny and, in some formulations, action
- The relationship has evolved historically from near-absolute sovereign prerogative to a more qualified or "conditional" conception of sovereignty in contemporary international law and practice, though this evolution remains contested and incomplete
- The debate is not merely academic — it directly structures the legal and political permissibility of external human rights monitoring, conditionality, sanctions, and intervention

### The Westphalian Sovereignty Framework

#### Origins and Core Principles

The Peace of Westphalia (1648), while its precise role in originating the modern sovereignty concept is debated among historians, is conventionally treated as the symbolic origin point of the modern state system, establishing the principle that states possess supreme authority within their territorial borders and that other states have no legitimate basis for interfering in another state's internal affairs, including its religious and domestic governance arrangements.

#### Codification in the UN Charter

The UN Charter embeds Westphalian sovereignty as a foundational organizing principle:

- **Article 2(1)** affirms the "sovereign equality" of all UN member states
- **Article 2(4)** prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state
- **Article 2(7)** prohibits UN intervention in matters "essentially within the domestic jurisdiction of any state," with the explicit exception of Chapter VII enforcement measures

**Key Points**

- The simultaneous inclusion of strong non-intervention language (Article 2(7)) alongside human-rights-promotion commitments (Articles 1, 55, 56) within the same founding document reflects an unresolved tension built into the UN Charter's original architecture, rather than a problem that emerged only later
- This structural ambiguity has allowed both sovereignty-protective and human-rights-protective legal and political arguments to claim textual grounding in the Charter itself

### The Erosion and Qualification of Absolute Sovereignty

#### Human Rights as Legitimate International Concern

The development of binding international human rights treaties (the ICCPR, ICESCR, and subsequent specialized conventions) established that a state's treatment of its own population, even absent any cross-border effect, generates binding international legal obligations — a significant departure from a strictly domestic-jurisdiction conception of internal state conduct.

#### Sovereignty as Responsibility

The Responsibility to Protect framework (2005) explicitly reframed sovereignty not as a shield against external scrutiny but as entailing an affirmative responsibility toward one's own population, with that responsibility reverting to the international community when a state manifestly fails to fulfill it with respect to the four mass atrocity crimes (genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity). This represents the most prominent contemporary formal articulation of "conditional sovereignty" in international practice.

#### Humanitarian Intervention Practice

State practice regarding humanitarian intervention — including contested unilateral cases (e.g., NATO's 1999 Kosovo intervention, conducted without explicit Security Council authorization) — has generated ongoing debate over whether a customary international law exception to the non-intervention principle exists for cases of severe humanitarian emergency, even though such an exception has not been definitively established as settled law.

### Legal and Institutional Mechanisms Reflecting the Tension

#### Non-Coercive Mechanisms (Widely Accepted as Compatible with Sovereignty)

- Treaty ratification and reporting obligations (states voluntarily consent to monitoring)
- Universal Periodic Review and Special Procedures (peer review and expert monitoring, non-binding recommendations)
- Diplomatic pressure and public criticism ("naming and shaming")

#### Semi-Coercive Mechanisms (More Contested)

- Economic conditionality (linking aid, trade preferences, or loan terms to human rights performance)
- Targeted sanctions against individuals responsible for violations
- Universal jurisdiction prosecutions in third-country domestic courts

#### Coercive Mechanisms (Most Contested)

- Security Council-authorized enforcement action under Chapter VII (accepted as legally compatible with sovereignty because the Charter itself authorizes this exception)
- Unilateral or coalition military intervention without Security Council authorization (the most legally and politically contested category, exemplified by Kosovo)

**Example**

The contrast between Security Council-authorized action in Libya (2011, Resolution 1973) and the absence of authorized action in Syria (2011–present, due to repeated Russian and Chinese vetoes) illustrates how the formal legal pathway for reconciling human rights concerns with sovereignty protection — Security Council authorization — remains structurally dependent on great-power consensus, meaning the sovereignty/human-rights tension is not resolved by law alone but continues to be mediated by geopolitical power distribution.

### IR Theoretical Perspectives

#### Realism

Realists tend to treat sovereignty as the enduring foundational organizing principle of the international system, viewing human-rights-based challenges to sovereignty with skepticism, and arguing that when powerful states do act on human rights grounds, such action is typically explicable by underlying strategic interest rather than genuine prioritization of human rights over sovereignty as a matter of principle.

#### Liberalism

Liberal theorists tend to view the erosion of absolute sovereignty in favor of human rights accountability as a positive-sum development consistent with the deepening of a liberal international order, connecting domestic rights-respecting governance to broader liberal peace and cooperation dynamics.

#### Constructivism

Constructivist scholars analyze the sovereignty/human-rights tension as a case of **norm contestation**, in which the historically dominant Westphalian sovereignty norm has been gradually challenged and partially reconstituted by an emergent human rights norm through processes of norm entrepreneurship, socialization, and institutionalization — while noting that norm change of this kind is typically incomplete, contested, and unevenly applied rather than a clean linear replacement of one norm by another.

#### Critical and Postcolonial Perspectives

Critical and postcolonial scholars note that the practical application of "conditional sovereignty" has disproportionately constrained the sovereignty of weaker, often non-Western states, while major powers have generally retained effective sovereign immunity from equivalent external human rights enforcement (e.g., no realistic prospect of Security Council-authorized intervention against a P5 member), raising concerns that the sovereignty/human-rights framework, in practice, encodes rather than transcends existing global power hierarchies. [Inference: whether this pattern reflects a structural feature inherent to any enforcement system operating without centralized coercive authority above powerful states, or reflects a more specific critique of how the human rights regime itself has been selectively constructed and applied, remains a matter of ongoing scholarly debate.]

### Contemporary Manifestations of the Tension

- **Cyber sovereignty and digital governance**: debates over the extent to which international human rights norms (freedom of expression, privacy) should constrain states' assertions of sovereign authority over domestic internet governance and content regulation
- **Migration and refugee policy**: tension between states' sovereign prerogative to control borders and membership versus international refugee and human rights law obligations (e.g., non-refoulement)
- **Counter-terrorism and national security exceptions**: states frequently invoke security exceptions and derogation clauses within human rights treaties to justify measures that would otherwise constitute rights violations, testing the boundaries of legitimate sovereign discretion under international law
- **Populist and nationalist pushback**: a number of governments across different regions have in recent years explicitly invoked sovereignty and non-interference arguments to resist international human rights monitoring, treaty body findings, or ICC jurisdiction, reflecting a degree of political pushback against the post-1945 trajectory toward qualified sovereignty [Speculation: whether this represents a durable reversal of the post-1945 trajectory toward qualified sovereignty or a cyclical phase within a longer-term trend remains genuinely uncertain and contested among scholars.]

### Critiques and Unresolved Debates

- **Definitional ambiguity of "domestic jurisdiction"**: The UN Charter's Article 2(7) exception for matters "essentially within domestic jurisdiction" has never been given a precise, stable legal definition, leaving substantial interpretive latitude that different actors exploit according to their political interests
- **Selectivity in practice**: The degree to which sovereignty is treated as inviolable versus conditional varies significantly depending on the target state's relative power, generating persistent legitimacy concerns about consistency
- **No settled hierarchy**: International law does not provide a clear, generally accepted hierarchy resolving conflicts between sovereignty and human rights principles in hard cases, meaning outcomes often depend on the specific institutional and political context (e.g., Security Council composition) rather than a determinate legal rule
- **Sovereignty as protection for the vulnerable**: Some scholars and states argue that robust sovereignty protection itself serves a human-rights-protective function for weaker states, shielding them from potentially self-interested intervention by powerful states — complicating any simple characterization of sovereignty and human rights as strictly opposed values
