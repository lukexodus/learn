## Dry Cleaning


Dry cleaning — in tradecraft terminology — refers to the techniques used to detect surveillance before reaching a sensitive location, making a covert contact, or conducting any activity that must not be observed. The term derives from the concept of arriving "clean" — confirmed free of coverage. It is distinct from surveillance detection runs (SDRs) in that dry cleaning can refer to both the broader practice and specific techniques within it, though the terms are sometimes used interchangeably in open-source literature.

The foundational principle: you never go directly to anything sensitive. Every covert contact, dead drop, or sensitive location is preceded by a confirmed clean route. The value of this principle is not just detection — it is the habit of never assuming you are clean without verifying it.

---

### Surveillance Detection vs. Dry Cleaning

These terms are related but distinct in careful usage.

**Surveillance detection** is the broad discipline of identifying whether you are under coverage — active monitoring by a hostile service, law enforcement, or adversarial actor.

**Dry cleaning** refers specifically to the operational techniques used to confirm or disconfirm surveillance presence before proceeding to a sensitive act. It is the applied expression of surveillance detection in motion.

A **surveillance detection run (SDR)** is a planned route or sequence of activities specifically designed to surface surveillance coverage through controlled exposure at choke points.

---

### How Surveillance Works

Understanding detection requires understanding what you are detecting. Professional surveillance — as documented in open-source law enforcement and intelligence literature — operates on several principles:

**Team coverage**: Single-person surveillance is detectable. Professional coverage uses rotating teams of three to ten or more individuals, with handoffs designed to prevent any single face from appearing repeatedly. Mobile surveillance teams use vehicles, foot personnel, and sometimes fixed observation points in combination.

**Distance and patience**: Professional surveillance maintains distance and accepts losing brief visual contact rather than risk burning coverage by closing in. The team knows the subject's likely destination and can reacquire.

**Blend**: Surveillance personnel are chosen and dressed to match the environment. A team covering a business district looks like business district occupants. Coverage in a residential area uses vehicles and behaviors consistent with that environment.

**Trigger and follow**: A fixed observer — the trigger — monitors a known location (residence, workplace) and alerts mobile units when the subject moves. The mobile units do not need to be present until the subject is already moving.

The implication for dry cleaning: you are not looking for a single person following you. You are looking for a system — repeated elements, pattern breaks, behavioral anomalies — across an extended route.

### Core Principles of Dry Cleaning

#### Natural Cover for Action

Every move in a dry cleaning route must have a plausible innocent explanation. Behavior that is inexplicable to a surveillance team signals counter-surveillance awareness, which may escalate their response or alert them that the subject is trained.

The discipline is: every stop, turn, pause, or direction change is covered by a natural reason — entering a shop, checking a phone, waiting for someone. The route looks like a normal person going about normal business. The surveillance-detection function is invisible within that normality.

#### The Choke Point

A choke point is a location through which surveillance must pass if it is to maintain coverage. Narrow streets, single entry/exit venues, turnstiles, stairwells — anywhere the surveillance team cannot bypass or parallel-route around you without losing coverage.

Dry cleaning routes are designed around choke points. At each choke point, the practitioner has an opportunity to observe who passes through after them, how quickly, and whether any element repeats from a prior choke point.

The choke point observation requires a cover reason to pause and look back — consulting a phone, examining a window display, waiting at a crossing — without appearing to surveil the surveillance.

#### The Time-Distance Trap

Surveillance teams require time to respond to unexpected direction changes. A subject who turns unexpectedly into a narrow alley or makes a rapid transit connection creates a gap — the team must either rush to maintain coverage (potentially burning themselves) or lose contact.

The time-distance trap is not primarily about losing surveillance. It is about forcing observable behavior: a team that rushes to maintain coverage produces anomalies that are detectable. The goal is to create conditions under which surveillance, if present, must reveal itself.

#### Repeated Elements

A single repeated element — a face, a vehicle, a behavioral pattern — is insufficient confirmation of coverage in isolation. Two repetitions, separated by sufficient time and distance to make coincidence implausible, warrant serious attention. Three repetitions in varied contexts constitute a strong indicator.

The difficulty is calibrating the coincidence threshold accurately. Urban environments produce genuine coincidental repetition. The practitioner must assess: given the population density, the route taken, and the time elapsed, what is the probability of this repetition by chance? Systematic false positives produce operational paralysis; systematic false negatives produce operational exposure.

### Dry Cleaning Techniques

#### The Stop and Linger

Pausing at a display, a notice board, a menu outside a restaurant, or any surface that provides a reflective or angular view of the following environment. The practitioner uses the pause to observe who is also pausing, who passes, and who stops nearby.

The reflective surface variant — using glass, mirrors, or polished surfaces to observe the following environment without turning — is a standard technique but is less reliable than direct observation because angles are distorted and resolution is low. It is useful for initial alerting but not for detailed identification.

The linger should be long enough to allow a surveillance team to settle — to make a decision about whether to pass, stop, or reposition. A team that has been following at distance must respond to your stop, and their response is observable.

#### The About-Face

Turning and walking back the way you came — covered by a natural reason: forgetting something, a phone call that changes your direction, realizing you passed your destination. The about-face forces any following surveillance to either turn with you (potentially exposing themselves) or allow you to walk toward them at close range (providing observation opportunity).

The about-face is most effective in moderate-density pedestrian environments. In very low-density environments, it is too obvious. In very high-density environments, it provides insufficient observation opportunity.

A variant is the **U-turn vehicle technique**: in a vehicle, taking an unexpected U-turn on a road where a following vehicle must either follow visibly or abandon contact.

#### The Single-Entry/Exit Venue

Entering a location with only one practical entrance and exit — a small café, a bookshop, a building lobby — and spending enough time inside to allow any following coverage to make a decision. If surveillance is present, they must either enter behind you (becoming observable at close range), post outside (becoming observable on exit), or lose contact.

The venue entry requires a genuine reason and sufficient time inside to allow the surveillance response to develop. A thirty-second entry and exit provides insufficient time. Ten to fifteen minutes provides enough time for a team to make a decision and commit to it.

#### The Choke Point Transit

Using transit systems — subways, buses, ferries — that create natural choke points. Boarding a train and observing who boards in the same car or the adjacent cars. Exiting at an unexpected stop. Boarding again.

Transit environments are particularly effective for dry cleaning because they impose natural choke points (turnstiles, platform barriers, doors) and because the observation environment is controlled — the population in a subway car is finite and observable.

The technique requires comfort with transit environments and the ability to make boarding/exiting decisions at the last moment without appearing to do so deliberately.

#### The Box Route

A pre-planned route that brings the practitioner back to a point they passed earlier from a different direction, allowing observation of who is in that location twice under different approach conditions. If the same element appears in both passes, coincidence probability drops significantly.

The box route is designed in advance for the specific operational environment, with attention to: where choke points exist, where natural cover-for-action pauses can be placed, and where the practitioner will have observation opportunities looking back along their route.

#### The Stairwell and Elevator

In buildings, using stairs to floors above or below the destination before proceeding — or taking an elevator, noting who is in the lobby when you enter, then observing who arrives at your floor. Building interiors impose observation conditions that outdoor environments do not: the population is smaller, movement is constrained, and repetition is more significant.

#### Time Extension

Extending the duration of a dry cleaning route beyond what surveillance teams plan for. Professional surveillance teams rotate personnel on defined schedules. Extended routes increase the probability of observable handoffs — moments when one surveillance element transfers coverage to another, producing brief behavioral anomalies.

A route that appears to meander — extended by natural-seeming detours — without signaling deliberate delay is difficult to maintain coverage on indefinitely without either revealing the team through accumulated presence or reducing coverage to the point of gaps.

### SDR Design Principles

A well-designed surveillance detection run incorporates the above techniques within a coherent route architecture. The design principles:

**Multiple choke points at varying intervals**: Not evenly spaced, which would be predictable. Varied spacing prevents a team from timing their distance management to your pattern.

**Observation opportunities without obvious counter-surveillance behavior**: Every point at which you observe the following environment should look natural. The route is designed so that natural activities — shopping, eating, transit — provide observation opportunities.

**An abort decision point**: A location before the sensitive act at which you make a final clean/not clean determination. If not confirmed clean at this point, the sensitive act does not proceed. The discipline to abort is as important as the technique to detect.

**Appropriate duration**: Long enough to force observable behavior from surveillance if present; not so long that it signals counter-surveillance awareness through its own unusual length. In professional practice, SDRs for high-sensitivity acts can run several hours. For lower-sensitivity applications, thirty to sixty minutes of conscious technique is a meaningful baseline.

**Route familiarity**: A pre-walked, pre-planned route. Operating a dry cleaning route in an unfamiliar environment degrades performance — you are managing navigation while managing observation, which splits attention. Familiarity with the route allows full attention to go to observation.

### Vehicle Dry Cleaning

Many of the same principles apply to vehicle environments with adaptations.

**Distance and mirrors**: Professional vehicle surveillance maintains significant distance and uses multiple vehicles to avoid mirror-detection. Counter: observing not just immediate following vehicles but vehicles at greater distance that move when you move and stop when you stop.

**The unexpected turn**: Turning without signal indication, or turning at the last moment, forces a following vehicle to either turn visibly or continue past and hand off to another element. A vehicle that turns late or awkwardly at an unexpected turn is a candidate for attention.

**The fuel/parking stop**: Stopping in a location where a following vehicle must either also stop (visibly) or drive past and reposition. Parking lots, petrol stations, and dead-end streets all impose decisions on surveillance vehicles.

**The speed variation**: Varying speed in a way that requires a following vehicle to vary speed correspondingly. A vehicle that mirrors your speed changes — particularly at unusual times — warrants attention.

**Route doubling**: Driving a route that passes the same intersection or landmark twice from different approaches. A vehicle present in both passes is an indicator.

### What You Are Not Looking For

A common error: looking for people who look suspicious. Professional surveillance does not look suspicious. Surveillance personnel are selected and trained to be unremarkable, to behave naturally, and to blend into the environment.

What you are looking for is **system behavior** — patterns that are inconsistent with the coincidental presence of unconnected individuals:

- Repetition across time and space that exceeds coincidence probability
- Behavioral responses to your own movements: pausing when you pause, turning when you turn, speeding up when you do
- Attention directed at you without social justification
- Communication behavior — phone use, earpiece presence, glances between individuals — that suggests coordination
- Incongruence between claimed behavior and actual behavior: someone who has been "reading" a newspaper for forty minutes without turning pages

The observation posture is not paranoid scanning. It is the relaxed, continuous baseline reading trained in Kim's Game — applied to the human environment in motion.

### Indicators and False Positives

Not every repeated element is surveillance. The practitioner must maintain calibrated assessment rather than hypervigilance.

**Genuine coincidence zones**: Areas with high foot traffic, commuter routes, and commercial districts produce genuine repeated elements. The threshold for significance must be higher in these environments.

**Behavioral normalcy**: Most people who pause when you pause are pausing for their own reasons. Most people who turn when you turn are going in the same direction. The threshold for significance is pattern, not instance.

**Confirmation bias risk**: A practitioner in a heightened alertness state will find surveillance indicators because they are looking for them. Every dry cleaning assessment should include an explicit consideration of the null hypothesis: is there a coincidental explanation for what I am observing that is more probable than surveillance?

The discipline is the same as ACH from the SATs module: consider all hypotheses, weight evidence diagnostically, and do not confirm the concerning hypothesis simply because evidence is consistent with it.

### The Abort Discipline

The most important dry cleaning decision is the abort. If, at the final decision point before a sensitive act, you are not confirmed clean, the act does not proceed.

This discipline is documented consistently across open-source intelligence tradecraft literature as one of the hardest to maintain operationally because of sunk cost pressure, time constraints, and the discomfort of having made preparations that are not used. The practitioner who has arranged a covert contact, traveled an extended route, and invested significant time and preparation experiences significant pressure to proceed despite residual uncertainty.

The discipline is pre-committed and unconditional: if not clean, abort. The value of the discipline is precisely its unconditional nature. A threshold that can be argued away under pressure is not a threshold.

This mirrors the ethical threshold mapping covered at the beginning of this syllabus: limits decided in advance, before the pressure that would erode them.

### Integration with the Broader Syllabus

Dry cleaning draws on and integrates several prior modules:

**Kim's Game**: The observational encoding and baseline-reading skills developed there are the perceptual foundation of surveillance detection. Identifying repeated elements requires encoding them accurately on first encounter.

**Structured Analytic Techniques**: The ACH framework applies directly to surveillance assessment — generating multiple hypotheses for observed behavior, weighting diagnostic evidence, and maintaining the null hypothesis as a live option.

**Pattern recognition**: Surveillance detection is fundamentally pattern recognition applied to the human environment in motion — identifying behavioral signatures that exceed coincidental explanation.

**Honor/shame and rapport**: Understanding how professional surveillance personnel think and behave — their operational constraints, their pressure to maintain coverage without burning — provides predictive leverage for anticipating their responses to your movements.

---

**Key Points**

- Dry cleaning is the operational confirmation of surveillance-free status before a sensitive act — it is not optional and not a single technique but a structured process
- You are detecting a system, not an individual — professional surveillance rotates personnel and uses teams; single-element observation is insufficient
- Every dry cleaning move requires natural cover for action — inexplicable behavior signals counter-surveillance awareness and may escalate coverage response
- Choke points force observable decisions from surveillance teams — route design is built around them
- The abort discipline is unconditional: not confirmed clean means the act does not proceed, regardless of sunk cost or time pressure
- Calibrated assessment, not hypervigilance — confirmation bias produces false positives; every indicator requires consideration of the coincidental explanation
- [Inference] Dry cleaning techniques documented in open-source literature represent general principles; specific professional implementations vary by service, environment, and operational context — effectiveness under real professional coverage is not guaranteed by technique knowledge alone
