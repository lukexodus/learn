## Foot and Vehicle Surveillance


### Framing the Domain

Surveillance is the systematic observation of a subject — their movements, contacts, behavior, and patterns — without their awareness. Counter-surveillance is the detection and defeat of surveillance directed at oneself. Both competencies are required: an operative who can conduct surveillance but cannot detect it directed at them is operationally incomplete.

This module addresses the practical mechanics of both, drawing from open-source tradecraft literature, military field manuals, law enforcement surveillance training documents, and academic work on urban movement and attention. Classified operational procedures of any specific service are not covered here and are not the subject of this module.

Foot and vehicle surveillance are treated together because real operations combine them fluidly. A subject may transition from foot to vehicle to foot; the surveillance team must transition with them. Understanding both domains and the transitions between them is necessary for either to be used effectively.

---

### Foundational Principles

Before mechanics, the governing principles that apply across all surveillance and counter-surveillance activity:

**The surveillance paradox**: Effective surveillance requires proximity sufficient to observe; proximity creates exposure. Every move toward better observation increases the risk of detection. Every move away from detection reduces observation quality. The entire craft of surveillance is the management of this tension.

**Pattern is the primary intelligence product**: A single observation is rarely operationally decisive. The value of surveillance is cumulative — building a pattern of movement, behavior, association, and routine that allows prediction and assessment. This means surveillance operations must be sustained and disciplined across time, not episodic.

**The subject's attention is the primary threat**: Detection does not require the subject to identify the surveillance team — it only requires the subject to notice that something is wrong, become alert, and modify their behavior. A subject who is alerted but cannot identify the team has still defeated the operation.

**The surveillance team is not watching the subject — the subject's environment is**: Experienced surveillance practitioners describe their awareness as environmental rather than target-focused. They are watching what is natural in the subject's context and flagging what deviates. This attentional stance is the practical expression of the foundational principle.

### Foot Surveillance

#### The Single-Operator Problem

Single-operator foot surveillance is structurally compromised. A person following another person faces two irreconcilable demands: maintaining visual contact with the subject, and avoiding being noticed. These demands compete directly. The subject who walks at varying speeds, stops suddenly, reverses direction, enters a confined space, or makes a natural observation of their environment will expose a single follower through simple geometry.

Single-operator surveillance is therefore treated as a last resort — used only when no alternative is available, and with explicit acknowledgment that it will likely produce detection in any subject with basic counter-surveillance awareness.

The baseline capability in professional foot surveillance is a **three-person team**. Some sources cite four to six as optimal for sustained urban coverage. The mechanics below describe team-based surveillance unless otherwise noted.

#### Team Roles and Formation

**The eyeball**: The team member who currently has direct visual contact with the subject. This is a rotating role — no single team member maintains the eyeball for extended periods. Duration in the eyeball position increases detection probability. The eyeball communicates the subject's position and direction to the rest of the team and hands off as the situation requires.

**The backup**: The second team member positioned to assume the eyeball at handoff, typically one block behind or on a parallel route. The backup maintains situational awareness of the environment ahead and anticipates handoff opportunities.

**The floater / third**: The third team member who operates more freely — taking parallel routes, getting ahead of the subject to establish a forward position, or recovering coverage when the formation is disrupted by the subject's route choices. In a larger team, additional members increase flexibility and resilience.

**Key Points**

The team communicates continuously — through earpieces and concealed microphones in professional contexts, or through prearranged signals and phone communication in lower-resource contexts. Communication discipline is critical: over-communication clutters the channel; under-communication breaks coverage.

#### The Follow: Basic Mechanics

**Distance and coverage**: The appropriate following distance depends on the environment. In a dense urban environment with high pedestrian traffic, the eyeball can maintain closer proximity because they are less salient. In a sparse environment — a quiet residential street, a park, a rural road — greater distance is required, but greater distance increases the risk of losing the subject at turns. The eyeball must continuously recalibrate to the environment.

**Side-street advantage**: When the subject is moving along a predictable route, team members on parallel streets — one block over in either direction — can maintain pace without being on the same street as the subject. This allows the eyeball to fall back or break contact while coverage is maintained.

**The natural stop**: When a subject stops — to look in a shop window, check their phone, tie a shoe, look at a map — an operator behind them must do something natural. The worst response is stopping suddenly in the middle of a pavement with no apparent purpose. Practiced operators have a repertoire of natural pauses: checking their phone, appearing to consult a map or address, entering a nearby shop, studying a menu. The naturalness of the pause is what protects the position.

**Chokepoints**: Any location where the subject must pass through a narrow or predictable space — a doorway, a turnstile, a bridge, a building lobby — is a handoff opportunity. The eyeball can break contact before the chokepoint; another team member can pick up on the other side. This prevents the same face appearing in the subject's field of view on both sides.

**Acceleration matching**: When the subject accelerates, the team must decide whether to match acceleration or risk a gap. Matching acceleration visibly is itself a surveillance indicator — natural pedestrians do not suddenly increase pace when a stranger ahead of them does. The correct response is usually to allow a gap to develop, rely on forward-positioned team members to maintain coverage, and close the gap when the subject settles.

**Loss and recovery**: The subject will sometimes be lost — in crowds, in complex indoor environments, at unexpected route changes. Loss is a normal event in surveillance operations; it is managed, not catastrophized. Recovery options: move quickly to the last known position, predict the most likely route and get ahead of it, use team members already positioned on alternate routes, and establish surveillance on the subject's likely destination if known.

#### The Mobile Box

A more structured team formation for foot surveillance is the **mobile box** — team members positioned ahead of, behind, and to both sides of the subject, maintaining a loose perimeter that moves with the subject. This provides coverage regardless of which direction the subject moves or turns, reduces the duration of any individual member's time in the eyeball position, and allows any single member to break contact without losing coverage.

The mobile box requires more team members (typically four to six), clear communication, and practiced coordination. Its advantage is resilience: it is not disrupted by the subject's route choices in the way that a simple linear follow is.

#### Indoor Surveillance

Indoor environments — shopping centers, transit stations, museums, airports — present specific challenges:

**Chokepoint concentration**: Entrances, exits, escalators, and lifts are natural chokepoints where the subject will be in confined proximity to the team. The same face at multiple chokepoints is a strong surveillance indicator.

**Surveillance cameras**: Modern indoor environments have dense camera coverage. An operator who is behaving naturally does not need to manage camera exposure; an operator who is not behaving naturally will be recorded regardless.

**Store entry**: If a subject enters a shop, following them in creates an immediate confined-space problem. One operator may follow; others should cover the exits. The operator inside should have a clear natural reason to be in that shop.

**Dwell time mismatch**: In a shopping environment, a subject who moves purposefully without shopping while an operator matches their pace without shopping is a visible anomaly. Operators in shopping environments should interact with the environment — handle merchandise, examine products — even while maintaining coverage.

### Vehicle Surveillance

#### The Vehicle Advantage and Its Limits

Vehicle surveillance extends the distances over which coverage can be maintained, allows faster response to subject movement, and provides a more natural presence in many environments than a pedestrian team. Its limits are the inverse: vehicles are constrained to roads, are identifiable by make, model, color, and registration, and are difficult to use inconspicuously in low-traffic environments or in stops.

A key asymmetry: a vehicle that the subject notices once may be unremarkable; a vehicle the subject notices twice in different contexts is a strong surveillance indicator; a vehicle noticed three times across different environments is conclusive. Vehicle surveillance teams must manage cumulative exposure with the same discipline as foot teams — and with fewer substitution options, since vehicles cannot change their appearance the way operators can.

#### Team Composition and Formation

Professional vehicle surveillance uses a minimum of **three vehicles**, with additional assets improving flexibility. The team roles mirror foot surveillance:

**The trigger**: The vehicle with current visual contact with the subject. In some formulations, the trigger is not a mobile follow vehicle — it is a static asset near the subject's origin point (home, office) that observes departure and initiates the follow by communicating direction and timing.

**The eyeball**: The vehicle in the follow position behind the subject. As with foot surveillance, this role rotates.

**The backup and flanks**: Vehicles on parallel routes, positioned ahead of the subject, or in reserve to take the eyeball when the current eyeball must break.

**The forward position**: A vehicle that moves ahead of the subject and monitors from the front. A subject checking their mirrors for a following vehicle will not see a forward surveillance vehicle. This is particularly useful when the subject's route is known or predictable.

#### Vehicle Follow Mechanics

**Following distance**: Appropriate following distance in vehicle surveillance depends on traffic density, road type, and subject behavior. In dense urban traffic, closer follow is necessary to avoid being separated by signals. On open roads or motorways, much greater distance is possible and necessary. The team should be spread across the follow — one vehicle immediately behind, one further back, one on a parallel route where possible.

**Traffic light discipline**: Traffic signals are the primary mechanism by which a simple vehicle follow is broken or exposed. If the subject passes a signal that turns red before the eyeball vehicle can follow, the subject has a gap and the team must predict and recover. Conversely, if the eyeball vehicle runs a red light to maintain contact, they have violated a traffic rule that is itself a conspicuous behavior.

Professional teams manage signal risk by positioning a second vehicle ahead of the signal, or by accepting the gap and recovering through prediction. Running red lights to maintain surveillance is generally not an acceptable operational practice because the conspicuous behavior it produces outweighs the coverage benefit.

**Overtaking and handoff**: When the eyeball has been in the follow position for too long, they must hand off. The backup vehicle accelerates to take the eyeball; the previous eyeball breaks off and repositions — dropping back, taking a different route, or getting ahead. This rotation must be smooth and timed to intersections or environmental features that provide natural cover for the transition.

**Roundabouts and complex junctions**: These are high-detection-risk environments because the subject naturally scans around them and may observe following vehicles clearly. Teams should distribute vehicles across multiple exits of a roundabout before the subject arrives, reducing the risk that a specific vehicle is observed in the follow position.

**U-turns and reversals**: A subject who executes a sudden U-turn is either navigating or checking for surveillance. A single vehicle that follows the U-turn is immediately conspicuous. The correct response is for the eyeball to continue on their original course — not following the U-turn — while another team member who was on the opposite route assumes coverage. This requires prior communication and positioning.

**Motorway surveillance**: High-speed road surveillance offers the advantage of greater natural following distance without losing contact. It carries the disadvantage that exits occur at longer intervals, exit choices are binary, and a subject who suddenly exits without signaling can lose a closely positioned team. Best practice on motorways is to position one vehicle well ahead (potentially having overtaken the subject earlier) to provide coverage if the subject accelerates or exits unexpectedly.

#### Static and Trigger Surveillance

Not all vehicle surveillance involves mobile following. **Static surveillance** — positioning observation assets near a fixed location to observe a subject's arrival, departure, and associates — is often more productive and less risk-prone than mobile following.

A **trigger** is an observation asset — vehicle, foot, or technical — positioned near a location the subject is expected to visit, whose sole function is to observe departure and communicate initiation of movement. The trigger does not follow — it only detects and reports. This separation of functions means the trigger asset can be optimally positioned for observation rather than follow.

Static and trigger assets can be parked vehicles with observers, positions in nearby buildings, or technical sensors. The key requirement is that they are positioned with a natural reason to be there — a vehicle parked on a residential street requires a different explanation than one parked on a commercial street.

### Surveillance Detection Routes

The **Surveillance Detection Route (SDR)** is the primary counter-surveillance tool. It is a deliberately designed route through an environment whose characteristics create conditions under which surveillance coverage becomes difficult to maintain without exposure.

The SDR is not a random walk — it is an engineered sequence of movements whose properties are chosen specifically to stress and reveal surveillance operations. It is used by a subject who suspects surveillance, by an operative before a sensitive meeting, or as standard OPSEC practice in certain environments.

#### SDR Design Principles

**Chokepoints with observation opportunities**: The SDR should include points where the subject can naturally observe anyone who has followed them through — a turn onto a narrow street, entry to a specific building, a pause at a café from which the approach route is visible. The subject is not looking for specific individuals — they are looking for anomalies and patterns.

**Direction changes**: Multiple changes of direction, particularly in sequences that are not efficient for the stated destination, stress a following team's formation. A linear route requires the team to simply follow; a route with four or five turns in different directions requires continuous repositioning. Errors in that repositioning produce observation opportunities.

**Variable pace**: Alternating between fast and slow movement, with pauses, disrupts the team's ability to maintain optimal following distance without being visible in the pauses.

**Chokepoints with single-entry**: Entering a building with a single entrance and exit means any surveillance team member who follows must enter the same chokepoint. The subject can observe who enters shortly after them.

**Transit transitions**: Moving from foot to transit (underground, bus) and back to foot forces a surveillance team to either maintain personnel on transit or lose coverage at the transition. If the subject watches who boards the same vehicle, particularly those who boarded immediately after them, and then watches who exits when they exit, transit creates natural observation windows.

**Doubling back**: Reversing direction — turning around and walking back along the route just traveled — creates an immediate and unambiguous observation opportunity. Anyone in the follow who does not immediately have a natural reason to also reverse direction is exposed. This is a high-risk technique because it is itself a surveillance indicator to any sophisticated team; it is best used within a longer SDR where it reads as a natural behavior (forgetting something, checking a window again) rather than as a deliberate counter-surveillance act.

#### SDR Execution

The subject executing an SDR must behave naturally throughout. An SDR executed with visible anxiety, excessive environment scanning, or behaviors that are consistent with surveillance awareness is itself a surveillance indicator. The goal is to create conditions for observation without revealing that observation is being conducted.

The behavioral discipline required is specific: the subject uses **peripheral vision and reflective surfaces** — shop windows, mirrors, glass doors, the backs of polished vehicles — rather than direct scanning. Direct looking behind oneself is a natural behavior in limited contexts (checking for traffic before crossing); outside those contexts it signals counter-surveillance awareness.

**Timing observations**: The subject notes time elapsed between their movement and any repeated individuals or vehicles. A person who appears in the same area fifteen minutes after the subject arrived, and then reappears near the subject at a later point, has been in the area longer than casual presence explains.

**Appearance variation recognition**: Surveillance teams vary their appearance between exposures — changing a jacket, removing or adding a hat, altering their gait. A subject trained in appearance variation recognition attends to features that are harder to change rapidly: height, build, characteristic gait, facial structure, and footwear. Footwear in particular is frequently cited in surveillance training as a reliable identifier because it is rarely changed during a follow.

### Technical Surveillance

While this module focuses on human surveillance, technical surveillance devices interact with foot and vehicle operations in ways that require awareness.

**Vehicle tracking devices**: Magnetic GPS trackers can be placed on vehicles in seconds. They eliminate the primary difficulty of vehicle surveillance — maintaining visual contact — but they do not provide observation of what the subject does at a destination. They are detectable through physical inspection. Periodic sweeps of one's vehicle — particularly after it has been in an unsecured location — are a basic counter-measure.

**Mobile phone positioning**: A subject's mobile phone continuously reports its location to the network and, under certain conditions, to applications on the device. This provides surveillance capability without any human follow. Counter-measures include physical separation from the device for sensitive activity, use of devices not associated with the subject's identity, and awareness of the data collection behavior of installed applications.

**CCTV and fixed camera infrastructure**: In urban environments with dense camera coverage, a subject's movements are recorded regardless of whether a human team is present. This is not directly countered by the subject's behavior in the moment — it is managed through route planning (avoiding known camera-dense environments where possible) and through awareness that camera-captured data may be reviewed after the fact.

### Surveillance Indicators: Recognition Framework

Whether conducting surveillance awareness from the subject's perspective or assessing one's own team from the operational perspective, the following categories of indicators have diagnostic value:

#### Individual Indicators (Possible surveillance team member)

- **Repeated sighting across time and location**: The same individual appears in the subject's environment at two or more separate times and locations without a natural explanation
- **Interest without purpose**: An individual who is present in an environment but whose presence serves no apparent purpose — not shopping, not waiting with visible patience, not engaged with a companion
- **Equipment anomalies**: Earpieces (single, small, flesh-tone), jackets worn in warm weather (concealing equipment or to allow rapid appearance change), backpacks that seem underutilized
- **Communication behavior**: Quiet, low-profile speaking — into a collar, a wrist, or a phone held close — while observing the subject's direction
- **Behavioral mimicry of the subject's stops**: Stopping when the subject stops, without a natural reason visible for that stop
- **Gait and pace matching**: Maintaining the same pace as the subject over multiple blocks in a way that is inconsistent with the apparent destination

#### Vehicle Indicators

- **Same vehicle in different contexts**: A vehicle that appears near the subject's origin and then again near their destination, or in multiple distinct locations across a route
- **Vehicles that do not park**: A vehicle that circles or passes the same area multiple times without stopping in a way that explains the passes
- **Occupancy patterns**: Parked vehicles in residential areas with occupants who have been there for extended periods without apparent purpose
- **Following behavior at junctions**: A vehicle that consistently makes the same turns as the subject, particularly at junctions where the subject's choice was non-obvious

#### Environmental Indicators

- **Coordinated presence**: Multiple individuals who do not appear to know each other but whose movements are correlated — all moving when the subject moves, all pausing when the subject pauses
- **Absence where expected**: A location or route that normally has certain ambient human traffic, but where that traffic is unexpectedly absent — potentially because it has been cleared or is being managed
- **New presence in familiar environments**: In environments the subject uses regularly, the appearance of new individuals or vehicles that do not fit the normal pattern of that environment

### The Surveillance-Counter-Surveillance Dynamic

Surveillance and counter-surveillance are not sequential activities — they occur simultaneously and interact. A competent surveillance team is conducting its own counter-surveillance: watching for indicators that the subject has detected them, managing their own behavior to avoid triggering counter-surveillance awareness, and adjusting their operation when they detect that the subject's behavior has changed.

The dynamic has a game-theoretic structure: each side is trying to act naturally while monitoring for unnaturalness in the other. The equilibrium is resolved by:

**Quality of training**: A team with more sophisticated surveillance training can maintain coverage closer to the subject's awareness threshold without triggering it. A subject with more sophisticated counter-surveillance training has a lower detection threshold.

**Resource asymmetry**: A large, well-resourced team can absorb more of the subject's counter-surveillance measures without exposure than a small team. Single-operator surveillance against a counter-surveillance-aware subject is structurally unwinnable.

**Environmental advantage**: The party who has better environmental knowledge — who knows the exits, the camera positions, the natural traffic patterns, the building layouts — has a structural advantage. This favors the subject in environments they know well and the surveillance team in environments they have pre-surveyed.

**Patience**: Rushed surveillance produces errors. A team under time pressure to obtain a specific observation is more likely to close distance, hold the eyeball too long, or make positioning errors that expose the operation. A subject under time pressure to reach their destination may not execute a full SDR. Time pressure disadvantages both sides, but differently.

### Urban Movement and Pattern Analysis

Beyond the tactical mechanics of following and detection, surveillance serves an analytical function: the accumulation of data about a subject's patterns, associations, and routine. This analytical output is often more valuable than any single observation.

**Pattern of life mapping**: Systematic surveillance builds a picture of when the subject moves, where they go, who they meet, and what behaviors accompany different destinations. Deviations from the established pattern are operationally significant: they indicate either a new routine, a specific event, or counter-surveillance awareness.

**Association mapping**: Identifying who the subject contacts, in what contexts, and with what regularity. Contacts who appear infrequently and outside the subject's normal pattern are the most analytically interesting.

**Behavioral tells**: Some subjects exhibit specific behaviors in proximity to sensitive activity — changes in route, extended SDR-like behavior, use of different transport modes, changes in phone behavior (powering down, leaving behind, switching devices). These tells are themselves surveillance indicators: they signal that the subject is approaching something they regard as sensitive, even if the specific activity has not been observed.

**Dead time vs. active time**: A subject's day includes periods of low analytical value (routine commuting, known regular appointments) and periods of high value (unexplained meetings, deviations from routine, contacts with unknown individuals). Surveillance resources should be allocated toward high-value periods rather than distributed uniformly across the subject's movement.

### Practical Degraded Conditions

Real surveillance and counter-surveillance operations occur under degraded conditions: limited resources, incomplete team training, environmental unpredictability, and time pressure. The following principles apply when operating below the theoretical optimum:

**Prioritize coverage at nodes over coverage in transit**: If full route coverage is not possible, concentrate resources at origin, destination, and key intermediate locations. Transit coverage is the hardest to maintain and the most resource-intensive; node coverage is more economical.

**Accept gaps in coverage over exposure**: A gap in surveillance is recoverable; exposure is not. When the choice is between pushing coverage and risking detection, break off.

**Use the environment as a force multiplier**: Natural environmental features — cafés, shops, parks, transport infrastructure — can substitute for team members when used intelligently. A team member positioned in a café can observe a block of street more naturally than a team member standing on that block.

**Simplify communications discipline**: In resource-limited contexts, simplified communications protocols — agreed prior to the operation — are more reliable than improvisational communication. Misunderstandings in surveillance communication produce coverage gaps and positioning errors.

**Conclusion**

Foot and vehicle surveillance are complementary disciplines governed by a single underlying tension: the need for proximity sufficient to observe, against the need for distance sufficient to avoid detection. The resolution of this tension is team coordination, environmental awareness, and behavioral naturalness — which are skills acquired through practice rather than conceptual understanding alone. Counter-surveillance competency — SDR design, indicator recognition, reflective observation discipline — is built on the same foundation: understanding how surveillance operates well enough to recognize and defeat it. The most important single principle across both domains is that the subject's level of alert is the primary threat variable, and everything in the craft of surveillance is directed at managing it.
