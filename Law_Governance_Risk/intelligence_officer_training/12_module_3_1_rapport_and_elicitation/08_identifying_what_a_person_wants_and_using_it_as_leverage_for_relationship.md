## Identifying What a Person Wants and Using It as Leverage for Relationship


---

### Framing the Objective

The word "leverage" requires precise definition at the outset. In the context of relationship building for intelligence or operational purposes, leverage does not mean coercion — applying pressure through threat or exposure. It means the identification of what a person values, needs, or desires, and the calibration of your behavior to make yourself relevant to those things. The distinction is not merely ethical; it is practical. Coercive leverage produces compliance. Value-aligned leverage produces cooperation, which is more durable, more productive, and less operationally fragile.

The practitioner's goal is to become genuinely useful to the target of relationship development — useful in ways the target recognizes and values. This requires accurate identification of what the target actually wants, which is rarely identical to what they say they want, what they are supposed to want given their role, or what they wanted six months ago.

This module sits at the intersection of HUMINT tradecraft and applied psychology. It draws on motivational psychology, social exchange theory, needs-based frameworks, and elicitation technique. The output is not a manipulation script — it is a relationship architecture built on accurate modeling of another person.

---

### The Distinction Between Stated, Revealed, and Latent Wants

The first analytical task is distinguishing between three categories of want that are rarely identical.

#### Stated Wants

What a person explicitly says they want. Stated wants are the surface layer and are the least reliable guide to actual motivation. They are shaped by social desirability — people say what they believe they are supposed to want given their role, their audience, and the norms of the context. A bureaucrat says they want efficiency. A soldier says they want to serve their country. A scientist says they want to advance human knowledge. These statements are not necessarily false, but they are incomplete and often strategically presented.

Stated wants also shift with audience. The same person will articulate different wants to a superior, a peer, and a subordinate. The practitioner who is building a relationship occupies a specific social position relative to the target, and the stated wants presented to that position are filtered through that relationship dynamic.

#### Revealed Wants

What a person's behavior demonstrates they actually pursue, independent of what they say. Revealed wants are read from behavioral evidence: how they spend discretionary time and money, which relationships they invest in, what topics animate their conversation, what risks they voluntarily take, what they avoid. Behavioral data is more reliable than verbal data because behavior has costs — it represents actual resource allocation rather than social performance.

Revealed wants sometimes contradict stated wants sharply. A person who states a commitment to family but consistently works evenings and weekends has revealed a different priority structure. A person who states indifference to status but responds with visible energy when their title is used correctly has revealed that status matters. These contradictions are not necessarily signs of dishonesty — people frequently have incomplete self-knowledge about their own motivational structure.

#### Latent Wants

Wants the person has not yet articulated even to themselves — desires, needs, or fears that are present in their motivational structure but have not been brought to conscious awareness or have been actively suppressed. Latent wants are the highest-value category because they represent needs that no one else is currently addressing. The practitioner who identifies and responds to a latent want before anyone else has is providing something the target did not know they needed from a relationship.

Latent wants are accessible through observation of emotional responses — what produces visible pleasure, visible discomfort, visible energy, visible deflation — and through the pattern of what a person returns to across multiple conversations and contexts.

### Motivational Frameworks as Diagnostic Tools

No single motivational framework is sufficient. Each illuminates a different layer of the motivational structure. The practitioner should use multiple frameworks as parallel lenses rather than committing to one.

#### Maslow's Hierarchy — With Operational Caveats

Maslow's hierarchy — physiological needs, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization — provides a useful first-pass framework. The operational insight is not the hierarchy itself but the principle that unmet lower-order needs dominate motivational attention. A person under financial stress is not primarily motivated by esteem or self-actualization, regardless of what they say. A person whose physical safety is threatened is not primarily motivated by belonging.

[Unverified: Maslow's hierarchy as a rigid sequential model has been challenged by subsequent empirical research. The specific claim that lower needs must be satisfied before higher needs become motivationally active is not consistently supported. Treat it as a heuristic, not a law.] The more defensible version of the insight is that acute unmet needs in any category dominate attention and motivational energy.

Operationally: identify whether the target has acute unmet needs in any category. Acute need creates leverage potential — not through exploitation of the need, but through becoming associated with its resolution.

#### Self-Determination Theory

Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan) identifies three core psychological needs: autonomy (the need to be the author of one's own actions), competence (the need to feel effective and capable), and relatedness (the need for genuine connection with others). These are presented as universal — present across cultures in varying expressions.

The operational value of this framework is that it identifies needs that are frequently unmet in organizational and institutional contexts. Bureaucratic structures routinely undermine autonomy. Competitive or hierarchical environments routinely threaten competence. High-mobility professional lives routinely undermine relatedness.

A practitioner who provides authentic experiences of autonomy, competence affirmation, and genuine connection to a target embedded in a bureaucratic or institutional environment is providing something of significant motivational value that the institutional context systematically withholds.

#### Achievement, Affiliation, and Power — McClelland

McClelland's three motivational needs — need for achievement, need for affiliation, and need for power — describe motivational orientation at the personality level rather than the situational level. They are relatively stable traits that shape how a person approaches relationships and work.

**High achievement motivation:** The person is primarily driven by the accomplishment of difficult tasks. They respond to challenge, to feedback on performance, to opportunities to demonstrate competence. They are less motivated by relationship for its own sake and more motivated by relationship as a vehicle for getting things done. They respond to being taken seriously as a competent actor.

**High affiliation motivation:** The person is primarily driven by the quality of relationships. They respond to warmth, inclusion, and genuine connection. They are motivated by being liked and by belonging. They are sensitive to social rejection and highly responsive to expressions of genuine interest in them as people. They are less motivated by challenge and more motivated by harmony.

**High power motivation:** The person is primarily driven by influence, impact, and control. They respond to opportunities to shape outcomes and to be recognized as influential. They are motivated by the extension of their sphere of effect. They may present as affiliation-motivated — charming, socially engaged — but the underlying drive is toward influence rather than connection.

Identifying which orientation dominates requires behavioral observation rather than self-report. Most people with high power motivation will not describe themselves as power-motivated — they will describe themselves as wanting to make a difference or wanting to lead. The behavioral signature is different: they consistently maneuver for influence, track who has access to decision-makers, and respond with energy to opportunities that extend their reach.

#### MICE Framework

The MICE framework — Money, Ideology, Coercion, Ego — is a traditional intelligence community tool for categorizing the motivational basis of agent recruitment. It is reductive but operationally useful as a starting taxonomy.

**Money:** Financial need or desire is the most straightforward motivational lever. It is also the most fragile — a relationship built primarily on financial exchange is vulnerable to outbidding and does not produce ideological loyalty. Financial motivation is most operationally relevant when there is acute need rather than chronic desire.

**Ideology:** Shared beliefs, values, or grievances. A person who believes that their organization, government, or institution is acting wrongly, that a cause is just, or that a particular outcome matters morally can be motivated through alignment with those beliefs. Ideological motivation is the most durable — it survives changes in circumstance that would dissolve financially-motivated relationships.

**Coercion:** Threat of exposure or harm. Excluded from this module's scope as a relationship-building mechanism. Coercive leverage produces compliance, not cooperation, and creates an adversarial relationship that is operationally fragile and ethically problematic.

**Ego:** Pride, vanity, desire for recognition, resentment at being undervalued. Ego is among the most universally available motivational levers because institutional environments routinely fail to provide adequate recognition to people who believe they deserve it. The person who feels overlooked, undervalued, or insufficiently recognized is highly responsive to a relationship that provides that recognition authentically.

A fifth category is often added: **MICE+S**, where S is Sex or romantic attachment. This is noted for completeness but is excluded from operational development in this module.

### Elicitation Methods for Want Identification

Want identification is not a single-event assessment. It is a continuous process conducted across multiple interactions through several parallel channels.

#### Active Listening for Motivational Signal

In ordinary conversation, people continuously signal what they care about through what they return to, what animates their affect, and what deflates it. The practitioner who is genuinely attending — not preparing their next contribution while the target speaks — will extract motivational data from every conversation.

Specific listening targets:

- **Topics of spontaneous elaboration.** When a person expands beyond what the question or context required, the direction of elaboration is motivationally significant. What they add, when they didn't have to, is what they care about.
- **Affect shifts.** Changes in vocal energy, facial expression, posture, and pace that accompany specific topics. Both positive and negative affect shifts are diagnostic — what deflates a person is as informative as what animates them.
- **Return patterns.** Topics a person returns to across multiple interactions without being prompted. Repetition indicates unresolved preoccupation — either an unmet need or an ongoing concern.
- **Complaint content.** What a person complains about reveals what they value. Complaints about being micromanaged reveal a need for autonomy. Complaints about credit not being given reveal esteem needs. Complaints about being excluded from decisions reveal power motivation. The content of grievances is a direct read on what matters.

#### The Elicitation of Aspiration

A productive elicitation line that is socially natural and produces high-value motivational data: ask about what the person is working toward, what they would do with more time or resources, what they wish their work allowed them to do. These questions access the aspiration layer — the gap between current state and desired state — which is precisely where motivational energy concentrates.

Framing that produces authentic response:

- "What would you focus on if the current constraints weren't there?"
- "What's the part of your work that you find most meaningful?"
- "What would you want to be doing in five years that you're not doing now?"

These are socially unremarkable questions that produce rich motivational data when the practitioner genuinely attends to the response.

#### The Elicitation of Grievance

Grievance is among the most motivationally dense categories because it combines unmet need with emotional charge. A person with a genuine grievance — against an institution, a superior, a colleague, a system — is motivated to be heard and validated. The practitioner who creates the conditions for authentic grievance expression, and who responds with genuine rather than performative understanding, is providing something of high relational value.

Grievance elicitation must be approached carefully. Directly soliciting complaint ("What bothers you about your organization?") is too blunt in most contexts and triggers social defenses. The approach is indirect: create conditions of safety and genuine interest, allow the conversation to move at the target's pace, and follow threads that the target themselves opens. When a person makes a qualifying statement — "not that I'm complaining, but..." or "I probably shouldn't say this, but..." — they are signaling that a grievance is present and testing whether the practitioner is a safe recipient.

#### Third-Party Elicitation

Information about a target's wants, needs, and motivations can be elicited from people in the target's social environment — colleagues, subordinates, family members in casual contact, social acquaintances. These third parties frequently have detailed observational knowledge of the target's motivational structure and will share it in contexts of natural conversation.

This must be conducted without signaling that the target is a subject of specific interest. The third-party conversation should feel like natural social exchange in which information about a mutual acquaintance arises organically. Questions that produce useful data: "What's [target] working on these days?", "How is [target] finding [current situation]?", "What does [target] do when they're not working?"

#### Behavioral Observation

Independent of conversation, behavioral observation produces motivational data. How does the target spend discretionary time and money? What is visible in their environment — books, objects, images, symbols? What relationships do they maintain and invest in? What do they visibly enjoy and what do they avoid?

Environmental observation is particularly productive in home or office settings. The objects a person chooses to display — awards, photographs, cultural objects, books — are self-presentations that reveal what they want others to know they value. The discrepancy between displayed objects and actual behavior is itself informative.

### Mapping the Want Structure

After sufficient observation across multiple channels, the practitioner constructs a want map for the target — a structured model of their motivational architecture.

The want map has four layers:

**Surface wants:** Stated desires and socially acceptable motivations. What the target would say they want in most contexts.

**Functional wants:** Revealed behavioral priorities. What the target actually pursues with their resources.

**Core needs:** The underlying psychological needs that the functional wants are serving. A person who pursues status may be serving an underlying need for security. A person who pursues achievement may be serving an underlying need for validation. The core need is the level at which leverage is most durable.

**Latent wants:** Unrecognized or suppressed desires that have not been addressed by any current relationship. The highest-value category.

The want map is a living document — it requires update as new data arrives and as the relationship develops. A want map that was accurate six months ago may not be accurate today; circumstances change motivational salience.

### Translating the Want Map into Relationship Architecture

Want identification is only instrumentally valuable if it informs relationship behavior. The translation from map to behavior operates through several mechanisms.

#### Genuine Relevance

The most fundamental principle: become genuinely useful or meaningful to the target's actual wants. This is not performance — it is calibration. The practitioner who has accurately identified that a target's core need is for their competence to be recognized and taken seriously should genuinely take their competence seriously, engage with their ideas substantively, and create contexts in which their expertise is visible and valued.

Performed relevance — pretending to care about what the target cares about — is detectable and produces the opposite of the intended effect when detected. The practitioner must either genuinely engage with the target's interests or create situations in which the target's interests and the practitioner's genuine engagement overlap.

#### Timing and Salience

Want-based leverage is most available when the want is currently salient — when the need is acute, when a relevant aspiration has recently been activated, or when a grievance is currently live. The same offer of relevance that produces a strong response at a moment of high salience may produce a minimal response at a moment of low salience.

The practitioner who has maintained continuous contact and accurate motivational modeling will recognize moments of high salience and respond to them specifically. This requires patience — the motivational landscape of a target shifts across time, and waiting for a salience window is often more effective than attempting to create one artificially.

#### Reciprocity Activation

Social exchange theory (Blau, Homans, Cialdini) documents the near-universal tendency toward reciprocity — when a person receives something of value, they experience motivation to return value. This tendency is not primarily conscious or calculated; it operates as an automatic social mechanism.

The practitioner who provides genuine value — information, connection, recognition, assistance, validation — activates reciprocity motivation in the target. The target is then more likely to provide value in return, and more likely to maintain and invest in the relationship.

Reciprocity is most effectively activated through:

- **Unrequested provision.** Value provided without being asked for is more activating than value provided in response to a request. The unsolicited favor carries higher relational weight than the solicited one.
- **Personalization.** Value calibrated specifically to the target's identified wants is more activating than generic value. A generically helpful person is less relationally compelling than a person who specifically addresses what you, specifically, need.
- **Appropriate scale.** Reciprocity norms are violated by both under-provision (providing too little to activate the norm) and over-provision (providing so much that the target cannot reciprocate and feels indebted in a way that is uncomfortable). Calibrating provision to a level the target can reciprocate at maintains the relational balance that sustains ongoing exchange.

#### Creating Dependency Without Creating Obligation

The operational goal is a relationship in which the target experiences the practitioner as genuinely valuable and difficult to replace — not one in which the target feels obligated, manipulated, or trapped. These are structurally different relationship states with very different operational characteristics.

Dependency is created by being uniquely relevant to something the target genuinely values. The practitioner who is the only person in the target's network who engages seriously with a specific interest, who has access to a specific resource, or who provides a specific type of validation has created a unique relevance that makes the relationship difficult to substitute.

Obligation — the feeling of being in debt or manipulated — is created by provision that is too large, too one-sided, or that comes with visible expectations attached. This is the failure mode of heavy-handed rapport building. The target who feels obligated may comply in the short term but will seek to exit the relationship as soon as the obligation feels discharged.

### Specific Motivational Levers and Their Application

#### Recognition and Validation

Among the most widely available and under-exploited leverage categories. Most people in professional and institutional contexts receive insufficient recognition relative to their self-assessed contribution. The practitioner who provides accurate, specific, and credible recognition of a target's genuine capabilities or contributions is providing something of genuine value.

The key qualifiers: **accurate, specific, and credible.** Generic flattery ("you're amazing at your job") is detectable as social performance and carries low weight. Specific recognition of a specific observed capability or contribution ("the way you managed that situation showed a level of judgment I haven't seen many people demonstrate in that context") is credible because it demonstrates that the practitioner genuinely observed and assessed what happened.

Validation of a suppressed or unofficial view — "the thing you're actually doing here that nobody's naming is X" — is particularly high-value because it provides recognition of something the target wants recognized but has not been able to get recognized through normal channels.

#### Access and Information

Information that is relevant to the target's wants — professional information, social information, access to networks or people — is a direct provision of value. The practitioner who can provide access to something the target values becomes associated with the provision of that access.

This requires that the practitioner have genuine access to offer. Fabricated or overstated access is detectable and produces rapid credibility damage. The practitioner's own network and knowledge must be real and must be genuinely deployed in the target's interest.

#### The Experience of Being Understood

Separate from recognition of competence, the experience of being genuinely understood — of having one's perspective, situation, or feeling accurately reflected back — is a powerful relational experience that most people encounter rarely. Genuine understanding requires accurate motivational modeling and the willingness to reflect that model back to the target in a way that lands as true.

This is distinct from empathic performance. The practitioner who says "that sounds difficult" in response to a described experience is performing empathy. The practitioner who says "it sounds like the thing that's actually frustrating you isn't the situation itself but the fact that nobody in that system is taking seriously what you already know about it" is demonstrating understanding. The latter is qualitatively different in its relational impact.

#### Shared Adversity and In-Group Formation

Shared experience of adversity — even minor — produces in-group bonding. The practitioner who navigates a difficult or ambiguous situation alongside the target creates a shared reference that strengthens the relational bond. This is the structural basis of military unit cohesion and the reason hazing and difficult shared experiences produce strong group identification.

In relationship development, this does not require manufacturing adversity. It requires recognizing and naming shared challenges when they exist, positioning the practitioner and target as sharing a perspective or situation that differentiates them from others.

### Sequencing the Relationship

Want-based relationship architecture follows a sequence. Attempting to use leverage before sufficient motivational modeling has been conducted, or before sufficient relational capital has been built, produces poor results.

**Phase 1 — Observation and modeling.** Prioritize data collection over relationship advancement. Build the want map before attempting to be relevant to what it contains. This phase should feel, to the target, like natural social interaction. The practitioner is asking questions, listening well, and being generally engaging — not pressing toward a specific relationship goal.

**Phase 2 — Genuine relevance provision.** Begin providing genuine value calibrated to the identified wants. This phase should produce reciprocity activation and increasing voluntary engagement from the target. The target begins to initiate contact, to bring the practitioner into their concerns, and to associate the practitioner with positive experiences.

**Phase 3 — Deepening and consolidation.** The relationship has sufficient depth and trust that the target is willing to extend it into more sensitive or significant territory. The practitioner can be more direct about the nature of the relationship and the mutual value it provides. Dependency and genuine loyalty begin to develop.

**Phase 4 — Maintenance.** Active relationships require maintenance. The motivational landscape continues to shift, the want map requires updating, and provision of value must continue. Relationships built on want-based leverage that ceases to be provided will atrophy.

### Failure Modes

**Premature deployment.** Attempting to use identified wants as leverage before sufficient relational capital has been built. The target experiences the approach as transparent and transactional.

**Want map inaccuracy.** Acting on a want map that is wrong — built on stated rather than revealed wants, or on outdated data. Provision calibrated to the wrong want misses and can signal that the practitioner does not actually understand the target.

**Visible instrumentality.** Any signal that the practitioner's interest in the target is primarily instrumental — that they are valued as a means rather than as a person — is highly aversive and will produce relationship withdrawal. The practitioner must genuinely engage with the target as a person, not merely perform engagement while pursuing an instrumental agenda.

**Over-provision.** Providing value at a level that the target cannot reciprocate, creating an uncomfortable imbalance. The target will seek to exit the relationship or to re-establish balance by limiting further provision on their end.

**Static modeling.** Treating the want map as fixed rather than continuously updated. People's motivational structures change with circumstance, life events, and the relationship's own development. A static model produces increasingly misaligned provision.

### Ethical Perimeter

This module describes methods that can be applied with a range of intentions, from genuinely relationship-building to exploitative. The distinction is not primarily in the technique but in the intent and the honesty of the exchange.

A relationship in which a practitioner accurately understands what a person needs and genuinely provides it — in exchange for genuine value received — is a functional and ethical relationship regardless of its operational context. A relationship in which understanding of a person's needs is used to manipulate them into actions against their own interests crosses an ethical line that this syllabus does not endorse.

The practitioner should maintain clarity about which they are doing. The line is: does the target, if fully informed of the relationship's nature and the practitioner's goals, suffer harm? A relationship in which both parties receive genuine value — even if the practitioner also receives operational benefit — is sustainable and defensible. A relationship in which the target is deceived into actions that damage their interests is not.

[Inference] This ethical distinction also has operational significance. Relationships built on genuine mutual value are more durable, more productive, and less likely to collapse under stress than relationships built on deception or manipulation. Ethical practice and operational effectiveness are more aligned here than they are opposed.

**Key Points**

- Stated, revealed, and latent wants are distinct categories; the most operationally valuable is latent — wants the target has not yet articulated even to themselves
- Multiple motivational frameworks used in parallel produce more accurate modeling than commitment to a single framework
- Want identification is a continuous process conducted across observation, elicitation, and behavioral reading — not a single assessment event
- Genuine relevance to identified wants is more durable than performed relevance; the target must experience real value, not simulated value
- Reciprocity is activated most effectively by unrequested, personalized, appropriately-scaled provision
- The experience of being genuinely understood is among the most powerful relational levers and is rarely provided by other relationships
- The failure mode of visible instrumentality — appearing to value the target as a means — is highly aversive and produces relationship withdrawal

---

**Output**

Select one existing relationship — professional or social — in which you want to deepen engagement:

1. Construct a three-layer want map: stated wants, revealed wants, and your best inference about core needs
2. Identify one latent want — something the person has not named but that behavioral evidence suggests is present
3. Identify what you currently provide that is genuinely valuable to this person, and what gap in their want structure is currently unaddressed by anyone in their network
4. Design one specific, unrequested provision of value calibrated to the identified want — concrete enough to execute in the next two weeks
5. Document what reciprocity response, if any, this produces and what it tells you about the accuracy of your want map
