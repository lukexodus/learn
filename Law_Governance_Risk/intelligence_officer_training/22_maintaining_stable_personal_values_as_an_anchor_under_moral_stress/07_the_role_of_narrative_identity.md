## The Role of Narrative Identity


Psychologist Dan McAdams argues that identity is fundamentally narrative — people construct a story of who they are, what they have done, and why, and this story is the primary mechanism by which moral coherence is maintained over time.

Under moral stress, this narrative is threatened. Decisions that do not fit the self-narrative create dissonance, which is resolved through one of two paths:

1. **Revision of the narrative to accommodate the act** — rationalization, which erodes the anchor
2. **Acknowledgment of the gap between act and standard** — which is painful but preserves the integrity of the anchor as a reference point

Path 2 requires what is sometimes called moral accountability without self-destruction: the capacity to acknowledge that you acted contrary to your values in a specific instance without concluding that your values were wrong or that you are irredeemably corrupt.

This is harder than it sounds. The natural response to acute guilt is either complete self-condemnation (which is destabilizing) or complete rationalization (which erodes the anchor). The functional middle — acknowledgment, accountability, and continued commitment — requires practiced psychological capacity.
