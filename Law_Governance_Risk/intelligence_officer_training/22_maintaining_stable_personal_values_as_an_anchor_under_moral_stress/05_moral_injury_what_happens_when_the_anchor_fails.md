## Moral Injury — What Happens When the Anchor Fails


Moral injury is a construct developed primarily in military and veteran contexts (Jonathan Shay, Brett Litz et al.) to describe the damage caused by:

- Perpetrating, witnessing, or failing to prevent actions that violate one's deeply held moral beliefs
- Betrayal by a trusted authority that requires or ignores such violations

It is distinct from PTSD, though they co-occur. PTSD is organized around fear and threat. Moral injury is organized around guilt, shame, and the fracturing of the individual's sense of themselves as a moral person.

**Symptoms associated with moral injury:**

- Persistent guilt or shame disproportionate to conventional psychiatric frameworks
- Sense of being permanently changed or corrupted
- Difficulty with trust (self and others)
- Withdrawal from relationships and meaning-making activities
- In some cases, profound nihilism or collapse of the belief that moral effort matters

[Unverified as a fully standardized clinical diagnosis — the construct is in active development; its distinction from PTSD is debated in some literature, though its clinical utility is recognized]

**Key Points**

- Moral injury is not the same as making a difficult decision under impossible circumstances. It is specifically the experience of having violated one's own moral standards, or of having been betrayed into doing so.
- [Inference] The presence of a pre-established values anchor does not prevent moral injury, but it may provide a more stable reference point for post-incident processing — the individual knows what they committed to and can assess what actually occurred against that standard.
