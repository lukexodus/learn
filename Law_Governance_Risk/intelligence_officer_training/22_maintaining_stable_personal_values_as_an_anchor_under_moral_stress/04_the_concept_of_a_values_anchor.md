## The Concept of a Values Anchor


A values anchor is a small set of non-negotiable commitments — pre-decided, explicitly articulated, and made prior to the conditions that would pressure them — that function as a decision-making floor.

The term "anchor" is precise: it does not prevent movement, but it constrains the range of drift. It does not tell you what the right decision is in every case. It tells you what decisions are categorically unavailable regardless of situational pressure.

### Design Principles for a Values Anchor

**1. Pre-commitment, not in-the-moment decision**

Decisions made under moral stress are made with impaired PFC function, depleted regulatory capacity, and active social pressure. They are the worst conditions for clear moral reasoning. The anchor must be established before the pressure arrives.

This is structurally identical to the logic of operational pre-commitment in other domains: rules of engagement, standing orders, trip wires. You decide in advance what you will and will not do so that the decision does not have to be made under fire.

**2. Specificity over abstraction**

"I value honesty" is not an anchor. It is too abstract to function as a constraint under pressure, because it can be rationalized around in almost any specific case.

An anchor needs to be specific enough that its application to a concrete situation is clear:

- Abstract: "I will not harm innocent people."
- More specific: "I will not provide information I know will be used to harm a person who has not been established as a direct threat."
- More specific still: "I will not identify the location of a civilian contact to any party, regardless of claimed justification."

The more specific the commitment, the harder it is to rationalize around.

**3. Small number of commitments**

A values anchor is not a comprehensive ethical code. It is a short list of hard limits — the commitments that, if violated, would constitute a fundamental betrayal of who you are. Three to seven commitments is a functional range. [Inference] More than this becomes difficult to hold in working memory under stress and introduces internal conflicts that create ambiguity at the moment you least need it.

**4. Articulated in advance — and written**

The act of writing your core commitments has two functions:

- It forces specificity (you cannot be vague on paper the way you can in thought)
- It creates an external reference point that is harder to retroactively revise than a memory

This is not a permanent document. It should be reviewed and revised, but in conditions of calm reflection, not under situational pressure.

**5. Distinguish between negotiable values and non-negotiable limits**

Not all values are equal in their function. A useful taxonomy:

- **Aspirational values** — ideals you strive toward; these can be compromised by circumstance without constituting a fundamental failure (e.g., generosity, patience)
- **Operational values** — commitments that guide standard decision-making; can be overridden in genuinely exceptional circumstances with full awareness and accountability
- **Hard limits** — the anchor itself; categorically non-negotiable; the violation of these represents a line you have decided in advance you will not cross under any framing

The anchor consists only of hard limits.
