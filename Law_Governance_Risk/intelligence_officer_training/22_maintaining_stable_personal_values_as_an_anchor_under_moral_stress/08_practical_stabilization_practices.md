## Practical Stabilization Practices


These are not guarantees. [Inference] Their efficacy depends on consistent prior practice, individual psychological profile, and the severity of the moral stress encountered. They are described as practices with documented use in relevant professional contexts, not as universally effective interventions.

### Pre-commitment Documentation

As described above: write your hard limits before you are in conditions that would pressure them. Review periodically in conditions of calm. Note any revisions and the reasoning behind them.

### Values Rehearsal Under Simulated Pressure

The training logic is identical to physical and cognitive stress inoculation: expose yourself to increasingly challenging ethical scenarios — through structured reflection, scenario training, or role-play — so that your values are not encountering serious pressure for the first time in a real situation.

This is part of the design of military ethics training, some law enforcement training, and certain professional contexts (medical ethics, legal ethics). Its transfer to operational reality is imperfect but non-zero. [Inference]

### Scheduled Reflection

Designate regular intervals — not crisis-driven — for reviewing your decisions against your stated values. This serves multiple functions:

- Catches incremental drift before it compounds
- Creates a record of decision-making that resists retroactive revision
- Provides a structured processing window for compartmentalized material

The format matters less than the regularity and the honesty. A journal, a trusted interlocutor, a structured self-interrogation protocol — any of these can function.

### Trusted Interlocutor

A person — outside your operational context where possible — with whom you can discuss moral weight honestly and without performance. This is not therapy (though therapy serves a related function). It is someone whose judgment you trust and who will not simply validate your existing narrative.

[Inference] Isolation is a significant amplifier of moral distress. The absence of any trusted interlocutor increases the probability of dysfunctional compartmentalization and decreases the availability of corrective feedback.

### The Pre-Mortem — Ethical Version

Before entering a situation with significant moral complexity, explicitly ask: what are the conditions under which I might compromise my hard limits in this situation? What would the pressure look like? What rationalization would I use?

Anticipating the specific form that temptation or pressure will take — rather than maintaining only an abstract commitment — increases the probability of recognition in the moment. [Inference; not a guaranteed effect]

### Recognizing Rationalization Signatures

Develop facility with the specific language of moral disengagement as it occurs in your own thinking:

- "This is an exceptional circumstance."
- "No one else would know."
- "The outcome justifies it."
- "Everyone in this position does the same."
- "I had no real choice."
- "It's not technically a violation."

None of these formulations is inherently false. Any of them can be genuine. But they are also the characteristic structure of rationalization. When you notice them appearing in your thinking, that is the signal to slow down and interrogate, not to proceed. [Inference]
