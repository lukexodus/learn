## The Psychology of Targeted Killing Decisions


---

### 1. Scope and Frame

Targeted killing — the deliberate, premeditated lethal action against a specific identified individual by a state or state-authorized actor — is distinct from battlefield killing in its psychological structure. It involves advance identification, deliberate selection, a decision process that unfolds over time, and often physical or temporal distance between the decision-maker and the act. Each of these features produces distinct psychological dynamics that differ from the immediate, reactive violence of combat.

This module examines the psychology of the human beings involved in these decisions — those who authorize, those who execute, and the institutional structures that mediate between them. It draws on open-source academic research, journalism, published memoirs, and documented policy frameworks. Where claims cannot be verified, they are labeled.

This module does not provide operational targeting guidance. It examines documented psychological phenomena.

---

### 2. The Decision Architecture

#### 2.1 Distributed Responsibility

Targeted killing decisions in documented state programs — the U.S. drone program, Israeli targeted killing policy, historical cases involving British, Russian, and other state actors — are structurally distributed across multiple actors and institutional layers. This distribution is not incidental. It is a design feature with both operational and psychological functions.

The chain typically involves:

- **Intelligence producers** — who identify the target and assess the case
- **Legal reviewers** — who assess lawfulness under applicable frameworks
- **Military or operational commanders** — who assess feasibility and proportionality
- **Political authorization** — at varying levels depending on the program and jurisdiction
- **Operators** — who execute

No single individual makes the decision in isolation. Each actor in the chain contributes a partial authorization. The full decision is the product of the chain.

**The psychological consequence** of this architecture is the diffusion of moral responsibility. Each participant can accurately represent their own role as partial: the intelligence analyst did not authorize; the lawyer did not target; the commander did not pull the trigger; the operator did not choose the target. This diffusion is documented in social psychology under Milgram's concept of agentic shift — the individual perceives themselves as an instrument of a system rather than a moral agent making an independent choice.

**[Inference]:** Distributed responsibility architecture may function not only to produce better decisions through multiple checks but also to reduce the psychological burden on any individual participant. Whether this is a deliberate design choice or an emergent institutional feature is not something I can verify across all documented programs.

#### 2.2 Temporal Structure of the Decision

Unlike reactive combat decisions, targeted killing decisions typically unfold over extended time periods — hours, days, weeks, or longer. This temporal structure produces specific psychological dynamics:

- **Habituation** — repeated exposure to the target as an abstraction (a file, a pattern of life, a name on a list) over time reduces the psychological salience of the target's personhood. The target becomes a case.
- **Commitment escalation** — as more resources, time, and institutional investment are committed to a targeting case, psychological pressure to complete the action increases. Sunk cost dynamics apply.
- **Normalization** — the repeated consideration of lethal action within an institutional context progressively normalizes the decision type. What is extraordinary becomes procedural.

#### 2.3 The Role of Bureaucratic Language

Documented targeting programs employ specific linguistic frameworks that are psychologically significant: "neutralize," "remove," "service the target," "kinetic action," "direct action," "high-value target elimination." This is not simply professional terminology. Euphemistic language is a documented mechanism for reducing the psychological weight of morally significant acts.

Albert Bandura's work on moral disengagement identifies euphemistic labeling as one of eight mechanisms by which individuals disengage their moral self-regulatory systems from their behavior. The language does not change the act; it changes the cognitive frame within which the act is processed.

### 3. Psychological Mechanisms in Decision-Makers

#### 3.1 Moral Disengagement

Bandura's framework, developed in the context of organizational behavior and later applied explicitly to military contexts, identifies the following mechanisms as operative in lethal decision-making:

- **Moral justification** — construing the action as serving a higher moral purpose (protecting civilians, preventing future attacks)
- **Euphemistic labeling** — as described above
- **Advantageous comparison** — comparing the action to a worse alternative ("if we don't, they will kill many more")
- **Displacement of responsibility** — attributing agency to the chain of command or legal authorization
- **Diffusion of responsibility** — distributing agency across multiple actors
- **Dehumanization** — reducing the target to a category (terrorist, combatant, threat) rather than a person
- **Attribution of blame** — construing the target as having forfeited protection through their own actions

In documented targeting programs, multiple mechanisms operate simultaneously. This is not a pathological condition — it is a normal psychological response to the structural and moral demands of institutionalized lethal decision-making.

#### 3.2 Dehumanization and Categorical Thinking

Dehumanization in this context does not require overt contempt or hostility. It occurs through the substitution of a categorical identity for a personal one. A target who is processed as a role — "the bomb-maker," "the network facilitator," "the commander" — is psychologically different from a target processed as a specific person with specific relationships, history, and interiority.

Intelligence systems that produce targeting packages — collections of surveillance, biographical data, network analysis — vary in the degree to which they present the target as an individual or as a node in a threat network. The framing of the package has psychological consequences for decision-makers that are independent of its factual content.

**[Inference]:** Targeting packages that emphasize threat role and network function rather than biographical particularity may facilitate the decision process by reducing the psychological salience of the target's personhood. Whether this is an intended design feature of any specific program is not something I can verify.

#### 3.3 Certainty, Probability, and the Tolerance for Error

Targeting decisions are made under uncertainty. Intelligence is probabilistic, not certain. The decision to authorize lethal action against a specific individual is always, in a strict epistemic sense, a decision made under conditions of incomplete information.

The psychological management of this uncertainty is a central feature of the decision process. Documented mechanisms include:

- **Confidence inflation** — the tendency to treat high-confidence assessments as certainties, particularly under institutional pressure to act
- **Threshold anchoring** — the establishment of explicit or implicit probability thresholds above which action is authorized, and the psychological tendency to treat threshold-meeting as certainty
- **Collateral damage estimation** — formal frameworks for estimating non-combatant harm that, in producing a number, may produce false precision and reduce the psychological weight of uncertainty

Research on decision-making under uncertainty — including work by Kahneman and Tversky on probability weighting — documents consistent human tendencies to treat high probabilities as certainties and to underweight small probabilities of catastrophic outcomes. These tendencies apply to targeting decision-makers.

#### 3.4 Affect and Moral Intuition

The dual-process model of moral judgment — associated with Joshua Greene's work among others — proposes that moral decisions involve both fast, intuitive, emotionally-driven responses and slower, deliberative, rule-based reasoning. These systems can produce conflicting outputs.

In targeting decisions:

- **Affective responses** may generate resistance to lethal action against individuals who have been encountered personally, who belong to salient social categories (women, elderly individuals, individuals with families), or whose suffering is made vivid and proximate
- **Deliberative reasoning** may override these responses through the mechanisms of moral disengagement described above, or may itself generate resistance through explicit ethical reasoning

The emotional distance produced by drone and remote targeting — the physical separation of the operator from the target — reduces the activation of affective moral responses. This is documented in research and discussed explicitly in the ethics literature on drone warfare. The reduction of affective input does not eliminate moral reasoning; it changes its inputs.

### 4. The Operator's Psychology

#### 4.1 Distance and the "Playstation Mentality" Question

The characterization of drone operators as experiencing lethal action as a video game — the "Playstation mentality" — has been both asserted and contested in open literature. The assertion was made by some early commentators and military officials; subsequent research and reported experience of drone operators substantially complicates this characterization.

What is documented in open journalism and academic research, including work by Dave Grossman and subsequent researchers:

- Physical distance reduces the immediate physiological stress response associated with proximate killing
- Temporal proximity — watching a target over extended periods before a strike — creates a form of familiarity that can intensify, not reduce, the psychological weight of the act
- Post-strike observation — watching the aftermath — is reported by some operators as among the most psychologically significant aspects of the role
- PTSD rates among drone operators are documented and are not negligible, though comparison with other military roles is methodologically complex

**[Unverified]:** Claims about the precise psychological experience of drone operators are based on reported accounts, journalism, and limited academic research. The sample is not representative, reporting may be subject to social desirability bias, and institutional constraints on disclosure limit the available evidence.

#### 4.2 Moral Injury

Moral injury — distinct from PTSD — is the psychological harm produced by participating in, witnessing, or failing to prevent acts that violate one's moral beliefs, or by the betrayal of trust by authorities. The concept is associated with Jonathan Shay's work and has been extensively developed by Brett Litz and colleagues.

In the context of targeted killing:

- Operators who later question the legality, proportionality, or accuracy of strikes they executed may experience moral injury specifically related to the institutional authorization they acted under
- Decision-makers who authorized strikes later found to involve targeting errors may experience moral injury related to institutional responsibility
- The temporal delay between action and consequence — learning weeks or months later that a strike killed civilians — produces a different moral injury pattern than immediate feedback

Several published accounts by former drone operators — including Brandon Bryant's public statements — describe experiences consistent with moral injury. These accounts are open-source, self-reported, and not independently verified in all particulars.

#### 4.3 Compartmentalization as Psychological Function

Operators and decision-makers in targeting programs typically operate under strict compartmentalization — the institutional and psychological separation of the targeting role from other aspects of identity and daily life. This compartmentalization serves operational security functions and simultaneously serves psychological ones: it creates a bounded context within which lethal decision-making occurs, separated from the moral frameworks governing ordinary life.

The sustainability of this compartmentalization varies individually and over time. Cases in which it breaks down — operators who cannot maintain the boundary between professional and personal moral frameworks — are documented in open journalism as a source of both psychological distress and, in some cases, whistleblowing behavior.

### 5. Institutional Psychology

#### 5.1 The Targeting Culture

Organizations that conduct targeted killing develop institutional cultures that shape the psychology of individual participants. These cultures include:

- **Normalized risk thresholds** — what level of collateral damage probability is routinely authorized, what intelligence confidence threshold is treated as sufficient
- **Peer norms** — what decision-making behaviors are respected or stigmatized within the professional community
- **Success metrics** — how outcomes are evaluated, what counts as a successful operation, how errors are processed institutionally

**[Inference]:** Institutional cultures that treat targeting as primarily a technical and procedural problem — rather than a moral one — may systematically reduce the moral deliberation applied to individual decisions. Conversely, cultures that create space for dissent and ethical challenge may produce slower but more defensible decision processes. Whether any specific program has achieved either of these conditions is not something I can verify.

#### 5.2 Bureaucratic Momentum

Once a targeting program is institutionally established, it develops momentum that is independent of its original justification. Personnel are trained, infrastructure is built, legal frameworks are developed, metrics are established, careers are built around the program's continuation. This creates institutional inertia that is psychologically relevant to individual decision-makers: the program's continuation is a given; the individual's role is to execute within it.

This dynamic is documented in organizational psychology and applies to targeting programs as it does to other institutions. It does not determine individual behavior, but it shapes the choice architecture within which individual decisions are made.

#### 5.3 Groupthink and Confirmation in Intelligence Assessment

Targeting decisions depend on intelligence assessments that are themselves subject to groupthink dynamics — the tendency of cohesive groups to converge on shared interpretations and suppress dissent. In targeting contexts:

- Assessments that support a pre-existing case for targeting may receive less scrutiny than assessments that undermine it
- Analysts who raise doubts may face social and professional disincentives
- The institutional investment in a targeting case increases pressure toward confirmatory assessment

These dynamics are documented in post-hoc reviews of intelligence failures, including reviews related to cases of mistaken identity in targeting operations. Several such cases are publicly documented, including through journalism and official inquiries.

### 6. Targeting Errors and Their Psychological Aftermath

#### 6.1 Mistaken Identity

Cases in which the wrong individual was killed — either through intelligence error or misidentification — are documented in open sources. Examples include:

- The 2011 NATO airstrike in Afghanistan that killed a group of civilians misidentified as insurgents (documented in official investigation)
- Multiple documented cases of wedding party strikes based on pattern-of-life misinterpretation
- The CIA's acknowledged killing of Warren Weinstein and Giovanni Lo Porto, U.S. and Italian nationals, in a 2015 strike targeting a suspected al-Qaeda facility

In each case, the targeting process operated as designed — the error was in the intelligence input, not the procedural chain. This is psychologically significant: the process produced a false sense of legitimacy for an erroneous decision.

#### 6.2 Psychological Response to Error

The psychological response to discovering a targeting error — that one authorized or executed the killing of the wrong person, or that civilian casualties were greater than assessed — is documented in limited open sources as a source of significant moral injury.

The institutional response to error matters for the individual's psychological processing: programs that acknowledge error, conduct genuine review, and modify practice create different psychological conditions than those that minimize, classify, or deny error.

**[Inference]:** Institutions that systematically suppress acknowledgment of targeting errors may reduce immediate institutional accountability while increasing the long-term psychological cost to individual participants who have independent knowledge of the error.

### 7. Ethical Frameworks Applied by Decision-Makers

#### 7.1 Just War Theory

The dominant explicit ethical framework in Western military targeting doctrine is derived from just war theory, particularly the principles of:

- **Distinction** — between combatants and civilians
- **Proportionality** — between military advantage gained and civilian harm caused
- **Military necessity** — that force is necessary to achieve a legitimate military objective
- **Precaution** — that all feasible steps are taken to minimize civilian harm

These principles are operationalized in legal frameworks including Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions and domestic military legal guidance. Decision-makers are trained in these frameworks and apply them, at least formally, as part of the targeting process.

The psychological function of the just war framework is not only to constrain action but to provide moral authorization for action. A strike assessed as lawful under the framework is experienced by decision-makers as morally authorized, regardless of whether the framework itself fully captures the moral weight of the act.

#### 7.2 Consequentialist Reasoning and Its Distortions

Much operational targeting reasoning is implicitly consequentialist: the harm of the strike is justified by the harm it prevents. This reasoning is subject to documented cognitive distortions:

- **Optimistic projection** — future harms prevented are speculative; present harm caused is certain. Psychological asymmetry between certain and uncertain outcomes produces systematic bias.
- **Scope insensitivity** — documented tendency to respond similarly to small and large numbers of potential victims, which distorts proportionality assessments
- **Attribution bias** — harms caused by inaction (not striking) may be attributed to the adversary; harms caused by action (striking) are attributed to the decision-maker. This asymmetry affects the weighting of action versus inaction.

#### 7.3 Deontological Resistance

Some decision-makers apply deontological constraints — rules that are not subject to consequentialist override. "We do not kill civilians regardless of military advantage" is a deontological constraint. The psychological function of bright-line rules is to remove certain decisions from cost-benefit calculation, reducing both the cognitive load and the moral vulnerability of the decision-maker.

The erosion of bright-line rules under operational pressure — the gradual expansion of who counts as a legitimate target, what collateral damage is acceptable — is documented in multiple program histories and is psychologically consistent with the normalization process described above.

### 8. Psychological Sustainability and Long-Term Effects

#### 8.1 Career-Length Exposure

Individuals who participate in targeting decisions over extended careers — years to decades — face a different psychological challenge than those involved in isolated decisions. The cumulative weight of repeated decisions, the accumulation of cases including errors, and the progressive normalization of decision types all affect long-term psychological condition.

Open accounts by former senior intelligence and military officials — including published memoirs — describe varied long-term responses ranging from sustained conviction in the necessity of the work to significant retrospective moral doubt.

#### 8.2 Post-Service Processing

The transition from a role in which targeted killing decisions are routine to civilian life — where those decisions exist in a radically different moral context — is documented as a significant psychological transition. The compartmentalization that sustains performance during service may become maladaptive in its absence.

**[Unverified]:** Systematic data on long-term psychological outcomes for targeting decision-makers specifically — as distinct from combat veterans generally — is not available in open sources to my knowledge. Claims about specific prevalence rates of moral injury or PTSD in this population should be treated as unverified.

### 9. The Target's Psychology: A Brief Note

Most psychological research on targeted killing focuses on decision-makers and operators. The psychology of individuals who know they are targeted — and who live under that condition — is less documented in academic literature but appears in journalism and memoir.

Documented behavioral responses in open sources include:

- Alteration of movement patterns and communication to reduce targeting vulnerability
- Delegation of functions to reduce the targeting value of any single individual
- Psychological adaptation to sustained threat that resembles chronic stress responses documented in other high-threat populations
- In some documented cases, behavioral changes that themselves created intelligence signatures

This dimension is operationally relevant: a target who knows they are targeted behaves differently from one who does not, and those behavioral changes affect both targeting difficulty and the intelligence picture.

---

**Key Points**

- The distributed responsibility architecture of institutional targeting programs is psychologically functional: it reduces the moral burden on any individual participant through agentic shift and diffusion of responsibility. This is documented in social psychology and observable in targeting program structures.
- The metacognitive failure that makes sleep deprivation dangerous has a structural analog in targeting: the moral disengagement mechanisms that make lethal decisions psychologically sustainable also reduce the decision-maker's capacity to perceive the full moral weight of what they are doing.
- Physical and temporal distance reduce affective moral input but do not eliminate moral consequences — they alter the form in which those consequences manifest, typically delaying and displacing them.
- Targeting errors are psychologically significant not only for their direct consequences but because they reveal that procedural compliance does not guarantee moral correctness. This revelation, when it occurs, is a primary source of moral injury.
- Institutional culture, bureaucratic momentum, and linguistic framing are not peripheral to targeting psychology — they constitute the environment within which individual psychological processes operate. Individual psychology cannot be understood in isolation from these structural factors.
- No ethical framework applied in practice fully resolves the moral weight of targeted killing decisions. The frameworks provide structure, constraint, and authorization — they do not eliminate the moral residue that participants carry.
