## Intelligence Ethics: Literature, Frameworks, and Contested Terrain


---

### 1. Scope and Framing

Intelligence ethics is a subfield at the intersection of applied ethics, political philosophy, and security studies. It addresses the moral dimensions of state-sponsored collection, analysis, covert action, and deception — activities that are institutionally sanctioned but that would, in most other contexts, constitute serious moral and legal violations.

The literature is not unified. It spans philosophical ethics, just war theory, legal scholarship, political science, and practitioner memoir. There is no consensus framework. The field is characterized by genuine disagreement at the foundational level — not merely disagreement about applications of shared principles, but disagreement about which moral framework is appropriate to apply at all.

**Key Points**

- Intelligence ethics is not a settled discipline with established conclusions; it is an ongoing contested domain
- The practitioner literature and the philosophical literature often talk past each other
- Much of the most operationally relevant material is classified and therefore absent from the open-source record [Unverified: the extent to which classified ethics doctrine diverges from published literature is unknown]

### 2. Foundational Texts

#### 2.1 Philosophy and Applied Ethics

**Michael Walzer — _Just and Unjust Wars_ (1977)** The foundational text of modern just war theory. Not specific to intelligence, but provides the framework most commonly applied to covert action, targeted killing, and the ethics of political violence. Walzer's doctrine of double effect, non-combatant immunity, and the conditions of legitimate war-making are direct antecedents to intelligence ethics arguments.

Walzer's later work, including essays collected in _Arguing About War_ (2004), extends his framework to asymmetric conflict and anticipatory action — both directly relevant to intelligence contexts.

**Intelligence-specific application:** The question of whether covert operations can satisfy jus ad bellum and jus in bello criteria is a direct extension of Walzer's framework. Whether his framework was designed to handle state-sponsored deception and non-attributable violence is contested.

---

**Sissela Bok — _Lying: Moral Choice in Public and Private Life_ (1978)** A systematic philosophical treatment of deception. Bok argues that lying is presumptively wrong and that the burden of justification always falls on the deceiver, not the deceived. She examines cases where lying may be justified — self-defense, protection of third parties — but maintains that institutional deception is particularly corrosive because it degrades the epistemic commons on which society depends.

Directly relevant to intelligence: Bok's framework makes a strong case that state-sponsored deception requires a justification that most intelligence practice does not explicitly provide. The book is frequently cited in intelligence ethics literature as establishing the baseline presumption against deception that intelligence agencies must overcome.

---

**Jeff McMahan — _Killing in War_ (2009)** A revisionist challenge to Walzer. McMahan argues that the moral permissions of war must be grounded in individual moral responsibility, not in the institutional status of combatants. His framework, if applied to intelligence, implies that the question of whether an intelligence operation is permissible cannot be answered by appeal to institutional authorization alone — it depends on the moral facts of the specific case.

McMahan's work is philosophically rigorous and technically demanding. It is relevant to intelligence ethics primarily through its challenge to the institutional legitimacy argument: the claim that state authorization makes otherwise impermissible acts permissible.

---

#### 2.2 Intelligence-Specific Philosophical Literature

**Michael Herman — _Intelligence Power in Peace and War_ (1996)** Herman is a former GCHQ officer and one of the few practitioners to engage seriously with ethical questions at a philosophical level. The book addresses the ethics of collection, the use of intelligence in decision-making, and the moral status of intelligence as a profession.

Herman's central argument is that intelligence is a legitimate instrument of statecraft whose ethics must be evaluated within the framework of state responsibility for national security — not by the standards applicable to private individuals. He acknowledges that this framework leaves many questions unresolved.

**Key Points**

- Herman is one of the most analytically rigorous practitioner-scholars in the field
- His framework is broadly consequentialist at the state level; critics argue this is insufficient

---

**Loch K. Johnson — _The Threat on the Horizon_ (2011) and extensive journal work** Johnson is the most prolific academic scholar of intelligence ethics in the American context. His work spans oversight, accountability, covert action, and the relationship between intelligence agencies and democratic governance.

His framework of **an ethical ladder of escalation** for covert action — ranging from propaganda and political influence at the low end to assassination and paramilitary operations at the high end — is one of the few structured ethical frameworks specific to intelligence. Higher rungs require more stringent justification and more robust oversight.

Johnson's work is primarily within a liberal democratic framework: the central ethical constraint on intelligence is accountability to democratic institutions.

---

**Mark Phythian (ed.) — _Understanding the Intelligence Cycle_ (2013)** A collected volume addressing, among other topics, the ethical dimensions of the intelligence cycle at each stage. Includes contributions from multiple scholars. Useful as a survey of current academic positions rather than as a unified argument.

---

**Jennifer Sims and Burton Gerber (eds.) — _Transforming U.S. Intelligence_ (2005)** Practitioner-oriented. Contains chapters addressing the ethics of intelligence collection and covert action from the perspective of former officers. Less philosophically rigorous than the academic literature but more grounded in operational reality.

#### 2.3 Torture, Coercive Interrogation, and the Post-2001 Literature

The post-September 2001 period generated a substantial body of ethics literature specifically focused on interrogation, detention, and targeted killing.

**Henry Shue — "Torture" (1978, _Philosophy & Public Affairs_)** Written well before the post-2001 debates, Shue's article is the foundational philosophical treatment of torture. He argues that torture is morally impermissible in all circumstances, including the ticking-bomb scenario, on the grounds that it involves the complete subjugation of a helpless person — a violation that cannot be justified by consequences alone.

Shue's article is the reference point for all subsequent philosophical debate on coercive interrogation.

---

**Alan Dershowitz — _Why Terrorism Works_ (2002)** Argues the opposing position: that in genuine ticking-bomb scenarios, torture may be permissible, and that the appropriate response is legal regulation (torture warrants) rather than absolute prohibition. Dershowitz's position is a minority view in the philosophical literature but has had significant influence on policy debates.

**Note:** The empirical claim that coercive interrogation produces reliable intelligence is contested and has been addressed directly by the U.S. Senate Intelligence Committee report on CIA detention and interrogation (2014), which concluded that the program did not produce unique, actionable intelligence that could not have been obtained through other means. [This is a summary of the committee's publicly stated findings; the underlying evidence is classified and the findings are disputed by former CIA officers.]

---

**David Luban — "Liberalism, Torture, and the Ticking Bomb" (2005, _Virginia Law Review_)** A systematic critique of the ticking-bomb argument. Luban argues that the scenario is constructed to make torture seem reasonable by stipulating away all the features that make it unreliable and unjustifiable in practice. His analysis of how exceptional permissions become normalized institutional practice is widely cited.

---

**Philippe Sands — _Torture Team_ (2008)** A legal and journalistic account of how coercive interrogation was authorized following September 2001. Not primarily philosophical but provides detailed documentation of how legal and ethical constraints were formally bypassed — relevant to the study of institutional ethics failure.

---

#### 2.4 Targeted Killing and Drone Warfare

**Kenneth Roth and others — Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International reports** The NGO literature provides systematic documentation of targeted killing operations and applies international humanitarian law (IHL) frameworks. Not philosophical ethics but applied legal ethics grounded in treaty law.

**Bradley Jay Strawser (ed.) — _Killing by Remote Control_ (2013)** A collected philosophical volume on the ethics of drone warfare and targeted killing. Contributions range from arguments that remotely conducted killing is permissible under just war principles to arguments that the removal of physical risk from the operator changes the moral calculus of the act.

**Michael Gross — _The Ethics of Insurgency_ (2015)** Addresses irregular warfare and asymmetric conflict, including the ethical dimensions of intelligence operations in counterinsurgency contexts. Gross argues for a modified just war framework that accommodates the specific features of non-state armed conflict.

---

#### 2.5 Surveillance and Privacy

**Priscilla Regan — _Legislating Privacy_ (1995)** A political science treatment of privacy as a collective rather than merely individual value. Relevant to intelligence ethics because it reframes surveillance not as a violation of individual rights but as a degradation of social conditions.

**Neil Richards — _Intellectual Privacy_ (2015)** Argues that surveillance of thought and communication — even when non-coercive — produces chilling effects on intellectual activity that are harmful independently of any specific disclosure. Directly relevant to bulk collection and communications intelligence.

**Glenn Greenwald — _No Place to Hide_ (2014)** Journalistic account of the Snowden disclosures. Not philosophical ethics, but provides the factual record against which surveillance ethics arguments must be evaluated.

**David Cole and Federico Labaton — legal scholarship on surveillance and constitutional law** The legal ethics literature on signals intelligence is substantial. Cole's work in particular addresses the tension between national security imperatives and constitutional privacy protections within the U.S. legal framework.

### 3. Primary Ethical Frameworks Applied to Intelligence

#### 3.1 Consequentialism

The dominant implicit framework in intelligence practice. Operations are evaluated by their outcomes: does the intelligence collected, or the covert action undertaken, produce net benefit (in terms of security, lives, national interest) sufficient to justify the means employed?

**Strengths in the intelligence context:**

- Corresponds to how practitioners actually reason
- Accommodates the reality that intelligence involves morally costly means; the question is whether the cost is justified by the benefit
- Flexible enough to handle novel situations without requiring categorization

**Weaknesses:**

- Requires accurate prediction of consequences, which is notoriously difficult in intelligence contexts — the second and third-order effects of operations are frequently miscalculated
- Permits any means in principle if the outcome is sufficiently good — a framework that provides no categorical prohibitions is operationally and institutionally dangerous
- Who counts in the consequentialist calculus? Targets, citizens of adversary states, allies, future populations — the answer to this question determines the conclusion, and intelligence agencies have structural incentives to define the calculus narrowly

---

#### 3.2 Deontological Ethics

Rights-based frameworks impose categorical constraints on means regardless of consequences. Certain acts — torture, assassination of non-combatants, deception of one's own population — are impermissible regardless of outcome.

**Strengths:**

- Provides categorical prohibitions that resist institutional erosion through exception-making
- Corresponds to the intuitions most people hold about what states should not do
- Compatible with international humanitarian law, which is structurally deontological

**Weaknesses:**

- Conflicts directly with intelligence operational reality: collection, covert action, and deception are all potentially prohibited by a strict rights-based framework
- The question of whose rights constrain intelligence activity — citizens, foreigners, adversaries — is not resolved within the framework itself
- Absolute prohibitions may produce perverse outcomes in genuine cases of catastrophic threat

---

#### 3.3 Just War Theory

The most commonly applied framework in the academic intelligence ethics literature. Just war theory provides criteria for:

**Jus ad bellum** (justice of going to war / initiating an operation):

- Just cause
- Right intention
- Proportionality
- Reasonable prospect of success
- Last resort
- Legitimate authority

**Jus in bello** (justice in the conduct of operations):

- Discrimination (distinction between combatants and non-combatants)
- Proportionality (civilian harm proportionate to military advantage)
- Military necessity

**Application to intelligence:**

- Covert action that involves violence is evaluated against jus in bello criteria
- The question of whether intelligence agencies constitute legitimate authority under jus ad bellum is not resolved in the literature
- Non-violent collection operations fall outside the original scope of just war theory and require extension of the framework [Inference: the fit between JWT and non-violent intelligence is imperfect and the literature reflects this]

---

#### 3.4 Virtue Ethics

A framework less commonly applied in intelligence ethics but increasingly present in the literature. Rather than asking what acts are permissible, virtue ethics asks what character traits an intelligence officer ought to have, and what institutional culture supports or degrades those traits.

**Relevant virtues in intelligence contexts:**

- Practical wisdom (phronesis) — the capacity to make good judgments in complex, ambiguous situations without recourse to rules
- Honesty — particularly in the tension between the deception required in operations and the accuracy required in analysis
- Loyalty — to institution, to nation, to principles; the conflicts between these loyalties are a central moral feature of intelligence work
- Courage — including moral courage: the willingness to report accurately and speak against institutional consensus

**Key text:** Seumas Miller's work on the ethics of policing and intelligence applies virtue ethics frameworks to institutional contexts. His _Institutional Corruption_ (2017) addresses how institutional pressures degrade individual moral character over time — directly relevant to intelligence agencies.

---

#### 3.5 Democratic Accountability Frameworks

A tradition that does not map neatly onto the philosophical frameworks above. Its central claim is that in a democracy, intelligence activities derive their legitimacy from democratic authorization and oversight, and their ethics must be evaluated within that framework.

**Key texts:**

- Loch K. Johnson's work (above)
- Frederick Hitz — _Why Spy?_ (2008)
- Amy Zegart — _Spying Blind_ (2007); _Eyes on Spies_ (2011)

**Central questions:**

- What oversight structures are necessary for intelligence to be democratically legitimate?
- What should intelligence agencies be permitted to do without prior authorization?
- What is the appropriate relationship between intelligence agencies and elected officials?

**Key Points**

- This framework treats democratic accountability as the primary ethical constraint, which implies that intelligence activities authorized by appropriate democratic processes are presumptively permissible
- Critics argue that democratic authorization does not resolve the underlying moral questions — a democratic majority can authorize torture [Inference: this is a standard objection in the literature]

### 4. Specific Contested Terrain

#### 4.1 The Ethics of Deception

Deception is constitutive of intelligence work — not incidental to it. The ethics of deception in intelligence contexts is therefore foundational, not peripheral.

**The presumption against deception (Bok):** Lying violates the autonomy of the deceived by substituting the deceiver's judgment for their own without consent. The deceived cannot make rational choices because they are operating on false information.

**The national security exception:** States have routinely claimed that deception of adversaries — foreign governments, terrorist organizations — is permissible because the relationship is not one of mutual obligation. This is the most widely accepted position in both practitioner and academic literature.

**The contested cases:**

- Deception of allied governments
- Deception of one's own population (cover stories, disinformation, domestic operations)
- Deception of non-combatant civilians (false-flag recruitment, unwitting asset development)
- Deception of the oversight structures nominally responsible for accountability

The last case — deception of oversight — is addressed in the congressional testimony and inspector general literature following multiple intelligence scandals. It represents a breakdown of the democratic accountability framework from within.

---

#### 4.2 Assassination and Targeted Killing

Whether states may deliberately kill specific individuals outside of conventional armed conflict is one of the most contested questions in both legal and ethical intelligence literature.

**Permissibility arguments:**

- Targeted killing of a specific combatant is more discriminate than conventional warfare and may produce fewer civilian casualties
- Anticipatory self-defense permits action against imminent threats
- Individuals who have forfeited combatant status through terrorist acts may be targeted

**Impermissibility arguments:**

- Extrajudicial killing violates due process and the right to life
- The category of "targetable individual" is defined by the killing state without independent review
- Killing at distance, without warning, removes all possibility of surrender or capture
- The precedent, if universalized, permits any state to kill anyone it designates as a threat

**Key legal framework:** Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions; Additional Protocol I; the UN Charter Article 51 on self-defense. These are legal rather than philosophical sources, but the philosophical literature takes them as constraints that any ethical framework must engage.

---

#### 4.3 Mass Surveillance

Whether bulk collection of communications data — without individualized suspicion — is ethically permissible is a post-Snowden debate with substantial literature.

**Privacy as individual right:** Surveillance of an individual without cause violates their reasonable expectation of privacy and their autonomy.

**Privacy as collective condition (Regan):** Mass surveillance degrades the social conditions of free inquiry and association independently of any specific disclosure.

**The security argument:** Bulk collection enables pattern detection that targeted collection cannot achieve; the harm to any specific individual whose data is collected but never examined is minimal.

**The chilling effect:** Awareness of potential surveillance changes behavior — in journalists, activists, lawyers, political opponents — without any specific disclosure being necessary. The harm is diffuse and structural.

---

#### 4.4 The Dirty Hands Problem

Originating in Sartre's play _Dirty Hands_ (1948) and developed philosophically by Walzer in his 1973 essay "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands" (_Philosophy & Public Affairs_), this is one of the most intellectually serious problems in intelligence ethics.

The dirty hands problem: a political actor may be required to do something morally wrong in order to achieve a morally necessary outcome. The act remains wrong — it cannot be justified — but it was necessary. The actor incurs genuine moral guilt even if the outcome was worth it.

**Application to intelligence:** An intelligence officer who authorizes torture, or runs an operation that kills non-combatants, or deceives an ally, may have acted rightly given the constraints — but has still done something morally wrong. The correct response is not to claim the act was permissible, but to acknowledge the moral cost while accepting it was necessary.

**Key Points**

- The dirty hands framework resists both consequentialist (the outcome justifies the means) and deontological (the act was categorically wrong regardless of outcome) resolutions
- It is uncomfortable institutionally because it requires acknowledging wrongdoing that was authorized
- Walzer's position is that the politician or officer who feels no guilt has failed morally, even if the outcome was good — moral seriousness requires acknowledgment of the cost

#### 4.5 Whistleblowing and Loyalty

The ethics of disclosing classified information to the public or oversight bodies is addressed in a specific literature:

**Sissela Bok — _Secrets: On the Ethics of Concealment and Revelation_ (1983)** A systematic treatment of the ethics of secrecy, including when revelation of secrets is justified. Bok's framework requires that whistleblowers exhaust internal channels, that the wrongdoing be serious, and that the disclosure be to an appropriate party rather than indiscriminate.

**Frederick Elliston — "Anonymity and Whistleblowing" (1982)** Addresses the ethics of anonymous disclosure — directly relevant to intelligence contexts where identified disclosure may be impossible.

**The institutional loyalty conflict:** Officers in intelligence agencies take oaths of secrecy and institutional loyalty. Whistleblowing violates institutional loyalty in service of a higher obligation — to law, to democratic accountability, or to public safety. The ethics literature does not resolve this conflict uniformly; it identifies it as a genuine moral dilemma in which both obligations are real.

### 5. Practitioner Ethics Literature

#### 5.1 Memoir and Practitioner Reflection

Most practitioner memoirs do not engage in explicit ethical analysis, but some are exceptions:

**Stansfield Turner — _Secrecy and Democracy_ (1985)** Turner, as DCI under Carter, addresses the tension between intelligence effectiveness and democratic accountability. He argues for more robust oversight, a position that put him in conflict with parts of the intelligence community.

**Robert Baer — _See No Evil_ (2002)** A practitioner account that, while primarily operational, reflects on the moral costs of counterterrorism work. Baer is critical of institutional risk-aversion that, he argues, increased rather than decreased harm.

**Tony Mendez — _The Master of Disguise_ (1999)** Memoirs of a senior CIA technical operations officer. Relevant to ethics primarily for its account of the psychological dimensions of cover and deception — the moral weight of sustained identity construction.

**Key Points**

- Practitioner literature tends toward consequentialism and institutional loyalty as primary ethical frameworks
- Explicit engagement with philosophical ethics is rare; the ethical reasoning is usually implicit in the framing of decisions
- Practitioner accounts are subject to institutional review before publication and may not reflect the author's complete views [Unverified: the extent of editorial constraint on specific texts varies and is not always disclosed]

---

#### 5.2 Inspector General and Oversight Reports

Declassified oversight documents constitute a primary source for the study of institutional ethics failures:

- **Church Committee Report (1976)** — Senate investigation of CIA, FBI, NSA abuses including assassination plots, domestic surveillance, and COINTELPRO. The foundational document of the democratic accountability critique.
- **Tower Commission Report (1987)** — Iran-Contra affair; addresses the ethics of covert action conducted outside statutory authorization.
- **Senate Intelligence Committee Report on CIA Detention and Interrogation (2014)** — Documents the post-2001 interrogation program; the executive summary is publicly available. The report's findings on efficacy and authorization are disputed by former agency officials.
- **PCLOB (Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board) reports on Section 215 and Section 702 programs** — Post-Snowden legal and ethical assessment of NSA collection programs.

### 6. Academic Journals and Institutional Sources

**Primary journals:**

- _Intelligence and National Security_ — the leading peer-reviewed journal in the field; publishes both historical and ethical analysis
- _International Journal of Intelligence and CounterIntelligence_ — practitioner-oriented but includes ethical dimensions
- _Ethics and Information Technology_ — relevant to surveillance and digital intelligence ethics
- _Journal of Military Ethics_ — applies just war and related frameworks; relevant to covert action and targeted killing
- _Philosophy & Public Affairs_ — where foundational texts (Walzer, Shue, Luban) appear; not intelligence-specific but the source of the most rigorous philosophical work

**Institutional sources:**

- Brookings Institution — policy-oriented analysis of intelligence oversight and covert action
- RAND Corporation — security studies with occasional ethical dimensions
- Georgetown Center for Security and Emerging Technology
- Hoover Institution — tends toward practitioner and security-oriented perspectives; counterpoint to more critical academic work

### 7. Frameworks for Evaluating Specific Operations

Drawing from the literature, the following evaluative criteria appear across multiple frameworks — not as a unified protocol but as a convergent set of questions that any serious ethical analysis must address:

|Question|Framework Origin|
|---|---|
|What is the justifying purpose, and is it legitimate?|Just cause (JWT); consequentialism|
|Are the means proportionate to the end?|Proportionality (JWT, consequentialism)|
|Have less harmful means been considered and found inadequate?|Last resort (JWT)|
|Who bears the costs, and are they the same people who receive the benefits?|Distributive justice|
|Is there meaningful authorization from a legitimate authority?|Democratic accountability|
|Would the operation withstand scrutiny if disclosed?|The "Washington Post test" / publicity principle (Kant, adapted)|
|What is the moral residue if the act is wrong but necessary?|Dirty hands|
|What does this operation do to the character of the institution that conducts it?|Virtue ethics|

**Key Points**

- No single framework answers all questions; the literature reflects genuine pluralism
- The convergence of multiple frameworks on the same conclusion provides stronger grounds than any single framework alone
- Operations that fail multiple criteria simultaneously represent the clearest cases of ethical impermissibility — the hard cases are those where frameworks diverge

### 8. Structural Tensions That the Literature Has Not Resolved

The following are genuine unresolved tensions in the intelligence ethics literature, not questions with accepted answers:

**Secrecy vs. accountability:** Effective intelligence requires secrecy; democratic accountability requires transparency. No framework has resolved this tension; oversight mechanisms are structural compromises, not solutions.

**Universal vs. partial ethics:** Most philosophical ethics is universalist — the same rules apply to everyone. Intelligence practice is inherently partial — it serves a specific state against other states. Whether a universalist ethics can coherently permit intelligence activity at all is unresolved.

**Individual vs. institutional moral agency:** When an intelligence operation produces harm, who is morally responsible — the individual officer, the chain of command, the institution, the state, the elected official who authorized the policy? The literature offers multiple answers without consensus.

**The legality/morality gap:** Many intelligence operations are legal under domestic law but arguably impermissible under ethical analysis. Others may be illegal but ethically defensible. The relationship between law and morality in this domain is a continuing source of debate.

**Exceptional permissions and institutional creep:** Every argument that extraordinary circumstances justify extraordinary means creates a template that can be applied to progressively less extraordinary circumstances. The history of intelligence oversight failures is substantially a history of this dynamic. How to permit genuine exceptions without enabling institutional normalization of those exceptions is unresolved.
