## Detecting Surveillance


Surveillance detection is not primarily about identifying specific surveillance assets. It is about developing and maintaining sufficient situational awareness to recognize that the pattern of your environment has changed in ways that are statistically unlikely to be coincidental. The goal is not certainty — certainty is rarely achievable. The goal is **recognition of anomaly at a threshold that permits informed response**.

This module covers the conceptual framework, perceptual skills, behavioral methodology, and counter-surveillance integration required to detect surveillance in real-world conditions.

---

### Foundational Concepts

#### The Surveillance Detection Problem

Surveillance is designed to be invisible. Professional surveillance operations prioritize **non-detection** over coverage efficiency — meaning a competent surveillance team will accept losing you rather than risk being burned. This has a direct implication: the absence of detected surveillance is not evidence of its absence. It is evidence only that surveillance, if present, has not exceeded your detection threshold.

Your detection capability is therefore defined by two variables:

- **Your awareness baseline** — how accurately you perceive and process your environment under normal conditions
- **Your anomaly threshold** — how much deviation from baseline is required before you register it as significant

Both are trainable. Neither is reliable without deliberate development.

#### What Surveillance Looks Like Operationally

[Inference] Open-source literature on surveillance operations, drawn from law enforcement, military, and security training publications, describes surveillance as typically structured around the following:

- **Fixed surveillance** — stationary observation from a position with sightlines to the target's location, residence, workplace, or route
- **Mobile surveillance** — following a target on foot or in vehicle, typically using multiple assets to reduce individual exposure
- **Technical surveillance** — electronic, photographic, or acoustic collection not requiring physical proximity
- **Layered surveillance** — combinations of fixed, mobile, and technical assets that hand off coverage between themselves

This module focuses on detecting fixed and mobile surveillance, as technical surveillance detection requires specialized equipment and falls primarily under TSCM (Technical Surveillance Countermeasures), which is a separate domain.

#### The Principle of Multiple Indicators

No single observation constitutes evidence of surveillance. Surveillance detection operates on **convergence**: multiple independent anomalies, observed across different locations and time periods, that share a common element — typically a person, vehicle, or behavioral pattern.

One sighting of an unfamiliar face is noise. The same face in two unrelated locations is signal requiring attention. The same face in three unrelated locations over two days is a pattern requiring a response decision.

The threshold for action is not the same as the threshold for awareness. You can note and track an anomaly without acting on it immediately. Premature action — especially overt counter-surveillance behavior — alerts surveillance assets that they have been detected, which has its own consequences.

### Perceptual Foundations

Before methodology, perceptual capability must be developed. Most people operate in a state of **inattentional normalcy** — filtering out environmental information that does not immediately demand response. Surveillance detection requires partially suspending this filter without producing paranoia or unsustainable cognitive load.

#### Baseline Establishment

Every environment has a baseline: the typical density, pace, behavior, and composition of people and vehicles in that space at that time. Anomaly is only detectable against an accurate baseline.

Baseline varies by:

- **Location type** — commercial, residential, transit, institutional
- **Time of day and day of week**
- **Weather and seasonal conditions**
- **Local events** — construction, public gatherings, scheduled activities
- **Cultural norms** — what constitutes normal behavior in this specific environment

A person sitting in a parked car for forty minutes is anomalous in a quiet residential street at 2am. The same behavior in a commercial district during business hours is baseline. The analysis is not of the behavior in isolation but of the behavior relative to its context.

Developing accurate baseline requires deliberate practice: spending time in environments you frequent, observing without agenda, building a working model of what is normal. This cannot be done in the moment of potential surveillance — it must be done in advance.

#### The TEDD Framework

Drawn from open-source surveillance detection training literature. TEDD describes the four axes along which surveillance assets may be identified:

**Time** — the same person or vehicle appearing across an extended time window beyond what chance would explain

**Environment** — the same person or vehicle appearing in multiple distinct environments that you have moved through

**Distance** — the same person or vehicle maintaining a consistent spacing relative to you across movement

**Demeanor** — behavioral indicators inconsistent with the apparent role or context of the person

TEDD indicators are cumulative. One axis alone is weak. Two or more axes on the same asset is significant. All four is a high-confidence indicator.

#### Demeanor Indicators

Demeanor is the axis most frequently overlooked because it requires reading human behavior rather than simply registering presence. Indicators documented in open-source surveillance training material include:

- **Anchor point behavior** — a person who appears to be in a location without a clear purpose for being there; their presence requires a cover rationale they do not seem to be enacting
- **Attention management** — someone who appears to be deliberately _not_ looking at you, especially when your movement or behavior would naturally draw a glance
- **Communication at transition points** — making a phone call or using a radio immediately after you change direction, enter a building, or stop unexpectedly
- **Mirroring your pace** — slowing when you slow, stopping when you stop, in contexts where this is not explained by traffic or crowd density
- **Dry-cleaning behavior** — a subject who appears to be running their own counter-surveillance, checking reflections, doubling back, or scanning behind them; this indicates operational awareness and is itself a surveillance indicator
- **Over-appropriate dress or props** — clothing or accessories that are slightly too well-matched to the cover role; professional surveillance operatives sometimes overcorrect in constructing their appearance

[Inference] Demeanor indicators are the least reliable axis in isolation because many behaviors have innocent explanations. Their diagnostic value increases when combined with Time and Environment indicators on the same individual.

### Surveillance Detection Routes (SDRs)

A Surveillance Detection Route is a planned movement through an environment specifically designed to surface surveillance assets by creating conditions under which they must either expose themselves or lose coverage. It is the primary active method for detecting mobile surveillance.

#### Principles of SDR Design

**Choke points** — locations through which both you and any surveillance must pass, reducing the ability to maintain distance or use parallel routes. Narrow corridors, specific doorways, single-exit buildings.

**Transition variety** — moving between different environment types (open street, enclosed space, vehicle, public transit) forces surveillance to adapt, increasing exposure risk and behavioral tell generation.

**Unexpected direction changes** — not random or erratic, which is itself suspicious, but naturally motivated changes in direction that create brief windows where surveillance must either follow visibly or lose contact.

**Natural stops with observation opportunity** — pausing at locations where it is contextually normal to stop (a shop window, a café, a transit map) while using the position to observe the environment behind you.

**Time pressure variation** — alternating between fast and slow movement, which stresses surveillance spacing.

#### SDR Construction

An effective SDR is built around your actual movement — it should not be obviously artificial. The route serves a plausible purpose. The surveillance-detection elements are embedded within it.

A basic SDR structure:

1. **Departure from origin** — move naturally, establish whether any fixed surveillance exists at the origin point
2. **First transition** — change environment type; observe who makes the same transition
3. **First natural stop** — pause with observation angle; note who adjusts to your stop
4. **Direction change** — alter route naturally; observe who mirrors the change
5. **Choke point** — pass through a controlled environment; note who follows
6. **Second natural stop with longer dwell** — sit in a café, browse a shop; a surveillance asset cannot maintain close proximity indefinitely without a cover reason
7. **Final transition** — return to a different environment; confirm or deny indicators accumulated during the route

An SDR is not a single event. Multiple SDRs over multiple days, in different environments, build a convergent picture. A single SDR tells you about that specific movement. A pattern of SDRs tells you whether surveillance is persistent.

#### What to Do When You Register an Indicator

The instinctive response — confrontation, overt counter-surveillance, dramatic route change — is almost always wrong. It confirms to the surveillance asset that you have detected them, which:

- Removes your information advantage
- May accelerate whatever operational action the surveillance precedes
- Confirms to whoever tasked the surveillance that you are surveillance-aware, which is itself intelligence

The operationally correct response depends on your assessment and context, but general principles from open-source tradecraft literature include:

- **Continue your planned route** — do not signal detection
- **Continue accumulating indicators** — one sighting is not confirmation; continue the SDR
- **Document mentally or physically** — description, location, time, behavior, direction of movement
- **Plan your response separately from the detection phase** — do not make response decisions while still in the surveillance environment

### Fixed Surveillance Detection

Fixed surveillance — a stationary observer watching a location rather than following a person — requires a different detection approach than mobile surveillance.

#### Indicators of Fixed Surveillance

**Vehicle-based fixed surveillance:**

- A vehicle parked in a location for an extended period without apparent purpose
- Occupants who remain in the vehicle without getting out
- Visible antenna configurations inconsistent with civilian vehicles
- Engine running without movement (for climate control during extended operations)
- Windows with reduced visibility from outside (tinted, fogged, angled away)
- Position chosen for sightlines rather than parking convenience — particularly to your residence, workplace, or regular location
- Vehicle repositioning slightly across time to maintain sightlines or avoid notice without actually leaving

**Foot-based fixed surveillance:**

- A person remaining in a location for longer than their apparent purpose requires
- Repeated presence at the same location across different days or times
- A person whose cover activity (reading, phone use, waiting) is sustained for implausibly long periods
- Reaction to your presence — looking away, turning, increased phone activity — specifically when you emerge from or approach your residence or workplace

#### The Exposure Test

One method for testing whether a suspected fixed surveillance position is active: move in a way that would require an observer to respond if they were watching you specifically, rather than the location generally.

- Leave your residence at an unusual time
- Take an unexpected route from a known fixed point
- Enter and exit a building via a different exit than normal

If a vehicle repositions, if foot traffic shifts, if communication behavior changes in correlation with your movement — this is not confirmation but it is a meaningful indicator for the TEDD accumulation.

### Vehicular Surveillance Detection

Vehicle surveillance introduces additional complexity because vehicles are faster, can use parallel routing, and are harder to visually identify across multiple sightings than individuals on foot.

#### Basic Vehicular Indicators

- A vehicle that appears behind you at multiple points across an extended drive, particularly after turns
- A vehicle that was ahead of you and is now behind you without an obvious explanation
- Consistent spacing maintenance across speed changes
- A vehicle that takes the same exit or turn at low-probability decision points

#### The Box Test

A standard open-source counter-surveillance technique. Make four consecutive turns in the same direction (four left turns or four right turns), returning approximately to your origin point. There is no innocent reason for another vehicle to make the same four turns. Any vehicle completing all four turns is a high-confidence surveillance indicator.

Variants: three turns forming a triangle, or a U-turn in a location where U-turns are uncommon. The principle is the same — create a movement with a low probability of being mirrored by chance.

#### Limitations

[Inference] Competent vehicular surveillance teams use multiple vehicles and hand off coverage between them, meaning no single vehicle will be visible across the full route. Detection of a team rather than a single vehicle requires noticing the pattern across vehicles — similar makes, coordinated spacing, communication tells — which requires a higher level of perceptual training than single-vehicle detection.

### Counter-Surveillance vs. Surveillance Detection

These are related but distinct activities:

||Surveillance Detection|Counter-Surveillance|
|---|---|---|
|Goal|Identify whether surveillance exists|Prevent or disrupt surveillance coverage|
|Method|Observation and pattern recognition|Route selection, behavior modification, detection routes|
|Timing|Active during movement|Planned before movement|
|Visibility|Should be invisible to surveillance|May or may not need to be covert|
|Output|Intelligence about surveillance presence|Operational freedom of movement|

Counter-surveillance is what you do _after_ detection, or as a preventive measure. Surveillance detection is the observational and analytical process that precedes any response. Conflating them leads to either premature action or failure to respond.

### Developing the Skill

Surveillance detection is a perishable skill that degrades without practice. It is also a skill that cannot be developed solely through reading — it requires repeated perceptual exposure in actual environments.

#### Practical Development Exercises

**Baseline logging** — spend fifteen minutes in a location you frequent, observing without a specific goal. Log what you see: who is present, where, doing what, for how long. Return to the same location at the same time on a different day and compare. This builds environmental baseline.

**Person tracking** — in a public environment, select an individual and track them at a distance for ten to fifteen minutes without losing them. Note what they do at transition points, how they use space, what their behavioral tells are. This develops the perceptual skills used in reverse — to detect someone tracking you.

**Vehicle logging** — on a regular commute or route, note makes, colors, and partial plates of vehicles behind you at different points. Assess recurrence. This builds the habit of vehicle awareness without requiring active surveillance conditions.

**SDR dry runs** — run a planned SDR on a day when you have no reason to believe you are under surveillance. Observe who is in your environment, log indicators, practice the route structure. Dry runs develop the skill and establish a baseline for comparison when conditions are operationally relevant.

**Reflection windows** — after any movement through a public environment, spend five minutes reconstructing what you observed: who was present, what was anomalous, what you may have dismissed that warranted attention. This develops retrospective awareness and surfaces gaps in real-time perception.

### Threat Calibration

Not all surveillance is equal in source, capability, or intent. Calibrating your response requires assessing:

**Who is likely surveilling and why?** The capabilities of a professional intelligence service differ from those of a private investigator, a criminal organization, or an untrained individual. The indicators, the asset quality, the persistence, and the appropriate response differ accordingly.

**What is the surveillance designed to accomplish?** Collection of information about your pattern of life differs from surveillance designed to precede a physical approach or intervention. The operational tempo and response urgency differ.

**What is your actual exposure?** Surveillance detection tradecraft is appropriate to your actual threat environment. Applying full SDR methodology to a threat that does not exist produces paranoia and unsustainable behavior. Failing to apply it when the threat is real produces vulnerability. Calibrate method to assessed risk.

[Inference] Over-application of surveillance detection behavior in low-threat environments carries its own risks: it is itself anomalous, it consumes cognitive resources needed for other tasks, and it can produce a pattern of hypervigilance that degrades rather than enhances accurate threat assessment.
