## Critical and Postcolonial Perspectives on Diplomacy


### Overview

Critical and Postcolonial perspectives constitute a family of IR approaches united less by a single unified theory than by a shared commitment to interrogating power, knowledge, and historical structures that mainstream (rationalist and even constructivist) IR theory tends to treat as neutral or given. Rather than asking how states pursue power or interest within an anarchic system, these approaches ask whose interests are served by the very concepts, institutions, and practices — including diplomacy itself — that structure the international order. Diplomacy, in this reading, is not a neutral technical practice of managing interstate relations but a historically constituted set of norms, protocols, and hierarchies that emerged from and continues to reproduce asymmetries rooted in colonialism, Eurocentrism, race, gender, and class.

### Intellectual Lineage

- **Critical Theory in IR**: Draws on the Frankfurt School (Horkheimer, Adorno, Habermas), particularly Robert Cox's foundational distinction between "problem-solving theory" (which takes existing power structures as given and works within them) and "critical theory" (which questions how those structures came to be and whose interests they serve) — articulated in Cox's 1981 essay *Social Forces, States and World Orders*
- **Postcolonial Theory**: Rooted in Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), which analyzed how Western knowledge production about the "Orient" constructed the non-West as an object of study, control, and civilizational hierarchy; extended by Gayatri Spivak (*Can the Subaltern Speak?*, 1988) and Homi Bhabha's work on hybridity and mimicry
- **World-Systems and Dependency Theory**: Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems analysis and earlier dependency theorists (Raúl Prebisch, Andre Gunder Frank) provided a structural-economic account of core-periphery relations that postcolonial IR scholars often engage with, though postcolonialism places greater emphasis on discourse, culture, and identity than on economic structure alone
- **Subaltern Studies**: A South Asian historiographical movement (Ranajit Guha and collaborators) that sought to recover the agency and perspectives of marginalized ("subaltern") groups excluded from elite-centered colonial and nationalist historical narratives, later influential in IR's postcolonial turn
- **Convergence into IR**: These strands entered IR proper in the 1990s-2000s as part of the broader post-positivist challenge (alongside constructivism, feminist IR, and poststructuralism) to the discipline's rationalist mainstream, with scholars like Siba Grovogui, Sankaran Krishna, and later Robbie Shilliam pushing explicitly for a "postcolonial IR"

### Cox's Problem-Solving vs. Critical Theory Distinction

**Key Points**

- **Problem-solving theory**: Takes the existing institutional and power framework (the state system, market structures, prevailing norms) as the given context, and focuses on making that framework function more smoothly or efficiently — this describes most mainstream Realist, Liberal, and even Institutionalist scholarship
- **Critical theory**: Stands apart from the prevailing order and asks how it came into being, whose interests it serves, and whether it is subject to transformation — Cox's own famous formulation is that "theory is always for someone and for some purpose"
- This distinction is foundational to why critical and postcolonial scholars treat diplomatic practice itself as an object of critique rather than a neutral tool: diplomatic protocols, the state system, and international law are read as historically contingent arrangements that consolidated during and after European colonial expansion, not as timeless or natural features of international life

### Core Postcolonial Critiques of Diplomacy and the State System

**1. The Colonial Origins of the "Standard of Civilization"**

- 19th-century international law and diplomatic recognition operated through an explicit hierarchy distinguishing "civilized," "barbarian," and "savage" polities (a framework also discussed within English School scholarship, but subjected to sharper critique here)
- Only states deemed "civilized" by European powers were entitled to full sovereign recognition, equal diplomatic treatment, and protection under international law; this framework was used to justify unequal treaties, extraterritorial jurisdiction (e.g., in China and the Ottoman Empire), and colonial domination
- [Inference] Postcolonial scholars argue that vestiges of this hierarchy persist in subtler forms in contemporary practice — for instance, in differential treatment of state legitimacy, differing thresholds applied to "fragile" versus "developed" states in international forums, or asymmetric expectations placed on postcolonial states regarding governance standards

**2. Diplomacy as a Eurocentric Institution**

- Modern diplomatic protocol (permanent embassies, diplomatic immunity, protocol and precedence rules, the language and etiquette of negotiation) developed within the European interstate system (particularly post-Westphalia and the Congress of Vienna, 1815) and was subsequently exported globally, often coercively, through colonial contact and the "expansion of international society"
- Critics argue that non-European diplomatic traditions and practices (e.g., pre-colonial African, South Asian, or East Asian tribute and gift-exchange systems) were displaced or delegitimized rather than incorporated as equally valid alternatives, producing a diplomatic order presented as universal but rooted in one civilizational tradition
- Postcolonial scholars examine how newly independent states, upon gaining formal sovereign equality, were nonetheless required to operate within institutional forms (UN protocol, international law, English as a dominant diplomatic language) not of their own making

**3. Structural Inequality Within Formally Equal Institutions**

- Formal sovereign equality (one state, one vote in the UN General Assembly) coexists with deep structural inequality (permanent UNSC seats with veto power held by former colonial and Cold War powers; unequal voting weights in the IMF and World Bank tied to capital contribution)
- Critical scholars argue this reveals a persistent gap between the formal, juridical equality that decolonization achieved and the substantive equality of voice and influence that remains elusive within existing multilateral architecture

### Comparative Framework: Critical/Postcolonial vs. Mainstream IR Theory

| Dimension | Critical/Postcolonial Perspectives | Neorealism / Institutionalism | Constructivism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| View of theory | Explicitly normative and emancipatory; rejects claim to neutrality | Positivist; claims explanatory neutrality | Positivist (thin) or interpretive (thick), variably normative |
| Object of analysis | Power/knowledge relations, historical structures, marginalized voices | State behavior under material/institutional constraints | Identity, norms, intersubjective meaning |
| Treatment of the state system | Historically contingent, colonially produced, contestable | Given, foundational unit of analysis | Constructed but often treated as a stable ontological category |
| Methodology | Genealogical, discourse analysis, historical-interpretive, often interdisciplinary (literature, history) | Rational choice, positivist causal modeling | Case-study, interpretive, sometimes causal-variable |
| Stance toward diplomacy | Object of critique — a site reproducing hierarchy | Neutral tool/mechanism for managing interstate relations | Constitutive practice shaping identities and norms |

### Key Concepts

- **Orientalism** (Said): The discursive construction of the "East" as fundamentally different from, and inferior to, the "West," producing a body of knowledge that simultaneously describes and dominates its object — applied in IR to critique how Western diplomatic and security discourse frames non-Western states and regions (e.g., persistent tropes about "failed states," "tribalism," or civilizational incompatibility)
- **Subalternity** (Spivak, Guha): The condition of groups excluded from dominant structures of political representation and historical narration; Spivak's provocative question "Can the subaltern speak?" interrogates whether even well-intentioned scholarly or diplomatic efforts to "represent" marginalized voices can escape reproducing the power relations they seek to critique
- **Hybridity and mimicry** (Bhabha): Concepts describing how colonized and postcolonial subjects/states adopt, adapt, and subvert colonial forms (including diplomatic and legal forms) rather than either wholly rejecting or wholly accepting them — producing new, hybrid political forms rather than simple imitation
- **Epistemic violence**: The idea that colonial (and continuing) knowledge production does not merely misrepresent but actively suppresses or delegitimizes alternative ways of knowing and organizing political life
- **Standard of civilization** (shared with, but more sharply critiqued than in, English School scholarship): See above

### Empirical Applications

- **Non-Aligned Movement (1955- )**: Emerging from the Bandung Conference (1955), the Non-Aligned Movement is analyzed as a collective postcolonial diplomatic project explicitly seeking to carve out political space outside the Cold War's bipolar structure and to challenge the residual hierarchies of the existing international order
- **New International Economic Order (NIEO) campaign, 1970s**: A coalition of postcolonial and developing states sought to restructure global economic governance (commodity pricing, technology transfer, terms of trade) — read by critical scholars as a direct challenge to the structural inequalities embedded in the postwar Bretton Woods architecture, and its ultimate defeat as illustrative of the limits formal sovereign equality places on substantive structural change
- **Reform debates over the UN Security Council**: Persistent calls (from African Union member states, India, Brazil, and others) for permanent seat expansion are frequently framed explicitly in postcolonial terms, as a demand to correct a governance structure frozen at a 1945 configuration reflecting WWII victors rather than contemporary global demographic and economic realities
- **Contemporary debates on "decolonizing" international law and diplomacy**: Ongoing scholarly and practitioner discussions about diversifying diplomatic language use (beyond English/French dominance), reforming international legal doctrine's colonial-era foundations (e.g., terra nullius, mandate/trusteeship legacies), and revisiting historical treaty obligations

### Critiques and Internal Debates

- **Fragmentation and lack of unified program**: Because critical/postcolonial IR draws from multiple, sometimes divergent intellectual traditions (Marxism, poststructuralism, subaltern studies, feminist theory), critics — including sympathetic ones — note the difficulty of specifying a single coherent postcolonial IR research program, as opposed to a loosely connected set of critical orientations
- **Charge of insufficient policy relevance**: Mainstream (particularly realist and institutionalist) critics argue these approaches, in prioritizing critique over prescription, offer limited practical guidance for diplomatic practitioners operating within existing institutional constraints — a critique postcolonial scholars often explicitly reject as reproducing precisely the "problem-solving" framing (per Cox) that treats existing structures as unquestionable
- **Essentialism concerns**: Some critics (including scholars within postcolonial studies itself) caution against essentializing "the postcolonial state" or "the Global South" as a homogeneous category, given the vast heterogeneity of historical colonial experiences, current political systems, and material conditions across formerly colonized states
- **Relationship to Constructivism**: Overlaps with constructivist attention to norms and identity, but postcolonial scholars often argue mainstream constructivism itself under-examines colonial history and race as constitutive forces in the emergence of the modern state system, rather than treating this omission as incidental

### Relevance to Diplomatic Leadership

- **Reflexivity about institutional inheritance**: Diplomatic leaders — particularly those representing states or institutions with colonial histories, whether as former colonial powers or formerly colonized states — benefit from understanding how existing diplomatic protocols and multilateral structures carry embedded historical asymmetries, which can inform more self-aware and credible engagement in reform negotiations
- **Coalition-building around structural reform**: Leadership within postcolonial or Global South-oriented diplomatic coalitions (successor efforts to the Non-Aligned Movement and NIEO, e.g., the G77, BRICS-adjacent coalitions) often explicitly invokes this critical framework to build solidarity and articulate shared grievances against existing governance structures
- **Cultural and linguistic sensitivity in negotiation**: Awareness of how diplomatic norms, protocol, and even negotiating language privilege particular historical traditions can inform more genuinely inclusive multilateral practice, including deliberate efforts to diversify representation and procedural norms in international forums
- **Legitimacy and representation claims**: Understanding subalternity and representation debates (Spivak's core question) is directly relevant to diplomatic leaders navigating questions of who speaks for whom in multilateral settings — for instance, debates over indigenous representation, civil society consultation, or representation of non-state and marginalized groups within formally state-centric diplomatic processes
