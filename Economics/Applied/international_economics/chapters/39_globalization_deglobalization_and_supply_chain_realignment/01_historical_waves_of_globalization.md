## Historical Waves of Globalization


### Definitional Scope

Historical waves of globalization refers to the periodization of international economic integration into distinct eras characterized by rising and falling levels of cross-border trade, capital flows, migration, and technological connectivity. Economic historians generally identify multiple distinct waves — typically two major waves (a "first globalization" roughly 1870–1914 and a "second globalization" roughly 1945/1980s–2008/2016) separated by a period of pronounced deglobalization (roughly 1914–1945), with an emerging debate over whether the post-2008 or post-2016 period constitutes a third distinct phase or "slowbalization."

### The First Wave of Globalization (c. 1870–1914)

**Key Points**

- **Trade integration:** Driven substantially by declining transport costs (steamships, railroads) and telecommunications innovation (transatlantic telegraph cable, completed 1866), the ratio of world trade to world GDP rose substantially, with several economic historians (notably Kevin O'Rourke and Jeffrey Williamson in *Globalization and History*, 1999) arguing that trade-to-GDP ratios for some economies did not durably exceed 1913 levels again until the late 20th century.
- **Capital mobility:** Gold-standard-era capital markets exhibited high mobility, with Britain in particular exporting a substantial share of its national savings abroad as foreign investment (notably in railways, government bonds, and resource extraction in the Americas, Australia, and parts of Asia) — some estimates cited in the historical literature place British foreign investment in the range of several percent of GDP annually in the decades before 1914, though [Unverified] the precise figures vary across sources and should be checked against current economic-history references for exact magnitudes.
- **Labor mobility:** Mass migration reached historically unprecedented levels, particularly transatlantic migration from Europe to the Americas; O'Rourke and Williamson's work emphasizes labor mobility as at least as important as goods trade in driving factor-price convergence between the Old World and New World during this period.
- **Gold standard as monetary integration mechanism:** The classical gold standard provided a fixed exchange-rate anchor across major economies, reducing currency risk for cross-border trade and investment, functioning as an institutional analog to today's discussions of monetary integration and currency unions.
- **Colonial and imperial trade structures:** A substantial share of first-wave globalization occurred within formal or informal empire structures (British, French, and other colonial trade networks), meaning integration patterns were shaped as much by political/imperial relationships as by market forces — a structural feature distinguishing this wave from later, more formally multilateral integration.
- **Heckscher-Ohlin-consistent factor price convergence:** Economic historians have used this period as a key empirical testing ground for Heckscher-Ohlin and factor-price-equalization predictions, generally finding substantial (though incomplete) convergence in real wages and land rents between labor-abundant Europe and land-abundant New World economies as trade and migration expanded.

### The Interwar Deglobalization (c. 1914–1945)

- **World War I disruption:** The outbreak of war in 1914 abruptly disrupted trade routes, capital flows, and labor migration patterns that had developed over the preceding four decades, with wartime blockades and requisitioning further fragmenting integrated markets.
- **Gold standard breakdown and restoration attempts:** The interwar period saw an unstable attempt to restore the gold standard (culminating in Britain's 1925 return at an overvalued parity, widely criticized by Keynes at the time) followed by its final collapse amid the Great Depression, removing the monetary-integration anchor that had underpinned first-wave globalization.
- **Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act (1930) and retaliatory protectionism:** The U.S. tariff increase and subsequent retaliatory tariff escalation by trading partners is the canonical example of self-reinforcing protectionist spirals during the Depression, contributing to a sharp contraction in world trade volumes through the early 1930s — though economic historians debate the precise magnitude of Smoot-Hawley's causal contribution relative to the broader monetary contraction and Depression-era demand collapse.
- **Capital controls and financial fragmentation:** The interwar and especially the World War II period saw widespread adoption of capital controls and bilateral clearing arrangements as countries sought to manage balance-of-payments pressures amid financial instability, reversing the high capital mobility of the pre-1914 era.
- **Migration restriction:** Major destination countries (notably the United States, via the Immigration Act of 1924) imposed substantial new restrictions on immigration, curtailing the mass-migration dynamic that had characterized the first wave.

### The Second Wave of Globalization (c. 1945–2008)

**Key Points**

- **Bretton Woods institutional architecture:** The 1944 Bretton Woods conference established the IMF and World Bank and a fixed-but-adjustable exchange rate system anchored to the U.S. dollar (itself convertible to gold), providing a new institutional foundation for reconstructing international economic integration after the interwar collapse.
- **GATT and progressive multilateral trade liberalization:** The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (1947) initiated successive rounds of multilateral tariff reduction among initially a subset of (mostly advanced) economies, culminating in the Uruguay Round (1986–1994) and the creation of the WTO (1995), progressively lowering average tariff levels among participating economies over several decades.
- **Bretton Woods collapse and floating exchange rates (1971–1973):** The U.S. suspension of dollar-gold convertibility in 1971 (the "Nixon Shock") and subsequent transition to floating exchange rates among major currencies marked a significant institutional shift within the second wave, moving from fixed-rate to flexible-rate international monetary arrangements without reversing the broader trend toward trade and financial integration.
- **Post-1980s acceleration ("hyperglobalization"):** Many economic historians and trade economists (notably Dani Rodrik, who coined the term "hyperglobalization") identify a distinct acceleration beginning roughly in the 1980s–1990s, driven by unilateral trade liberalization in many developing economies, the end of the Cold War and integration of former Eastern Bloc and Soviet economies into global markets, China's WTO accession (2001) and export-oriented growth model, containerization revolutionizing shipping logistics, and dramatic declines in telecommunications and computing costs enabling the fragmentation of production into global value chains (GVCs).
- **Rise of global value chains:** Second-wave (and especially post-1980s) globalization is distinguished from the first wave by the fragmentation of production processes themselves across borders (intermediate goods and components crossing multiple borders before final assembly), rather than primarily cross-border trade in finished goods — a structurally distinct form of integration enabled by falling communication and coordination costs alongside falling transport costs.
- **Capital account liberalization:** Many economies progressively liberalized capital controls from the 1970s onward, restoring and eventually substantially exceeding pre-1914 levels of gross international capital flows and foreign direct investment.

### Is There a Third Wave? The "Slowbalization" and Post-2008 Debate

**Key Points**

- **Trade-to-GDP plateau:** Several measures of global trade intensity plateaued or grew markedly more slowly after the 2008–09 global financial crisis relative to the 1990s–2000s acceleration, a pattern the *Economist* magazine and others have termed "slowbalization" — a deceleration rather than a reversal of integration.
- **Global value chain "reshoring" discussion:** Some evidence suggests a partial flattening or reconfiguration of the most extended global value chains after 2008, attributed variously to rising Chinese wages reducing offshoring cost advantages, increased automation reducing the labor-cost rationale for offshoring, and post-2016/2018 trade policy uncertainty (U.S.-China tariff escalation).
- **Post-2016 political shift:** The 2016 Brexit referendum and the 2016–2020 U.S. tariff escalation under the first Trump administration are frequently cited as markers of a political turn against hyperglobalization in several major advanced economies, contributing to renewed interest in whether a genuinely new, more fragmented phase of the international economic order is emerging (connecting directly to the broader "geoeconomic fragmentation" literature, including IMF analyses beginning around 2022–2023).
- **COVID-19 supply chain disruption:** The 2020–2021 pandemic exposed vulnerabilities in highly optimized, just-in-time global value chains, accelerating policy and corporate interest in diversification, friendshoring, and resilience-oriented supply chain redesign discussed in the prior chapter module.
- **Digital globalization as a counter-trend:** Some scholars argue that even as goods-trade intensity plateaus, cross-border data flows, digital services trade, and intangible-asset-based integration (software, streaming, cloud services) have continued to expand rapidly, suggesting globalization may be shifting in *composition* (from goods toward digital/services) rather than uniformly reversing — a distinction increasingly emphasized in the McKinsey Global Institute's globalization research and similar analyses. [Inference] Whether digital integration should be considered a genuine substitute for, or merely a partial offset to, goods-trade deceleration is an interpretive question without full consensus in the literature.
- [Unverified] Whether the current period should be formally classified as a "third wave," a continuation/deceleration of the second wave, or an early-stage genuine reversal remains an open and actively debated question among economic historians and trade economists; current-year trade and GVC data should be consulted for the latest empirical assessment given how quickly this characterization continues to evolve.

### Analytical Frameworks for Interpreting the Waves

- **Rodrik's "trilemma" framework:** Dani Rodrik's political trilemma of the world economy (2011) argues that democracy, national sovereignty, and deep economic integration (hyperglobalization) cannot all be simultaneously maximized — a framework often applied retrospectively to explain why each historical wave of deep integration eventually generated political backlash (the interwar collapse, and arguably the post-2016 populist shift) as domestic political constituencies reasserted sovereignty-based or democratic-accountability-based resistance to integration's distributional consequences.
- **Polanyi's "double movement":** Karl Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* (1944) framework — in which market expansion generates a societal counter-movement demanding protective regulation — is frequently invoked by economic historians to interpret both the interwar backlash against first-wave globalization and contemporary populist and protectionist responses to second-wave/hyperglobalization's distributional effects.
- **Stolper-Samuelson and distributional analysis:** Trade theory's Stolper-Samuelson theorem (relative factor returns shift with trade opening, benefiting the abundant factor and harming the scarce factor in each country) is commonly applied to explain the political economy of backlash in both waves — e.g., relatively lower-skilled labor in advanced economies bearing disproportionate adjustment costs during the post-1990s China-trade-integration phase of the second wave, echoing distributional tensions during first-wave integration as well.
