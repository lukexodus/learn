## Edge Cities and Employment Decentralization

### Overview

Edge cities are large, self-sufficient nodes of office, retail, and employment activity that develop at the periphery of traditional metropolitan cores, typically along highway interchanges, and that in many metropolitan areas rival or exceed the historic downtown in total employment and commercial floor space. Employment decentralization is the broader process by which jobs — not just population — disperse from central business districts (CBDs) toward suburban and exurban locations. This item extends the polycentric city models discussed elsewhere in this chapter by focusing specifically on the empirical phenomenon, defining criteria, causes, and consequences of edge-city formation and the wider decentralization of metropolitan employment.

### Garreau's Definition and Typology

Joel Garreau's *Edge City: Life on the New Frontier* (1991) popularized the term and proposed an informal but widely cited five-part definition of what qualifies as an edge city:

1. Has five million or more square feet of leasable office space (the size of a substantial downtown).
2. Has 600,000 square feet or more of leasable retail space (equivalent to a large regional shopping mall).
3. Has more jobs than bedrooms — i.e., it is a net employment destination, not primarily a residential suburb.
4. Is perceived by the local population as one place, with a distinct identity.
5. Was, as recently as 30 years before its edge-city status, mostly residential or rural — i.e., it is a genuinely new node rather than a historic secondary downtown.

**Key Points**

- Garreau's criteria are descriptive/journalistic rather than derived from formal economic theory, but they map closely onto the more rigorous, statistically identified "employment subcenters" of the academic polycentric-city literature (McMillen, Giuliano & Small), and the two literatures are generally treated as studying overlapping phenomena using different methodological traditions.
- Not every statistically identified employment subcenter meets Garreau's full edge-city criteria (many subcenters are smaller or lack the "more jobs than bedrooms" and self-contained identity features); edge cities represent a particularly large and prominent subset of the broader subcenter phenomenon.

### Historical Trajectory of Employment Decentralization

**Key Points**

- **Pre-automobile era (up to ~1920s)**: employment was overwhelmingly concentrated at rail- and streetcar-accessible central locations, since firms depended on centralized transportation nodes for both worker access and freight/goods movement — density gradients were correspondingly steep (see "Land rent and population density gradients").
- **Early automobile/highway era (1950s–1970s)**: population decentralized to suburbs substantially faster than employment initially, producing a period in which commuting was overwhelmingly inbound from suburb to central-city job (the classic monocentric commuting pattern), even as the underlying AMM density gradient flattened.
- **Employment catch-up (1970s onward)**: manufacturing, retail, and eventually office/service employment progressively followed population to the suburbs, driven by the same falling-commuting-cost and rising-income mechanisms as residential decentralization, plus firm-specific incentives (lower suburban land costs, access to a now-largely-suburban workforce, and highway-based logistics advantages for manufacturing/distribution).
- **Edge city emergence (1980s–1990s)**: by Garreau's account, this employment decentralization reached a critical mass in many metros sufficient to generate genuinely self-sufficient suburban employment/retail/office nodes rather than simple low-density dispersal, coinciding with the identification of substantial suburban employment subcenters in formal empirical studies of the era (Giuliano & Small's Los Angeles study, McDonald & McMillen's Chicago studies).

### Economic Drivers of Employment Decentralization

**Key Points**

- **Falling commuting/transport costs**: the same AMM mechanism driving residential sprawl applies to firms — as highway networks reduced the cost of moving goods and accessing a dispersed suburban workforce, the locational advantage of centralized CBD sites for many industries diminished.
- **Land cost differentials**: suburban/exurban land is typically far cheaper than CBD land, and firms not dependent on face-to-face CBD-specific agglomeration benefits (e.g., back-office operations, distribution/warehousing, light manufacturing, many retail formats) have strong incentives to relocate to lower-cost sites once transport/logistics costs allow.
- **Following the workforce**: as population suburbanized, firms — particularly retail and consumer-service firms whose customer base is local — followed population outward to remain proximate to their market, reinforcing the demand-linkage logic familiar from New Economic Geography's home market effect, applied at the intra-metropolitan scale.
- **Agglomeration at the new site**: once a critical mass of firms establishes at a suburban node (often triggered by a single large anchor employer, shopping mall, or highway interchange development), localized agglomeration economies (labor pooling, input-output linkages specific to the emerging cluster) can generate self-reinforcing growth at that secondary site, following the same circular-causation logic as the Fujita-Ogawa/Lucas-Rossi-Hansberg endogenous subcenter models.
- **CBD congestion and rising central costs**: as central cores became more congested and expensive, the relative cost-benefit calculation for firms without strong CBD-specific agglomeration dependence increasingly favored suburban relocation — this is the demand-side complement to the supply-side land-cost differential above.
- **Telecommunications and reduced face-to-face necessity** (for some functions): advances in telecommunications historically reduced the necessity of centralized, face-to-face-dependent office functions for certain back-office and routine administrative activities, enabling their relocation to lower-cost suburban or even more distant locations — a precursor dynamic to the more recent, larger-scale remote-work phenomenon discussed under knowledge spillovers and the geography of innovation.

### Which Industries Decentralize Most Readily

**Key Points**

- **High decentralization propensity**: manufacturing (space-intensive, logistics-dependent, weak CBD-specific spillover dependence), warehousing/distribution (directly logistics-cost-driven), routine back-office/administrative functions, and most retail (market-access/demand-following logic).
- **Low decentralization propensity (CBD-persistent)**: finance, headquarters functions, legal and professional services, and other activities with strong face-to-face, tacit-knowledge-dependent agglomeration benefits (directly connecting to the knowledge-spillover and localization-economies mechanisms discussed elsewhere in this chapter) have historically remained more concentrated in traditional CBDs, since the proximity-dependent spillover benefits are harder to replicate at a dispersed suburban site.
- This differential decentralization propensity is a major reason many metropolitan CBDs, even as they lost population and routine employment share over the twentieth century, retained or even strengthened their role as specialized centers for finance, headquarters, and high-end professional/creative services — a pattern consistent with the theoretical prediction that industries with the strongest localization/knowledge-spillover dependence should be the most resistant to the general decentralizing forces affecting other sectors.

### Consequences of Employment Decentralization

**Key Points**

- **Complex, multi-directional commuting**: as detailed under polycentric city models, employment decentralization generates reverse commuting (center-to-suburb), cross-suburban commuting, and other patterns inconsistent with the simple inbound-radial commuting of the pure monocentric model, complicating transit and highway infrastructure planning.
- **Spatial mismatch concerns**: the relocation of entry-level and blue-collar jobs to suburban/exurban locations, combined with persistent residential concentration of lower-income and minority populations in central cities and limited public transit access to dispersed suburban job sites, is central to the spatial mismatch hypothesis (Kain, 1968) discussed under urban sprawl, with edge-city-style employment decentralization often cited as a specific empirical manifestation of the underlying spatial mismatch mechanism.
- **CBD specialization and "hollowing out" risk**: as routine employment decentralizes while higher-value, spillover-dependent functions remain concentrated, CBDs in many metros have become increasingly specialized (finance, headquarters, high-end services, tourism/entertainment) rather than broad-based employment centers — a pattern with implications for central-city fiscal health (a narrower, though often higher-value, tax base) and for the resilience of central cities to sector-specific shocks affecting their now-more-specialized economic base.
- **Multiple downtowns / "100-mile city" patterns**: in the most extreme cases, employment decentralization combined with multiple large edge-city nodes produces metropolitan regions without any single dominant employment center, sometimes described informally as "edgeless cities" or "100-mile cities" (a term associated with descriptions of sprawling regions like the Dallas-Fort Worth or Atlanta metro areas), representing a further evolution beyond the classic polycentric (CBD-plus-subcenters) pattern toward a more genuinely dispersed structure.

### Empirical Findings on Edge Cities

**Key Points**

- **Giuliano & Small (1991, 1999)** identified over 30 employment subcenters in the Los Angeles region alone, with the traditional downtown retaining only a modest share of total metropolitan employment, providing one of the most influential early empirical demonstrations of large-scale edge-city-style decentralization.
- **Lang (2003)** proposed a distinction between "edge cities" (Garreau's original, more spatially concentrated concept) and more diffuse "edgeless cities" — sprawling low-density office development not concentrated into a single identifiable node — arguing the latter has become an increasingly common form of suburban office growth in many metros, representing a further stage of dispersal beyond the classic edge city.
- **Persistence of some downtown specialization**: despite substantial employment decentralization, empirical studies generally continue to find measurable CBD wage and rent premiums (consistent with retained agglomeration benefits for CBD-persistent industries), and downtown areas in many large metros have shown employment and population resurgence in specific high-value sectors in recent decades, even as their share of total metropolitan employment remains below mid-twentieth-century peaks.

### Recent Developments: Remote Work and Employment Geography

**[Inference]** The large-scale shift toward remote and hybrid work following the COVID-19 pandemic (2020 onward) represents a potentially significant new phase in employment decentralization, extending the logic beyond suburban edge cities to fully dispersed, location-independent work arrangements for some occupations. However, because the relevant post-pandemic time series remains relatively short as of this writing, the durability, magnitude, and ultimate geographic pattern of this shift (further suburbanization/exurbanization, growth of secondary/smaller metros, or a partial reversion toward central-city presence) is not yet fully established empirically, and the existing literature offers preliminary rather than settled conclusions on this specific development.
