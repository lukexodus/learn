## Local Projections

### Definition and Conceptual Foundation

Local projections (LP) are a method for estimating impulse response functions directly via a sequence of single-equation regressions at each forecast horizon, rather than by inverting a fitted VAR into its moving average representation. Introduced by Jordà (2005), the method estimates the response of a variable $h$ periods ahead directly by regressing the future value of the outcome variable on a shock (or shock proxy) and controls, repeating the regression separately for each horizon $h = 0, 1, 2, \dots, H$.

LPs are often presented as a "model-free" or "flexible" alternative to VAR-based IRFs, since each horizon's response is estimated from its own regression rather than being mechanically implied by a fixed-lag-order VAR structure propagated forward.

### The Local Projection Regression

For horizon $h$, the baseline linear local projection specification is:

$$Y_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h \, \text{shock}_t + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \gamma_{h,i} Y_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \delta_{h,i} X_{t-i} + \eta_{t+h}$$

estimated separately by OLS for each $h = 0, 1, \dots, H$. The coefficient $\hat{\beta}_h$ is a direct estimate of the impulse response of $Y$ at horizon $h$ to the shock at time $t$, holding lagged controls fixed.

**Key Points**

- Unlike VAR-based IRFs, which are generated by iterating a single fitted set of coefficients ($A_1, \dots, A_p$) forward through the VMA recursion, each LP horizon has its own separately estimated coefficient vector, so misspecification or nonlinearity at one horizon does not mechanically propagate errors to other horizons.
- The "shock" regressor can be a raw reduced-form residual, an identified structural shock (from a first-stage SVAR), or an external instrument/proxy — LPs are compatible with the same identification schemes (Cholesky, sign restrictions, external instruments) used in VAR-based SVAR analysis; LP is a method for *estimating* the dynamic response, not itself a method of *identification*.
- Standard errors must account for the serial correlation induced by overlapping, multi-period-ahead forecast errors $\eta_{t+h}$ (since $\eta_{t+h}$ and $\eta_{t+h+1}$ share information); Newey-West (HAC) standard errors are the standard correction, with the bandwidth typically set to at least $h$.

### LP-IV (Local Projections with Instrumental Variables)

When the shock of interest is measured with an external instrument $Z_t$ (as in Proxy SVAR), the LP-IV approach (Stock and Watson, 2018; Ramey, 2016) estimates:

$$Y_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h \, \widehat{\text{shock}}_t + \text{controls} + \eta_{t+h}$$

where $\widehat{\text{shock}}_t$ is obtained via a first-stage regression of the endogenous shock proxy on the instrument $Z_t$, following standard two-stage least squares logic, horizon by horizon. This combines the LP framework's horizon-by-horizon flexibility with the identification power of external instruments.

### Practical Computation Steps

**Example**

Step 1: Choose the shock variable — e.g., an identified monetary policy shock series (from an external instrument such as high-frequency interest rate futures surprises).

Step 2: Specify the baseline control set: typically several lags of all VAR-system variables (output, inflation, interest rate), matching what would appear in a comparable VAR specification.

Step 3: For $h = 0$: regress $Y_t$ (contemporaneous) on the shock and controls; for $h = 1$: regress $Y_{t+1}$ on the *same time-$t$* shock and controls; continue through $h = H$ (e.g., $H = 20$ quarters), re-estimating the regression fresh at each horizon.

Step 4: Collect $\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1, \dots, \hat{\beta}_H$ as the estimated impulse response path.

Step 5: Compute HAC (Newey-West) standard errors for each $\hat{\beta}_h$, with lag truncation increasing in $h$, and construct confidence bands (e.g., 90% or 95%).

**Output**

A typical LP-based IRF plot of output to a monetary policy shock might show a modest, statistically insignificant response at $h=0$–$1$, a gradual decline becoming significant around $h=4$–$8$ quarters, and a return toward zero by $h=16$–$20$, often with visibly wider confidence bands at longer horizons than a comparably specified VAR-based IRF — a commonly noted feature of LP estimates in the literature. [Inference: this qualitative pattern is commonly observed but the specific horizon timing and band widths are highly application-specific.]

### Local Projections vs. VAR-Based IRFs

| Aspect | VAR-based IRF | Local Projections |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Estimation | Single VAR fit, IRF from VMA recursion | Separate regression per horizon |
| Functional form flexibility | Implicitly imposes VAR's fixed dynamic structure across all horizons | Each horizon estimated independently; easier to allow horizon-specific nonlinearity |
| Efficiency (if VAR correctly specified) | More efficient (fewer parameters, shared structure) | Less efficient, especially at long horizons |
| Robustness to misspecification | Misspecification at any lag can propagate/compound across all horizons via recursion | Misspecification at one horizon does not mechanically distort other horizons |
| Standard errors | From VAR coefficient covariance, often bootstrapped | Newey-West/HAC per horizon, generally wider at long horizons |
| Extension to nonlinear/state-dependent effects | More complex (e.g., threshold VAR, Markov-switching VAR) | Comparatively straightforward (interact shock with state indicator) |

**Key Points**

- Simulation evidence (e.g., in Jordà 2005 and subsequent literature) suggests VAR-based IRFs can be more efficient when the true data-generating process is well-approximated by the fitted VAR's lag structure, while LPs can be more robust when the true dynamics deviate from that structure, though the practical size of this trade-off is debated and context-dependent. [Inference: relative performance depends on the true DGP, sample size, and horizon, and no single method dominates in all settings — this remains an active area of methodological research.]
- Plagborg-Møller and Wolf (2021) show that, under a correctly specified linear framework with the same information set and identification, VAR-based IRFs and LP-based IRFs are estimating the *same* underlying structural object asymptotically, differing mainly in finite-sample bias-variance trade-offs and robustness to misspecification, rather than representing fundamentally different economic quantities.

### Nonlinear and State-Dependent Extensions

**Key Points**

- LPs extend naturally to **state-dependent** impulse responses by interacting the shock with an indicator for the economic state (e.g., recession vs. expansion), allowing the estimated response to differ by regime — a common application in Ramey and Zubairy (2018) on fiscal multipliers across states of slack.
- **Smooth transition local projections (STLP)** use a continuous weighting function (e.g., a logistic transition function of a state variable) rather than a hard binary split, allowing gradual transitions between regimes.
- **Quantile local projections** extend the method to estimate the response of different quantiles of the outcome distribution, rather than only the conditional mean, useful for studying asymmetric or tail responses to shocks (e.g., growth-at-risk applications).

### Common Pitfalls

- **Autocorrelated errors uncorrected**: Failing to use HAC standard errors (or using an insufficient lag truncation) for overlapping multi-period forecast errors leads to understated standard errors and overstated significance.
- **Weak instrument problems in LP-IV**: As with Proxy SVAR, a weak first-stage relationship between the instrument and the shock proxy leads to unreliable, high-variance $\hat{\beta}_h$ estimates.
- **Overfitting at long horizons with limited data**: Estimating many horizons in a short sample can produce erratic point estimates at long $h$ due to shrinking effective degrees of freedom relative to the number of parameters across a full set of horizon regressions.
- **Inconsistent control sets across horizons**: Changing the lag structure or control variables across different horizon regressions (rather than holding the specification fixed) undermines comparability of the resulting IRF path.
- **Treating LP and VAR-IRF discrepancies as evidence one method is simply "wrong"**: Differences often reflect finite-sample bias-variance trade-offs rather than one method being invalid; robustness checks comparing both approaches are recommended practice.

### Software Implementation Notes

- **R**: `lpirfs` package (supports linear and nonlinear/state-dependent local projections, including LP-IV).
- **Stata**: `lpirf` (official Stata command for local projection IRFs, introduced in recent Stata releases) and user-written `jorda_lp`-type routines.
- **Python**: No single dominant standard package as of recent versions; commonly implemented manually via `statsmodels.OLS` with HAC covariance (`cov_type='HAC'`) looped across horizons.
- **MATLAB**: Widely used in academic replication code accompanying Jordà (2005), Ramey (2016), and related papers.
