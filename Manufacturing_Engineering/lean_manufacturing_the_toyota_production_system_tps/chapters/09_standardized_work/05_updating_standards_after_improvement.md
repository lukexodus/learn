## Updating Standards After Improvement

### Overview

Updating standards after improvement is the discipline that closes the Standardized Work loop: once a kaizen event, problem-solving cycle, or operator suggestion produces a better method, that new method must be codified into the official Standardized Work documents before the old standard is considered obsolete. Without this step, improvements decay — operators drift back to prior habits, the gain is not sustained, and the "check" and "act" phases of PDCA never actually complete. In TPS, standards are explicitly understood as the current best-known method, not a fixed rule: Taiichi Ohno's principle that "without standards, there can be no kaizen" is a two-way relationship — kaizen improves the standard, and the standard captures kaizen so it does not have to be reinvented.

### Key Points

- A standard is a hypothesis about the best current method, valid only until a better one is proven and documented
- The three core Standardized Work documents — Standardized Work Chart, Standard Work Combination Table, and Standardized Work Sheet — must all be revised together; updating one without the others reintroduces inconsistency
- Takt time, work sequence, and standard-in-process (SWIP) stock are the three elements that define a standard, and any of the three can change as a result of improvement
- The update is not administrative paperwork done after the fact — the revised standard is what makes the improvement real and repeatable
- Failure to update standards is one of the most common reasons kaizen gains are lost within weeks of an event

### Why Updating Standards Matters

An improvement that lives only in the mind of the person who made it, or in a temporary workaround, is fragile. It survives only as long as that person is present, remembers the change, and is not overridden by pressure to "do it the old way because that's how the sheet says to do it." Standardized Work exists precisely to prevent method variation between operators and shifts; if the written standard is not updated, the document itself becomes a source of variation, since some workers will follow the (better) unofficial method and others will follow the (worse) official one.

This connects Standardized Work directly to the PDCA cycle:

- **Plan**: current standard is the baseline; a problem or opportunity is identified
- **Do**: the improved method is trialed, often via a kaizen event or a PDCA experiment at the workstation
- **Check**: the trial is measured against takt time, quality, and safety criteria
- **Act**: if validated, the standard is formally updated, training is conducted, and the new method becomes the baseline for the *next* PDCA cycle

Skipping the "Act" step of formally revising the standard breaks the cycle and prevents the next round of kaizen from having an accurate baseline to improve upon.

### Preconditions for Updating a Standard

Before a standard is revised, the improvement should satisfy several conditions. These are not bureaucratic gates for their own sake — they exist to ensure the new standard is actually better and actually repeatable by anyone, not just the person who invented it.

1. **Validated against takt time** — the new work sequence must reliably complete within takt time across multiple cycles, not just once under ideal conditions
2. **Quality-proofed** — the change must not introduce new defect modes; ideally it incorporates or preserves poka-yoke (error-proofing) devices
3. **Safety-checked** — ergonomic or safety implications of the new motion sequence must be assessed, since Standardized Work sequences are also the basis for job-safety analysis
4. **Repeatable by multiple operators** — the standard must be tested by more than one person; a method that only works for the most skilled operator is not yet a standard
5. **Stable, not a one-off** — the improvement should hold up over enough cycles (commonly a minimum observation period, e.g. several shifts) to rule out coincidental or special-cause performance

[Inference] The specific number of validation cycles or shifts required before formalizing a standard is not universally fixed in TPS literature and is typically set by local quality/engineering procedure.

### The Update Procedure

**Step 1: Capture the current-state standard as the baseline**

Before changing anything, the existing Standardized Work Chart, Standard Work Combination Table, and Standardized Work Sheet must be on hand as the point of comparison. This is also the artifact that will be archived once superseded.

**Step 2: Time and document the new method**

Using a stopwatch or time-study sheet, re-measure the individual element times of the new work sequence, exactly as was done for the original standard. This produces updated data for the Standard Work Combination Table (the chart that layers manual time, walk time, and automatic/machine time against takt time).

**Step 3: Recalculate SWIP (standard-in-process stock)**

If the sequence of operations, the layout, or the number of operators changed, the minimum in-process stock needed to keep the line running without waiting must be recalculated and shown on the Standardized Work Chart.

**Step 4: Redraw the Standardized Work Chart**

The chart's layout diagram (operator walking path, machine positions, quality checkpoints, safety points, and standard WIP quantities overlaid on the physical layout) is redrawn to reflect the new sequence.

**Step 5: Rewrite the Standardized Work Sheet (job instruction detail)**

The Standardized Work Sheet (sometimes distinguished from the Job Instruction Sheet used for Training Within Industry) captures the key points of each step — what to do, how to do it, and *why* it matters (the quality, safety, or technique reasons for doing it a specific way). This is where the tacit knowledge behind the improvement gets made explicit.

**Step 6: Review and sign-off**

Team leader, group leader, and — depending on the scope of the change — engineering or quality sign off that the new standard is approved. This step also formally closes out the change control, so there is a documented "before" and "after" and a decision-maker of record.

**Step 7: Retrain all affected operators**

Every operator who performs the job — not only the one who trialed the improvement — must be retrained on the new standard. This uses the Training Within Industry (TWI) Job Instruction method: prepare the worker, present the operation breaking it into important steps and key points, have the worker perform it while explaining it back, then follow up. Retraining is not optional; a standard that has not been trained to every operator on every shift is not yet in effect.

**Step 8: Post the new standard at the workstation**

The Standardized Work Chart is physically posted at the workstation (visual management) so it is visible to the operator, team leader, and any auditor. The old version is removed or clearly marked superseded, with a revision date and number.

**Step 9: Update the version/revision record**

Every Standardized Work document should carry a revision number and date. The update event — what changed, why, who approved it, and effective date — is logged, often in a kaizen newspaper, an A3, or a change log tied to the process.

**Step 10: Monitor adherence to the new standard**

After rollout, team leaders audit against the new standard (layered process audits or standardized work observation) to confirm the documented method is actually what's happening on the floor, and to catch any drift or unaddressed obstacle to following it.

### Roles and Responsibilities

- **Operator**: performs the current standard, is often the source of the improvement idea, participates in timing/trialing the new method, and provides feedback during retraining
- **Team Leader**: owns the Standardized Work documents for the line, times the new sequence, drafts the updated charts, and conducts or coordinates retraining
- **Group Leader / Supervisor**: reviews and approves changes, ensures the update aligns with broader line balance and staffing (Yamazumi) considerations
- **Engineering / Quality**: validates that the change does not compromise product quality, tooling, or safety compliance, particularly for changes touching fixtures, torque specs, or inspection points
- **Kaizen facilitator (if applicable)**: for changes originating from a formal kaizen event, ensures the standardization step is scheduled as part of the event closeout, not left as a follow-up action that never gets done

### Common Failure Modes

- **The "kaizen event high" problem**: the event produces excitement and a working prototype method, but the team disbands before the paperwork and retraining are completed, and the line reverts within days
- **Partial updates**: the Standardized Work Chart is redrawn but the Standard Work Combination Table is not, so the posted documents contradict each other
- **No retraining of other shifts**: the day shift that ran the kaizen event adopts the new method; night shift or weekend crews are never told, so line-to-line and shift-to-shift variation reappears
- **Standard becomes stale on paper only**: the standard is updated in the document system but the physical posting at the workstation is not replaced, so operators keep referencing the outdated posted version
- **Improvement not tied to takt time**: a change makes the job "easier" or "faster" for the operator without being checked against the actual takt-time requirement, potentially creating new overproduction or starving downstream stations
- **No audit follow-up**: without periodic standardized work observation, undocumented "workarounds" to the new standard can silently reappear, and no one notices until a defect or safety incident traces back to unauthorized deviation

### Relationship to Standardized Work Documents

$$\text{Cycle Time} \leq \text{Takt Time}$$

The update must re-verify this relationship for every element affected by the change. A revised Standard Work Combination Table plots each operator's manual work, walking, and waiting time against the takt time line; if the new method pushes any operator's cycle time above takt, the "improvement" has actually created a bottleneck and is not ready to be standardized.

The three documents work together as a set:

| Document | What it captures | What changes on update |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standardized Work Chart | Layout, operator walking path, machine locations, SWIP, quality/safety symbols | New path, new SWIP quantity, new checkpoint locations |
| Standard Work Combination Table | Manual time, walking time, machine/auto time per element, plotted against takt time | New element times, new sequence order, updated takt-time comparison |
| Standardized Work Sheet / Job Instruction Sheet | Step-by-step key points, the "how" and "why" of each motion | New key points, new reasons (quality, safety, technique) behind each step |

### Example

A welding station currently has a Standardized Work Chart showing a 42-second cycle against a 45-second takt time, with the operator walking a triangular path between two fixtures and a parts rack. During a kaizen event, the team relocates the parts rack closer to the second fixture, cutting the walk distance and reducing element time from 42 to 37 seconds.

Before this becomes the new standard:

1. The team leader times the revised sequence over 20 cycles across two operators, confirming it consistently completes in 36–38 seconds, safely under the 45-second takt time
2. Quality confirms the relocated rack does not obstruct the torque-wrench access point used for the in-process check
3. The Standard Work Combination Table is redrawn with the new element times and walk-time bar
4. The Standardized Work Chart's layout diagram is updated to show the rack's new position and the new (shorter) walking path
5. The Standardized Work Sheet's key-point column is updated to note the new reason for the shortened path (e.g., "walk directly to fixture 2 — rack relocated to reduce travel, no change to torque sequence")
6. Both A- and B-shift operators are retrained using TWI Job Instruction before the new chart is posted
7. The old chart is pulled, the new one posted with a revision date, and the change is logged in the area's kaizen log
8. Two weeks later, the team leader performs a standardized work observation to confirm the 37-second cycle is being followed consistently by all operators on all shifts

### Conclusion

Updating standards after improvement is what converts a one-time gain into a permanent capability. It requires that all three Standardized Work documents be revised together, that the change be validated against takt time, quality, and safety before being formalized, and that every affected operator be retrained — not just the individual who discovered the improvement. Skipping this step is one of the most common reasons kaizen results fail to hold; the update step is not administrative overhead, it is the mechanism by which "kaizen" becomes "standard," setting the new baseline from which the next improvement cycle begins.
