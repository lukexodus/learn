## Normalization of Deviance

### Definition and Scope

Normalization of deviance describes the gradual process by which a practice that deviates from an established standard, procedure, or safety margin becomes accepted as normal within an organization — not because the deviation was formally reviewed and approved, but because it repeatedly failed to produce a bad outcome. The term was coined by sociologist Diane Vaughan in her analysis of the 1986 Space Shuttle Challenger disaster, where engineers' concerns about O-ring performance in cold weather had been progressively discounted after prior flights showed erosion without catastrophic failure.

The mechanism is distinct from a single at-risk decision (see Just Culture Principles in Causation Analysis): it is a *drift over time*, where each individual step away from the standard seems small and is validated by the absence of consequence, until the cumulative distance from the original safety margin becomes large without anyone experiencing it as a discrete decision to accept more risk.

### Key Points

- **Success is misread as safety margin**: Each time a deviation doesn't cause harm, it is interpreted as evidence the deviation is acceptable, rather than as a near-miss that the underlying risk simply did not materialize this time.
- **Deviance becomes the new baseline**: Once tolerated, a deviation is no longer compared against the original standard but against the most recent accepted practice, allowing further drift in the same direction.
- **It is a systemic and social process**, not an individual failing — it typically involves tacit or explicit management awareness, peer reinforcement, and often documentation or reporting that quietly stops flagging the deviation as an exception.
- **It sits structurally between organizational causes and at-risk behavior**: normalization of deviance is often the *mechanism* by which an organizational root cause (understaffing, schedule pressure) becomes routine at-risk behavior at the operational level.

### The Mechanics of Drift

Vaughan's original analysis and subsequent safety research describe a repeating cycle:

1. **A deviation from the standard occurs**, often under pressure (schedule, cost, workload) or due to an ambiguous procedure.
2. **No immediate negative consequence results.**
3. **The deviation is documented or discussed as acceptable "given the circumstances,"** and the review process that would normally flag it becomes less rigorous over repeated instances.
4. **The deviation is repeated**, now treated as within normal limits rather than as a tracked exception.
5. **A new, further deviation becomes easier to justify**, because it is now being compared to the already-drifted baseline rather than the original standard.
6. **The cycle repeats** until either a catastrophic outcome occurs, or an external audit/fresh perspective identifies the drift.

This is often visualized as "the normalization of deviance staircase," where each step down looks small relative to the step before it, but the cumulative distance from the top (the original standard) becomes severe.

### Illustrative Example: The Challenger Disaster

The commonly cited case: O-ring erosion in the shuttle's solid rocket boosters was first observed on an early flight, outside the parameters engineers had originally specified as acceptable. Rather than grounding the fleet to redesign the joint, the observed erosion was treated as an acceptable, "understood" risk because the flight succeeded. Over multiple subsequent launches, increasing degrees of erosion were documented and accepted as within experience, effectively redefining the flight-worthiness criteria based on prior success rather than the original engineering standard. On the fatal launch, unusually cold temperatures — a condition outside even the drifted experience base — caused the O-ring to fail to seal, and the accepted deviation combined with the temperature condition to cause the loss of the vehicle. [Unverified] Some retrospective accounts emphasize that engineers explicitly raised the temperature-related risk the night before launch and were overruled under schedule pressure — the exact internal decision dynamics are documented primarily through post-incident testimony and are subject to some interpretive dispute in the historical literature.

### Diagnostic Indicators of Normalization of Deviance

Investigators look for these signals when assessing whether a root cause involves normalized deviance rather than an isolated at-risk decision:

- **History of near-misses on the same failure mode** that were not escalated as safety concerns
- **A documented standard exists, but current practice systematically differs from it**, without a formal, reviewed change to the standard itself
- **Workers describe the deviation as "how it's always done here"** rather than as a known exception
- **Audits or reviews have become less rigorous over time** for this specific check or step
- **Absence of a discrete decision point**: no single person or meeting can be identified as having "approved" the current practice — it emerged incrementally

### The 5 Whys Applied to Normalization of Deviance

1. **Why** did the failure occur? → A safety check was skipped before the operation began.
2. **Why** was it skipped? → It has not been performed on this line for approximately a year.
3. **Why** did it stop being performed? → It was skipped once during a schedule crunch without incident, and then progressively treated as unnecessary.
4. **Why** did nobody flag the ongoing omission? → Audit checklists were updated to match current practice rather than the original standard, removing visibility into the gap.
5. **Why** were audit checklists updated that way? → (Chain continues into organizational causes: audit function is not independent of the operational unit it reviews, and no mechanism exists to compare current practice against the original engineering baseline rather than the prior audit.)

### Common Pitfalls in Identifying This Root Cause

- **Mistaking normalization for an isolated at-risk decision**: If the same deviation appears repeatedly across time, shifts, or personnel, it is systemic drift, not a one-off choice — the corrective action must differ accordingly (fixing the drift mechanism, not just coaching one individual).
- **Anchoring on the most recent baseline**: Investigators unfamiliar with the original standard may inadvertently accept the drifted practice as the correct comparison point, missing the root cause entirely.
- **Underestimating social reinforcement**: Peer normalization and management's tacit tolerance are often stronger drivers than any individual's risk calculation, meaning individual-level interventions (retraining, warnings) rarely stop the drift on their own.
- **Failing to distinguish absence of harm from absence of risk**: A common cognitive trap where the accumulation of successful outcomes is treated as proof of safety rather than as evidence that the hazard has not yet been triggered by the necessary combination of conditions.

### Corrective Approaches

- **Re-anchor audits to the original standard**, not the currently accepted practice, on a periodic basis independent of the operating unit
- **Treat near-misses as data points on the drift trajectory**, not as isolated non-events
- **Create explicit, visible decision points** for any deviation from a written standard, requiring formal review and sign-off rather than allowing tacit accumulation
- **Encourage outside or fresh-eyes review** (external audits, rotating reviewers) since insiders are subject to the same drifted baseline
- **Address the underlying organizational pressure** (schedule, cost, staffing) that made the first deviation attractive, since removing only the symptom without the pressure invites the drift to recur
