## Influence of Bonding Type on Material Properties


### Fundamental Concept

The type of interatomic/intermolecular bonding present in a material — ionic, covalent, metallic, or secondary (van der Waals, including hydrogen bonding) — is a primary determinant of that material's macroscopic physical, mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties. Because bonding type governs the strength, directionality, and electron behavior at the atomic scale, it directly influences bulk-scale behavior such as melting temperature, stiffness, hardness, ductility, and electrical conductivity.

### Summary of Bond Character by Type

| Bond Type | Electron Behavior | Directionality | Typical Bond Energy | Representative Materials |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ionic | Electron transfer | Nondirectional | ~600–1500 kJ/mol | NaCl, MgO, ceramic oxides |
| Covalent | Electron sharing (localized) | Directional | ~125–1150 kJ/mol | Diamond, Si, polymers (chain bonds) |
| Metallic | Electron delocalization ("sea") | Nondirectional | ~68–850 kJ/mol | Cu, Fe, Al, alloys |
| Secondary/van der Waals | Dipole attraction | Nondirectional, weak | ~4–30 kJ/mol (up to ~51 for H-bonds) | Noble gas solids, interchain polymer bonding, graphite interlayer |

### Influence on Melting Temperature and Bonding Energy

As established through the potential energy-separation curve framework, materials with deep energy wells (high bonding energy $E_0$) require more thermal energy to disrupt the bonded structure and transition to the liquid state, and therefore have higher melting temperatures.

- **Ionic and covalent network solids** (e.g., $\text{MgO}$, diamond) generally exhibit very high melting points due to strong, directional or highly electrostatic bonding
- **Metals** span a wide range depending on the number of delocalized valence electrons per atom — from mercury (liquid at room temperature) to tungsten (~3410°C)
- **Molecular solids and polymers**, bonded internally by covalent bonds but held together intermolecularly only by secondary bonds, generally have low melting or softening temperatures, since it is the weak secondary bonds — not the strong covalent chain bonds — that must be overcome for the material to melt or soften

### Influence on Mechanical Behavior (Hardness, Ductility, Brittleness)

Directionality of bonding is the key factor governing whether a material deforms plastically (ductile) or fractures without significant plastic deformation (brittle):

- **Metallic bonding (nondirectional)**: permits planes of ion cores to slip past one another without breaking bonds, since the electron sea redistributes freely around the shifted ion cores. This is the atomic-scale basis for the characteristic **ductility and malleability** of metals.
- **Covalent bonding (directional)**: bonds exist at fixed angles between specific atoms; displacing atoms from their bonded positions requires breaking bonds rather than merely sliding, which typically results in **brittle** behavior, along with high hardness in network solids such as diamond.
- **Ionic bonding (nondirectional but charge-sensitive)**: although nondirectional, any relative displacement of ion planes tends to bring like-charged ions into close proximity, producing strong electrostatic repulsion that leads to fracture — hence ionic solids are typically **hard and brittle**, despite bond nondirectionality.

### Influence on Electrical Conductivity

Electrical conductivity depends on the availability of electrons free to move under an applied field:

- **Metals**: high conductivity, due to the abundance of delocalized valence electrons in the electron sea
- **Ionic and (most) covalent solids**: generally poor electrical conductors in the solid state, since electrons are localized either transferred to specific ions or shared in specific bonds; ionic compounds do become conductive when molten or dissolved (ionic conduction via ion mobility)
- **Exceptions**: graphite (covalent/layered, but conductive within the basal plane due to delocalized $\pi$-electrons) and silicon/germanium (covalent network semiconductors) illustrate that bonding type is a strong but not absolute predictor of conductivity

### Influence on Thermal Properties

- **Thermal conductivity**: follows a pattern generally similar to electrical conductivity — metals conduct heat efficiently via free electrons, whereas ionic and covalent solids rely primarily on lattice vibration (phonon) transport, which is generally less efficient (though some covalent solids such as diamond are exceptions, having exceptionally high thermal conductivity via efficient phonon transport through their stiff, ordered covalent network)
- **Thermal expansion**: correlates with the shape of the interatomic potential energy curve; materials with deep, symmetric (steep) energy wells (e.g., high-melting covalent and ionic solids) tend to exhibit low thermal expansion coefficients, while materials with shallower, more asymmetric wells (many metals, and especially polymers where secondary bonds dominate expansion behavior) exhibit higher thermal expansion coefficients

### Influence on Optical/Appearance Properties

- **Metallic bonding**: free electrons readily interact with a broad spectrum of incident photons, producing characteristic metallic **luster and opacity**
- **Ionic and covalent solids**: often transparent or translucent in the visible range (e.g., many ceramics, gemstones, glasses) when the electronic band gap is large enough that visible-light photons are not absorbed by electron transitions

### Synthesis: Property-Bonding Correlation Table

| Property | Ionic | Covalent | Metallic | Secondary/van der Waals |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Melting point | High | Very high (network) / low (molecular) | Variable, wide range | Low |
| Hardness | Hard | Very hard (network) / soft (molecular) | Moderate | Very soft |
| Ductility | Brittle | Brittle | Ductile | N/A (typically weak solids) |
| Electrical conductivity (solid) | Poor | Poor (exceptions exist) | Good | Poor |
| Thermal conductivity | Moderate-low | High (network, e.g. diamond) / low | High | Low |

[Inference] While these correlations represent well-established, generally reliable trends used throughout introductory materials science, real materials frequently exhibit **mixed bonding character** (e.g., ceramics with partial ionic/covalent character, or polymers combining covalent backbone bonds with secondary interchain bonds), so predicting a specific material's properties in practice generally requires considering the relative contributions of each bonding type present, rather than assuming a single, pure bonding classification.

### Mixed Bonding in Real Materials

Few real materials exhibit perfectly pure bonding of a single type:

- **Ceramic compounds** (e.g., $\text{SiO}_2$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) typically exhibit a mixture of ionic and covalent character, with the percent ionic character estimable from the electronegativity difference between the constituent elements
- **Semiconducting compounds** (e.g., GaAs) similarly show mixed ionic-covalent bonding
- **Polymers** combine strong intrachain covalent bonding with weak interchain secondary bonding, giving rise to their characteristically low stiffness and melting/softening temperatures relative to metals and ceramics
- **Intermetallic compounds and some alloys** can exhibit bonding with combined metallic and covalent or ionic character
