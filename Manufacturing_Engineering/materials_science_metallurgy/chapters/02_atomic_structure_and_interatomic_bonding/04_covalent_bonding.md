## Covalent Bonding

### Definition and Fundamental Concept

Covalent bonding is a primary interatomic bond formed through the **sharing of valence electrons** between adjacent atoms, rather than the transfer of electrons (as in ionic bonding) or their delocalization across a lattice (as in metallic bonding). Each shared electron pair constitutes one covalent bond, and the shared electrons are considered to belong simultaneously to both bonded atoms, satisfying the stable electron configuration (typically an octet) for each.

This bond type is directional and localized, in contrast to the nondirectional bonding found in ionic and metallic materials. The directionality arises because bonds form along specific orbital orientations between atoms, which has major consequences for the resulting crystal structures and mechanical properties.

### Electronic Basis

Covalent bonds form preferentially between atoms with small differences in electronegativity, and generally between atoms located toward the right-hand side of the periodic table (e.g., Group IVA, VA, VIA elements), where atoms require a modest number of additional electrons to complete their outer shell.

For an atom needing $N'$ valence electrons to achieve a stable octet, the number of covalent bonds, $B$, it can form is:

$$B = 8 - N'$$

For example:

- Carbon (Group IVA, $N' = 4$): $B = 8 - 4 = 4$ bonds → tetravalent, as seen in diamond and methane ($\text{CH}_4$)
- Nitrogen (Group VA, $N' = 5$): $B = 8 - 5 = 3$ bonds, as seen in $\text{NH}_3$
- Oxygen (Group VIA, $N' = 6$): $B = 8 - 6 = 2$ bonds, as seen in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$
- Fluorine (Group VIIA, $N' = 7$): $B = 8 - 7 = 1$ bond, as seen in $\text{F}_2$

**Key Points**

- Each shared electron pair is one covalent bond
- Bonds are highly directional, formed at specific angles between orbitals
- Elemental solids such as diamond (C), silicon (Si), and germanium (Ge) are entirely covalently bonded, forming extended 3D networks
- Molecules like $\text{H}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{F}_2$, $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{HNO}_3$, and $\text{HF}$ are also covalently bonded
- A single compound can exhibit combinations of bonding types; for instance, in a compound like $\text{NaCl}$ bonding is essentially ionic, whereas polymer molecules can have covalent bonds along the chain backbone with weaker secondary (van der Waals) bonding between chains

### Bond Energies and Material Properties

Covalent bond energies and melting temperatures vary substantially depending on the specific atoms involved, generally spanning a wide range:

| Property | Range/Example |
| --- | --- |
| Bonding energy | Roughly 125–1150 kJ/mol |
| Melting temperature | Low (e.g., bismuth, ~270°C) to very high (diamond, decomposes/sublimes above ~3550°C) |
| Hardness | Can be very hard (diamond) or very soft/molecular (many polymers) |

[Inference] The wide spread in bond energy and melting point reflects the strong dependence of covalent bond strength on the specific atomic species, orbital hybridization, and network connectivity (3D network solids like diamond versus discrete molecules like $\text{Cl}_2$), rather than a single characteristic value for "covalent bonding" as a category.

Covalently bonded materials are typically **very hard**, exhibit **high melting points**, and are frequently **brittle**, since the directional nature of the bonds does not permit easy slip of atomic planes past one another, unlike the nondirectional bonding in metals. Many covalent materials (e.g., diamond, many ceramics) also tend to be electrically insulating, since valence electrons are localized in bonds rather than free to conduct, though this is not universal — graphite and silicon are notable covalently-bonded exceptions with significant electrical conductivity or semiconductivity.

### Hybridization and Directionality

The directional character of covalent bonds is explained through orbital hybridization. In carbon, for example:

- **$sp^3$ hybridization**: four equivalent hybrid orbitals arranged tetrahedrally at 109.5° angles — found in diamond and saturated hydrocarbons
- **$sp^2$ hybridization**: three hybrid orbitals in a plane at 120°, with one unhybridized p-orbital forming a $\pi$-bond — found in graphite and alkenes
- **$sp$ hybridization**: two hybrid orbitals at 180°, with two unhybridized p-orbitals — found in alkynes

### Comparison with Other Primary Bond Types

| Feature | Ionic | Covalent | Metallic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Electron behavior | Transferred | Shared (localized) | Delocalized ("sea" of electrons) |
| Directionality | Nondirectional | Directional | Nondirectional |
| Typical hardness | Hard, brittle | Very hard to soft (network vs. molecular), often brittle | Ductile, malleable |
| Electrical conductivity | Poor (solid), good (molten/aqueous) | Generally poor, exceptions exist (graphite, Si) | Good |
| Example materials | NaCl, MgO | Diamond, Si, polymers (chain bonds) | Cu, Fe, Al |
