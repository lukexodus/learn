## Metallic Bonding

### Definition and Fundamental Concept

Metallic bonding is a primary interatomic bond found in metals and their alloys, characterized by the delocalization of valence electrons throughout the entire solid rather than their being shared between specific atom pairs (covalent) or transferred between specific ions (ionic). In this model, metal atoms give up their valence electrons to form a "sea" of electrons that surrounds and permeates a periodic array of positively charged ion cores.

### The Electron Sea Model

In this simplified model:

- Each metal atom contributes its valence electrons to a common electron cloud
- The remaining atomic structure — the nucleus plus non-valence (core) electrons — becomes a positively charged **ion core**
- The free, delocalized valence electrons are not associated with any particular ion core and are free to drift throughout the entire metal
- These delocalized electrons act as a form of "glue" holding the ion cores together through electrostatic attraction between the negative electron sea and the positive ion cores

### Nature of the Bond: Nondirectional Character

Unlike covalent bonding, metallic bonding is **nondirectional** — the electrostatic attraction between the electron sea and the ion cores acts equally in all directions. This has significant structural and mechanical consequences:

- Metal atoms can pack into simple, dense crystal structures (FCC, BCC, HCP) since bond geometry does not constrain specific bond angles
- Ion cores can be displaced relative to one another (e.g., during plastic deformation) without breaking bonds, because the electron sea simply redistributes and continues to bond the shifted planes of atoms
- This nondirectionality is the atomic-scale origin of **ductility and malleability** in metals, in contrast to the brittleness typical of directionally-bonded covalent and ionic solids

**Key Points**

- Valence electrons are delocalized, not associated with any specific atom
- Bonding is nondirectional, unlike covalent bonds
- Bond strength (and consequently melting temperature and elastic modulus) generally correlates with the number of delocalized valence electrons per atom
- Applies to Group IA, IIA elements and, more generally, all metallic elements and their alloys

### Bond Energies and Physical Property Correlations

Metallic bonding energies span a wide range, generally from around 68 kJ/mol (mercury, which is liquid at room temperature) to over 850 kJ/mol (tungsten, which has one of the highest melting points of any metal, ~3410°C). [Inference] This large range reflects differences in the number of delocalized valence electrons per atom and the strength of the resulting electrostatic attraction, though quantitative correlation is complicated by additional factors such as atomic packing and electronic band structure.

| Mercury (Hg) | -39°C | Weak |
| Aluminum (Al) | 660°C | Moderate |
| Iron (Fe) | 1538°C | Strong |
| Tungsten (W) | 3410°C | Very strong |

### Properties Arising from Metallic Bonding

- **Electrical conductivity**: The free electron sea allows electrons to move readily under an applied electric field, giving metals characteristically high electrical conductivity
- **Thermal conductivity**: The same mobile electrons efficiently transport thermal energy, giving metals high thermal conductivity
- **Ductility and malleability**: Nondirectional bonding permits slip of atomic planes without bond rupture
- **Metallic luster**: Free electrons readily absorb and re-emit photons across a broad range of visible wavelengths, producing the characteristic reflective appearance
- **Opacity**: Incident electromagnetic radiation is absorbed by the free electron gas rather than transmitted

### Comparison with Ionic and Covalent Bonding

| Feature | Metallic | Ionic | Covalent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Electron behavior | Delocalized ("sea") | Transferred | Shared (localized) |
| Directionality | Nondirectional | Nondirectional | Directional |
| Electrical conductivity (solid) | High | Poor | Generally poor |
| Mechanical behavior | Ductile, malleable | Hard, brittle | Hard to soft, often brittle |
| Typical elements | Groups IA, IIA, transition metals | Metal + nonmetal | Nonmetal + nonmetal (or Group IVA network) |
