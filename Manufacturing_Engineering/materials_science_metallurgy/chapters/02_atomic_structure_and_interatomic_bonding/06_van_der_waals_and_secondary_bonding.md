## Van der Waals and Secondary Bonding


### Definition and Fundamental Concept

Secondary bonds, also called van der Waals bonds, are weak interatomic/intermolecular bonds arising from atomic or molecular **dipoles**, in contrast to the primary bonds (ionic, covalent, metallic) that arise from valence electron transfer, sharing, or delocalization. A dipole exists whenever there is some separation of positive and negative charge within an atom or molecule. Bonding results from Coulombic attraction between the positive end of one dipole and the negative end of an adjacent dipole.

Secondary bonds are:

- **Much weaker** than primary bonds, typically on the order of only ~4–30 kJ/mol (roughly one to two orders of magnitude weaker than primary bonds)
- Present **between virtually all atoms and molecules**, but their effects may be overshadowed if any of the three primary bonding types is present
- Evidence of their existence is clearest in noble (inert) gases, which have stable, filled valence electron shells and therefore no interatomic primary bonding — yet still condense to liquid and solidify, which is only explainable by secondary bonding forces

### Origin: Dipole Formation

An electric dipole exists when there is some separation of positive and negative charge on an atomic or molecular scale. There are two broad origins for the required dipoles:

1. **Induced (fluctuating) dipoles** — arise from the fact that electron distributions around atomic nuclei are constantly changing with time; at any instant there may be a short-lived, small concentration of electrons on one side of the nucleus relative to the other, creating a small, momentary electric dipole. This dipole then induces a similar, aligned dipole in an adjacent atom, and the two are attracted to one another. This is the mechanism responsible for the liquefaction and, at sufficiently low temperature, solidification of the noble gases and other electrically neutral, symmetric molecules such as $\text{H}_2$ and $\text{Cl}_2$.
2. **Permanent dipoles** — occur in polar molecules due to the asymmetrical arrangement of positively and negatively charged regions, as a result of bonding involving atoms of different electronegativity. Polar molecule-induced dipole bonds result from a polar molecule inducing a dipole in a nearby nonpolar molecule. Polar molecule-polar molecule bonds (also called **dipole-dipole bonds**) occur between two adjacent polar molecules that are permanently oriented so as to have their unlike charge ends in proximity, producing attractive forces stronger than fluctuating-induced dipole bonds.

**Key Points**

- Fluctuating dipole bonds are the weakest of the secondary bond types
- Permanent dipole bonds (polar molecule-polar molecule) are generally stronger than fluctuating dipole bonds
- Hydrogen bonding is the strongest type of secondary/van der Waals bond

### Hydrogen Bonding: A Special Case

Hydrogen bonding is classified as a special type of secondary/van der Waals bond, and it is significantly stronger than the other categories — bond energies can reach up to about 51 kJ/mol, approaching the lower end of primary bond energies in some cases.

Hydrogen bonding occurs specifically when hydrogen is covalently bonded to a highly electronegative element, most notably **fluorine (F), oxygen (O), or nitrogen (N)**. Because the H–F, H–O, or H–N bond is highly polar (the small hydrogen atom essentially has no electron shield and effectively exists as a bare proton), this hydrogen end of the molecule represents a highly positively charged region with no electron shielding. This exposed, essentially unshielded proton is then strongly attracted to the electron cloud (lone pair) of an electronegative atom in an adjacent molecule, forming the hydrogen bond between molecules.

The classic example is **water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)**: hydrogen bonding between adjacent water molecules is responsible for many of water's anomalous properties, including its relatively high boiling point for such a low molecular weight molecule, and the fact that ice is less dense than liquid water (due to the open hydrogen-bonded lattice structure in the solid state).

### Comparison of Secondary Bond Types by Strength

| Bond Type | Mechanism | Relative Strength | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fluctuating induced dipole | Instantaneous, random dipole induces neighbor dipole | Weakest | Solid noble gases (Ar, Ne), $\text{H}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$ |
| Polar molecule-induced dipole | Permanent dipole induces dipole in adjacent nonpolar molecule | Intermediate | HCl dissolving/interacting with nonpolar species |
| Polar molecule-polar molecule (dipole-dipole) | Permanent dipoles align, attracting oppositely charged ends | Stronger | $\text{HCl}$–$\text{HCl}$ interactions |
| Hydrogen bonding | H bonded to F, O, or N; unshielded proton attracts electronegative atom on adjacent molecule | Strongest secondary bond | $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{HF}$, DNA base pairing |

### Role in Molecular and Polymeric Materials

Secondary/van der Waals bonding plays a critical structural role in materials where **primary covalent bonds exist within a molecule or chain**, but only weak secondary bonds exist **between** adjacent molecules or chains:

- **Polymers**: covalent bonds hold atoms together along the polymer chain backbone, while intermolecular/interchain bonding is by van der Waals and/or hydrogen bonding. [Inference] This combination — strong intrachain covalent bonds paired with weak interchain secondary bonds — is generally understood to account for the comparatively low melting/softening temperatures, low elastic moduli, and general mechanical weakness of many polymers relative to metals and ceramics, since bulk deformation and flow occur primarily by chains sliding past one another via rupture of the weak secondary bonds rather than the covalent bonds themselves.
- **Layered materials such as graphite**: strong covalent (sp²) bonding exists within each carbon layer (basal plane), while only weak van der Waals bonding holds adjacent layers together — this is why graphite cleaves easily along the layers and is useful as a solid lubricant, despite the individual layers being extremely strong.
- **Molecular solids**: many organic crystals are held together entirely by secondary bonds, resulting in low melting points and low mechanical strength compared to ionic, covalent network, or metallic solids.
