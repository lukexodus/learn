## Seven Quality Management and Planning Tools


### Overview

The Seven Quality Management and Planning Tools (also called the "Seven New QC Tools" or "Seven Management Tools") are a complementary toolkit to the Seven Basic QC Tools, developed in Japan during the 1970s by the JUSE (Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers) committee. Where the basic seven tools analyze numerical, quantitative process data, the new seven tools address qualitative, verbal, and organizational problems — planning complex projects, structuring ambiguous problems, and building consensus among cross-functional teams. In a metrology and QC context, they are most heavily used in new product introduction (NPI), FMEA planning, calibration program design, and root-cause investigations that involve organizational rather than purely statistical complexity.

**Key Points**

- Developed by the JUSE Committee (led in part by Shigeru Mizuno) specifically to address management and planning problems the original seven QC tools could not
- Oriented toward qualitative/verbal data rather than numerical measurement data
- Frequently used together in sequence during the Plan phase of PDCA, particularly for complex, multi-stakeholder problems
- Foundational to structured new-product quality planning frameworks such as APQP (Advanced Product Quality Planning) in automotive metrology

### 1. Affinity Diagram

A brainstorming and organizing technique in which a large number of ideas generated by a team are grouped into natural clusters (affinities) based on their relationships, without pre-defined categories.

- Process: individual ideas written on cards/sticky notes, then silently sorted into groups by the team, with cluster headers assigned afterward
- Metrology application: organizing a large, unstructured list of potential causes of measurement uncertainty gathered from multiple inspectors into thematic clusters (e.g., environmental factors, fixturing issues, gauge calibration issues, operator technique)
- Distinguishes itself from a cause-and-effect diagram by not imposing predetermined categories (like the 6M's) before grouping begins

### 2. Relations Diagram (Interrelationship Digraph)

Maps cause-and-effect relationships among multiple interconnected factors when the relationships are too complex or multidirectional for a simple fishbone diagram.

- Arrows drawn between related items show direction of influence; items with the most outgoing arrows are typically root drivers, and items with the most incoming arrows are typically key effects
- Metrology application: mapping how calibration interval, environmental control, operator training, and gauge age interrelate to influence overall measurement uncertainty — useful when causes influence each other, not just the final effect

### 3. Tree Diagram

Systematically breaks down a broad goal or problem into progressively finer levels of detail, similar to a work breakdown structure (WBS).

- Structure: root goal on the left/top, branching into major means, then sub-means, down to actionable tasks
- Metrology application: decomposing a broad objective like "reduce overall measurement uncertainty in the CMM lab" into major branches (environmental control, calibration program, operator certification, fixture design), each further decomposed into specific action items

### 4. Matrix Diagram

Displays the relationship between two or more sets of factors in a grid, with symbols indicating the strength of relationship at each intersection.

- Common form: L-shaped matrix (two factor sets); also T-shaped, X-shaped, and Y-shaped variants for three or more factor sets
- Metrology application: this is the structural basis of the **House of Quality** in Quality Function Deployment (QFD), relating customer requirements (rows) to engineering/measurement characteristics (columns), with relationship strength indicated by symbols (e.g., ◎ strong, ○ moderate, △ weak)

**Example (simplified L-matrix)**

| Customer Requirement \ Measured Characteristic | Bore Diameter | Surface Finish | Roundness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Smooth assembly fit | ◎ | ○ | ◎ |
| Long service life | ○ | ◎ | ○ |
| Low noise operation | △ | ◎ | ○ |

### 5. Matrix Data Analysis (Prioritization Matrix)

A quantitative refinement of the matrix diagram in which factors are weighted and scored numerically to rank options objectively — often considered the most statistically rigorous of the seven new tools.

- Commonly implemented via the **Analytical Criteria Method**: criteria are weighted (e.g., cost, impact on measurement accuracy, implementation time), each option scored against each criterion, and weighted scores summed to rank options
- Metrology application: prioritizing which of several candidate corrective actions for a chronic gauge drift issue to implement first, based on weighted criteria of cost, expected uncertainty reduction, and implementation lead time

$$\text{Weighted Score}_i = \sum_{j} w_j \cdot s_{ij}$$

where $w_j$ is the weight of criterion $j$ and $s_{ij}$ is option $i$'s score against criterion $j$.

### 6. Process Decision Program Chart (PDPC)

Maps a planned sequence of activities alongside potential failure points or obstacles at each step, along with pre-planned countermeasures — essentially a proactive risk-mapping technique conducted before problems occur.

- Structurally resembles a tree diagram, but each branch node also documents "what could go wrong here" and "what we do if it does"
- Metrology application: planning the rollout of a new CMM programming standard across multiple production lines, mapping potential failure points at each rollout step (e.g., "operator resists new probe qualification sequence" → countermeasure: refresher training session) — conceptually parallel to risk mitigation planning in an FMEA, but applied to project/process execution rather than product design

### 7. Arrow Diagram (Activity Network Diagram)

A project-scheduling tool, derived from Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT) and Critical Path Method (CPM), that maps the sequence and dependencies of tasks required to complete a project, identifying the critical path — the longest sequence of dependent tasks determining minimum project duration.

- Metrology application: scheduling a facility-wide calibration system re-qualification project, sequencing tasks such as equipment audit, standard procurement, technician certification, and validation testing, with the critical path identifying which task sequence cannot slip without delaying the overall project

### Seven Basic Tools vs. Seven Management Tools

| Aspect | Seven Basic QC Tools | Seven Management/Planning Tools |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data type | Quantitative/numerical | Qualitative/verbal |
| Typical user | Shop floor operators, inspectors | Cross-functional planning teams, engineers |
| Primary phase | Check (analysis of existing data) | Plan (structuring new initiatives) |
| Origin era | 1950s–60s | 1970s (JUSE) |
| Typical output | Statistical evidence of a process condition | Structured plan, prioritized action list, or schedule |

### Integration with Metrology Program Planning

[Inference: while JUSE's original tools were developed for general TQM contexts, their heaviest documented modern adoption is within automotive and aerospace APQP/PPAP frameworks, where matrix diagrams underpin QFD and arrow diagrams underpin program timing charts — this pattern is well-established but the degree of adoption varies by industry sector.] In a metrology program specifically, the matrix diagram (via QFD) is most directly relevant when translating customer/regulatory requirements into specific measurable characteristics and associated gauge/inspection method selection, while the arrow diagram governs the scheduling of calibration lab re-qualification and equipment procurement projects.
