## Deep Sea Mining and Emerging Extraction Frontiers


### Overview: A New Extraction Frontier

Deep sea mining targets mineral deposits on the ocean floor — primarily in international waters — as a supply diversification frontier distinct from both terrestrial mining and midstream refining. The energy transition is driving up demand for nickel, cobalt, and manganese, with agencies projecting at least a doubling of demand over the next two decades, while supply chains for these minerals remain concentrated in a handful of countries, making importing nations wary of chokepoints. Deep sea resources are viewed by policymakers and firms as a hedge: a way to diversify sources of critical minerals for clean energy and military defense applications, entirely independent of existing terrestrial geopolitical concentration (whether Chinese midstream dominance or DRC/Indonesia mining concentration).

**Key Points**

- Deep sea mining is not yet a source of supply — it remains pre-commercial, with realistic commercial-scale operation estimated for 2027–2028 at the earliest.
- The primary geopolitical tension is jurisdictional: whether extraction in international waters is governed by the International Seabed Authority (ISA) under UNCLOS, or by unilateral national permitting regimes — the United States has chosen the latter path.
- Three distinct deposit types exist, each with different mineral profiles, extraction methods, and technical maturity.

---

### Deposit Types

**1. Polymetallic Nodules**

Potato-shaped rocks composed of concentric layers that form over millions of years as minerals from seawater and sediment pore water accrete around a hard nucleus (e.g., a shark tooth, whale ear bone, or rock fragment) lying on the deep seafloor. Found scattered across abyssal plains such as the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (CCZ) in the Pacific, typically four to six kilometers beneath the surface. Nodules contain cobalt, copper, lithium, manganese, nickel, tellurium, titanium, and some metallic rare earth elements.

**2. Polymetallic Sulfides**

Large deposits made up of sulfur compounds and other metals that form around hydrothermal vents on the seafloor — geologically distinct from nodules, forming through mineral-rich fluid venting rather than slow accretion.

**3. Metal-Rich Crusts (Seamounts)**

Cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts that form on the flanks and summits of underwater mountains (seamounts), a third distinct geological setting with its own extraction engineering challenges due to the hard, rocky substrate compared to loose nodules on sediment.

---

### The Clarion-Clipperton Zone (CCZ): Primary Focus Area

The CCZ is a vast abyssal plain administered by the ISA, spanning 1.7 million square miles in international waters between Hawaii and Mexico, and is earmarked for mining by 17 contractors under ISA licenses. The zone is estimated to contain more copper, cobalt, nickel, and manganese than all known land deposits combined, though the economic recoverability of nodules from the CCZ remains unknown.

As of January 31, 2026, the ISA had issued 31 exploration contracts to public and private mining enterprises for seabed mineral resources, including 17 exploration contracts specifically for polymetallic nodules in the CCZ.

### Extraction Technology (Standard Architecture)

Commercial nodule collection systems generally follow a common architecture, synthesized from publicly disclosed contractor system designs, since detailed proprietary engineering specifics are not fully published:

1. **Seafloor collector vehicle:** A tracked or buoyant collector unit moves across the seabed, using hydraulic or mechanical means (water-jet suction or mechanical scooping) to lift nodules from the sediment surface while minimizing sediment plume generation — the latter being a major environmental concern.
2. **Vertical riser/lift system:** Collected nodules are transported via a riser pipe (often using airlift or pumped slurry transport) from the seafloor (multiple kilometers deep) to a surface support vessel.
3. **Surface processing vessel:** Nodules are dewatered, and sediment/water is typically returned to depth (rather than discharged at the surface) to reduce surface-water ecological disturbance — though discharge depth and plume management remain contested regulatory and scientific issues.
4. **Transport to shore-based processing:** Raw nodules are shipped to onshore facilities for metallurgical processing (smelting or hydrometallurgical leaching) to extract nickel, cobalt, copper, and manganese.

[Inference] Current nodule lifting tests have only been conducted at relatively small volumes, and the full system still needs to be tested at scale, meaning a ramp-up period is unavoidable before true commercial-volume throughput is demonstrated.

### Regulatory Landscape: Two Competing Governance Tracks

**Track 1 — International Seabed Authority (ISA) / UNCLOS Framework**

The ISA is an autonomous organization established under UNCLOS that regulates and controls mineral-related activities in Areas Beyond National Jurisdiction (ABNJ), with a mandate to ensure the effective protection of the marine environment from harmful effects that may arise from deep-seabed-related activities. Parties to UNCLOS are members of the ISA. Despite more than a decade of negotiation, the ISA and its membership have been unable to agree on rules governing commercial mining operations in international waters — meaning no finalized "mining code" exists as of 2026, even though exploration contracts have been issued for years.

**Track 2 — Unilateral U.S. Domestic Framework**

The United States has not ratified UNCLOS and therefore is not a full ISA member (it holds observer delegate status), though it has followed ISA standards in the past. In 2026, the U.S. has pursued an independent regulatory path:

- Following President Trump's April 2025 executive order, "Unleashing America's Offshore Critical Minerals and Resources," the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) and NOAA have taken steps to facilitate commercial deep sea mining both within the U.S. exclusive economic zone (EEZ) and in international waters beyond national jurisdiction.
- Domestic seabed mining in U.S. federal waters is regulated by the Department of the Interior/BOEM under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA).
- International-water mining by U.S. entities proceeds under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act (DSHMRA), for which NOAA finalized a modernized, streamlined permitting rule.
- This jurisdictional approach directly overlaps and conflicts with ISA authority, since the U.S. is issuing its own permits for areas that may also fall under ISA licensing — the core geopolitical friction point of 2026 deep sea mining policy.

### Downstream Processing Gap

A critical structural weakness mirrors the terrestrial midstream bottleneck: critics point out that the U.S. currently has no facilities to process deep sea nodules at scale, meaning that even successful extraction would require either new domestic refining capacity (such as the proposed Glomar/Cobalt Blue refinery) or reliance on existing (often foreign) processing infrastructure — potentially reproducing the same midstream concentration risk that deep sea mining is meant to help escape. [Inference] This suggests deep sea mining's strategic value depends as much on parallel refining investment as on the extraction technology itself, consistent with the broader pattern seen in terrestrial critical minerals supply chains.

---

### Environmental and Political Controversy

- Deep sea mining companies argue that harvesting nodules causes less environmental damage than mining for critical minerals on land, a claim contested by conservation groups and marine scientists given the poorly understood ecology of abyssal plain ecosystems.
- Sediment plume generation, noise pollution, and disruption of slow-forming benthic ecosystems (nodules themselves take millions of years to form) are central environmental concerns; sustainability will hinge on system design, monitoring, and whether large-scale operations can demonstrate materially lower environmental and social impact compared to terrestrial mining.
- The U.S. unilateral approach has drawn criticism that it substantially cuts down on environmental review relative to the multilateral ISA process, and conflicts with the internationally negotiated governance model that most other nations (including China, which already holds ISA exploration permits) continue to operate under.
- **Pacific geopolitics:** Because the CCZ sits between Hawaii and Mexico and affects Pacific fishing and indigenous maritime interests, Pacific Island nations occupy an outsized stake in governance debates — Nauru, for example, was the first developing state to sponsor a polymetallic nodule exploration contract in the ISA's Reserved Area system, reflecting how smaller Pacific states have used ISA sponsorship provisions to gain a stake in seabed mineral development.

---

### Strategic Significance for Supply Chain Geopolitics

- Deep sea mining represents a potential long-run hedge against terrestrial mining and refining concentration, but remains speculative and pre-commercial as of 2026 — it should be understood as an emerging frontier under active geopolitical contestation rather than a near-term supply source.
- The U.S.-China dynamic extends to the seabed: China already holds ISA exploration permits under the multilateral framework, while the U.S. pursues a parallel unilateral track — creating a potential future scenario of competing, non-mutually-recognized claims to overlapping seabed areas. [Inference]
- Japan's active bilateral engagement (both with the U.S. and independently via DORD-GSR) reflects the same resource-insecurity logic seen in Japan's urban mining and recycling policy: a resource-poor industrial economy pursuing every available diversification lever, including frontier extraction technologies still years from commercial maturity.
