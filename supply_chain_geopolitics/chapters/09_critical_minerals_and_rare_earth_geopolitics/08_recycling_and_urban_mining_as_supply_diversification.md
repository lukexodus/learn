## Recycling and Urban Mining as Supply Diversification


### Overview: The Concept of "Urban Mining"

Urban mining is the process of recovering critical minerals — particularly rare earth elements — from anthropogenic stocks: the waste already produced by society, rather than from newly extracted ore. In practice this means recovering neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnets and other REE-bearing materials from hard drives, EV motors, wind turbine generators, MRI machines, and discarded consumer electronics. Recycling and urban mining sit at the intersection of the mining and midstream stages: they bypass the primary extraction step entirely and feed reclaimed material either directly into magnet manufacturing (short-loop) or into separation/refining processes (long-loop), making them a structurally different — and in some ways faster — diversification path than building new mines or refineries from scratch.

**Key Points**

- Recycling does not require new mining permits, ore-body discovery, or beneficiation infrastructure — its primary constraints are collection logistics, sorting technology, and processing scale-up.
- China's dominance is heavily concentrated in *primary* mining and refining; recycling infrastructure outside China is comparatively less encumbered by decades of incumbent advantage, making it a strategically attractive diversification lever for the US, EU, Japan, and allied states.
- The clearest signal that recycling has moved into mainstream supply chain planning is MP Materials' agreement with Apple, alongside major recycling lines being built at Mountain Pass as part of a broader vertical-integration strategy.

### Why Recycling Matters Strategically: The Byproduct/Balance Problem

Unlike ordinary metals, rare earths are always mined as a mixture of multiple elements in fixed natural ratios (light REEs like Ce, La, Nd, Pr occur in far greater abundance than heavy REEs like Dy, Tb, Ho). Demand, however, is skewed toward specific elements — particularly Nd, Pr, Dy, and Tb for high-strength permanent magnets. This creates a structural "balance problem": mining enough Dy/Tb to meet magnet demand produces a large oversupply of low-value light REEs as a byproduct.

- [Inference] Recycling partially escapes this balance constraint because end-of-life magnets already contain concentrated Nd-Pr-Dy-Tb in the ratios actually used by industry, rather than the ratios nature provides — making urban mining disproportionately valuable for the *heavy* rare earths that are hardest to source and separate from primary ore.
- This is a key reason policymakers treat recycling as strategically important beyond its raw tonnage contribution: it directly targets the scarcest, most geopolitically leveraged elements.

### Recovery Pathways: Three Technical Routes

Three distinct pathways exist for recovering rare earth content from end-of-life magnets, differentiated by feedstock quality tolerance, chemical intensity, and resource efficiency.

**1. Direct Short-Loop Processing (Magnet-to-Magnet)**

The best-known example is **Hydrogen Processing of Magnet Scrap (HPMS)**, developed at the University of Birmingham, which exposes NdFeB magnets to hydrogen gas at room temperature and atmospheric pressure, causing them to crumble into a demagnetized alloy powder, with nickel and copper coatings peeling away mechanically rather than requiring separate stripping.

Process sequence:

1. Disassembly/extraction of magnets from end-of-life devices (hard drives, motors, compressors).
2. Hydrogen decrepitation: $\text{Nd}_2\text{Fe}_{14}\text{B} + \text{H}_2 \rightarrow \text{hydride phases (powder)}$ — the magnet absorbs hydrogen, which expands the crystal lattice and causes the material to fracture into fine powder without dissolving it chemically.
3. Powder is de-hydrogenated, blended/adjusted for alloy composition, and re-sintered into new magnets.

This route achieves 90%+ recovery rates without the use of harsh mineral acids, and is the most resource-efficient pathway when feedstock is clean, sorted, and predominantly NdFeB.

**2. Hydrometallurgical Processing**

Used when magnets cannot be cleanly removed from a device. Scrap is shredded and the rare earth content dissolved in acid or solvent, then separated oxide by oxide — more chemically intensive than HPMS but tolerant of mixed, contaminated feedstock. This route mirrors primary-ore solvent extraction chemistry (see the midstream processing/refining bottleneck topic) but starts from a REE-enriched scrap concentrate rather than raw ore, often requiring fewer separation stages because the feed is already magnet-derived rather than a full 17-element natural mixture.

**3. Pyrometallurgical Smelting**

Handles the most heterogeneous waste streams but produces higher solid waste volumes and is the most resource-intensive of the three routes. Reserved for the hardest-to-sort material where mechanical or hydrogen-based separation is not viable.

**Example — Cyclic Materials' Two-Stage Platform:**

Cyclic Materials combines two proprietary technologies: MagCycle™, which mechanically separates and recovers magnet-bearing materials from end-of-life motors, electronics, and manufacturing scrap, and REEPure™, a hydrometallurgical refining process that converts the recovered magnet concentrate into recycled mixed rare-earth oxides, with increasing capability to reach high-purity materials suitable for direct alloy and magnet manufacturing. Cyclic's recycled mixed rare-earth oxides are shipped to Solvay for downstream separation and purification — illustrating that even successful mechanical/hydrometallurgical recovery often still depends on a midstream separation partner to reach final purity.

### Policy and Regulatory Landscape (2026)

**European Union — Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA):**

The most significant driver for rare earth recycling in 2026 is the full implementation of EU CRMA benchmarks; by early 2026, new restrictions on the export of permanent magnet scrap have been introduced specifically to keep high-value recyclable material within local value chains rather than being exported as low-value scrap.

**Japan — Urban Mining (*toshi kozan*) Policy:**

Japan's rare earth recycling programme, domestically termed "urban mining," is coordinated through JOGMEC (Japan Organization for Metals and Energy Security), which administers policy and manages strategic stockpiles. Japan's Environment Ministry allocated ¥37.9 billion ($238 million) in its fiscal 2026 budget to fund collection, processing, and extraction infrastructure for rare earths and rare metals across the full recovery chain — from collection at retired EV motors and air-conditioning compressors, through transport and quality testing, to element-specific extraction.

- [Inference] Japan's emphasis on recycling policy is disproportionately strong relative to its economic size because it holds no significant primary rare earth mining capacity and depends on Chinese-sourced imports for more than 60% of its rare earth supply, making circular domestic recovery one of the few diversification levers fully within its own policy control.
- A four-company consortium (Daikin, Hitachi, Shin-Etsu, and a fourth partner) represents Japan's first commercial magnet recycling loop, alongside a dedicated France-based facility targeting dysprosium and terbium recovery from magnet scrap, designed to supply approximately 20% of Japan's projected future heavy rare earth demand starting from late 2026.

**United States and Allied Private Capital:**

Recycling has attracted substantial strategic and institutional capital, reflecting its treatment as a national-security-adjacent supply diversification tool rather than a purely environmental initiative. Cyclic Materials' investor base includes T. Rowe Price, Microsoft's Climate Innovation Fund, Amazon, and the Canada Growth Fund.

### Technology Readiness: Promise vs. Scale-Up Reality

A more cautious counterpoint to industry press coverage comes from academic literature review. A University of L'Aquila systematic review of 51 studies, published in *Recycling* (April 2026), finds that while recycling rare earths from e-waste is technically feasible — especially from magnets and lighting — most technologies remain at proof-of-concept stage (TRL 3–4), far from commercial deployment. The review further notes that hydrometallurgical methods achieve over 90% recovery rates in lab settings, but fragmented processes, inconsistent metrics, and an inability to handle mixed real-world waste streams prevent scaling to meet Western policy targets such as 25% recycled supply.

**Key Points**

- There is a meaningful gap between headline industry announcements (commercial-scale claims from named companies) and the broader academic assessment that the *field as a whole* remains lab-stage for many process variants — both can be true simultaneously, since a handful of well-funded first-movers can be commercial while the underlying science base is still maturing. [Inference]
- Magnets dominate the recycling research base because NdFeB scrap is the richest and most accessible REE source relative to other REE-bearing waste streams (phosphors, batteries, catalysts).
- Automated AI sorting — high-speed robotics used to identify and extract tiny REE-bearing sensors and magnets from complex e-waste streams — is cited as a 2026 innovation addressing what was previously a labor-intensive, cost-prohibitive constraint on feedstock collection.

### Feedstock Availability and Long-Term Trajectory

EV and wind turbine end-of-life pathways are more structured than consumer electronics waste, since vehicles pass through dismantling facilities and wind turbines are decommissioned by their operators, creating natural collection points that consumer e-waste (scattered across households) lacks. Industry analysts project that by 2035, the volume of magnet rare earths available for recycling from end-of-life EVs alone could exceed 15,000 tonnes annually — enough to meaningfully supplement, though not replace, primary supply.

- [Inference] This creates a structural timing dependency: recycling volumes scale with the *installed base* of EVs and wind turbines reaching end-of-life, meaning meaningful recycled-supply contribution lags roughly one product-lifetime (10–20 years) behind the initial deployment boom of those technologies — a boom that itself only accelerated over the past decade. Recycling therefore complements but does not substitute for near-term primary mining and refining investment.
