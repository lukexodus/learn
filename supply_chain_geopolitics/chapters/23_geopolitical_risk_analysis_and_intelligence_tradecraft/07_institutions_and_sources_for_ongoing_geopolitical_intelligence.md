## Institutions and Sources for Ongoing Geopolitical Intelligence


### Overview

Sustaining a geopolitical risk function requires a curated portfolio of ongoing institutional sources — think tanks, multilateral organizations, government publications, commercial intelligence vendors, and academic research centers — that collectively provide the raw analytical inputs feeding indicator monitoring, scenario construction, and assessment writing. No single source is sufficient; source diversification across institutional type (governmental, multilateral, commercial, academic, journalistic) mitigates the risk of inheriting a single institution's analytical blind spots or biases.

### Multilateral and Governmental Institutions

**International organizations**:

- **World Bank** — Worldwide Governance Indicators, Logistics Performance Index, country economic and development reporting
- **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** — Article IV consultations providing detailed sovereign economic assessments, World Economic Outlook geopolitical risk commentary
- **United Nations bodies** — UN Comtrade (trade statistics), UN Panel of Experts reports (sanctions-evasion and conflict-financing investigations for specific sanctioned states), UNCTAD (trade and investment reporting)
- **World Trade Organization (WTO)** — trade policy reviews, dispute settlement records, notification database for new trade measures

**National government sources**:

- **U.S. Treasury OFAC** — sanctions designations (SDN list), general licenses, FAQs on sanctions program interpretation
- **U.S. Commerce Department BIS** — Entity List updates, Commerce Control List, export control determinations
- **U.S. intelligence community public products** — the annual Worldwide Threat Assessment (unclassified testimony to Congress), National Intelligence Council reports
- **UK OFSI, EU sanctions authorities** — parallel sanctions/export control regimes relevant for firms with EU/UK exposure
- **National statistical and customs agencies** — for granular trade flow data specific to jurisdictions of interest

### Think Tanks and Research Institutions

**General geopolitical/foreign policy focus**:

- Council on Foreign Relations (CFR), Chatham House (Royal Institute of International Affairs), Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), Brookings Institution, RAND Corporation, International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS — publishes the widely referenced annual Military Balance)

**Supply chain / economic security specialization**:

- Center for a New American Security (CNAS) — technology and economic security programs
- Peterson Institute for International Economics — trade policy and sanctions economics analysis
- Atlantic Council — GeoEconomics Center, focused specifically on the intersection of geopolitics and economic/financial systems

[Inference] Firms building a source portfolio often find greater practical value in specialized economic-security-focused think tank output than in generalist foreign policy commentary, given the direct relevance to supply chain and trade exposure — though the appropriate mix depends heavily on a given firm's specific sector and geographic footprint.

**Conflict and security-specific monitoring**:

- Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) — georeferenced conflict event data, widely used as a quantitative input for conflict-risk indicators
- International Crisis Group — conflict prevention-focused analysis with strong regional depth
- Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) — arms transfers, military expenditure data

### Commercial Intelligence and Risk Vendors

**Political/country risk ratings and advisory**:

- Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU), Control Risks, Verisk Maplecroft, S&P Global Market Intelligence country risk services, Eurasia Group (notably strong in political risk scenario/forecasting work), Oxford Analytica

**Supply chain and trade-specific data**:

- Panjiva/S&P Global (trade data), Kpler and Windward (maritime/commodity flow intelligence), Everstream Analytics and Resilinc (supply-chain-specific risk monitoring platforms integrating multi-source event data directly into supplier risk dashboards)

**Sanctions and compliance screening**:

- Dow Jones Risk & Compliance, Refinitiv World-Check, LexisNexis Risk Solutions — typically embedded directly into procurement/KYC workflow tooling rather than consulted as standalone research sources

### Academic and Semi-Academic Sources

- University-affiliated research centers (e.g., Belfer Center at Harvard Kennedy School, Center for International Security and Cooperation at Stanford) — often provide deeper methodological rigor and longer publication lead times than think tanks, valuable for foundational/structural analysis rather than fast-moving monitoring
- Peer-reviewed journals (International Security, Foreign Affairs — the latter more policy-commentary than strictly peer-reviewed) for longer-horizon strategic trend analysis

### Journalistic and News Sources

**Key Points**

- Distinguishing wire services (Reuters, AP, Bloomberg, AFP) from analysis/opinion outlets matters methodologically — wire services provide faster, more source-disciplined factual reporting suited to indicator monitoring, while analysis outlets provide interpretive context better suited to assessment-writing background
- Regional and local-language press access (directly or via translation/monitoring services) is frequently the weakest link in corporate source portfolios, despite often carrying the earliest signals of developments that later reach international wire coverage
- Specialized trade press (e.g., shipping industry publications like Lloyd's List, sector-specific trade journals) provides granular operational detail unavailable in general news coverage

### Building a Source Portfolio: Selection Criteria

- **Coverage fit** — does the source's geographic/sectoral specialization match the firm's actual exposure footprint, rather than defaulting to generalist "prestige" sources that may not cover the specific jurisdictions relevant to the firm's supply chain
- **Update frequency vs. depth tradeoff** — combining fast-moving wire/commercial monitoring sources with slower, deeper think tank/academic sources for structural understanding, mirroring the same triangulation principle applied to geopolitical risk indices
- **Independence and bias awareness** — understanding each institution's funding sources, national/political orientation, and any documented track record of bias, without necessarily excluding a source but calibrating appropriate skepticism (directly connecting to the Quality of Information Check SAT)
- **Cost/access tier** — commercial vendor subscriptions can be significant recurring costs; portfolio design should match subscription tier to the firm's actual risk exposure scale rather than defaulting to the most comprehensive (and expensive) available package

### Example: Source Selection for a Firm with Southeast Asian Manufacturing Exposure

**Applied portfolio**:

- **Governmental**: Relevant national trade/customs agencies, U.S. BIS Entity List monitoring (for any dual-use technology exposure), ASEAN Secretariat trade policy publications
- **Think tank**: Regional-specialist institutions with dedicated Southeast Asia programs, supplemented by CSIS/Chatham House for great-power dynamics affecting the region
- **Commercial**: Maritime tracking vendor for regional chokepoint monitoring (relevant straits), country risk ratings subscription covering the specific manufacturing jurisdictions
- **Journalistic**: Regional English-language press plus translation-monitoring service for local-language outlets in manufacturing-hub countries, supplementing international wire coverage that often under-covers hyper-local developments

**Conclusion**

No fixed "best" source list exists independent of a firm's specific geographic and sectoral exposure; effective source portfolio construction is an exercise in matching institutional source type, update cadence, and specialization depth to the firm's actual risk footprint, then maintaining deliberate diversity across institutional categories to guard against any single source's blind spots propagating unchallenged into the firm's risk assessments.
