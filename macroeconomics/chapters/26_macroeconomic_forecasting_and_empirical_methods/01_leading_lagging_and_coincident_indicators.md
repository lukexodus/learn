## Leading, Lagging, and Coincident Indicators


### Overview

Economic indicators are statistical time series used to assess the current state and likely future direction of economic activity relative to the business cycle. They are classified by their **timing relationship** to the reference cycle (typically aggregate output or a composite business cycle chronology) into three categories: **leading**, **coincident**, and **lagging**. This taxonomy underlies much of applied macroeconomic forecasting, including the composite index methodology developed by the NBER and popularized by The Conference Board.

### Conceptual Classification

**Key Points**

- **Leading indicators**: turn before the aggregate business cycle turns; used to anticipate future economic activity.
- **Coincident indicators**: move contemporaneously with the business cycle; used to identify the current phase of the cycle.
- **Lagging indicators**: turn after the business cycle has already turned; used to confirm that a cyclical turning point has occurred.

$$\text{Lead Time} = t_{\text{indicator turn}} - t_{\text{reference cycle turn}}$$

A negative lead time indicates a leading series (the indicator turns before the cycle); a positive value indicates lagging.

### Historical and Institutional Background

The classification system originated with **Wesley Mitchell and Arthur Burns** at the NBER in the 1930s-1940s, who empirically catalogued hundreds of series by their historical timing at business cycle peaks and troughs. **Geoffrey Moore** later refined and formalized these into composite indices. Today, **The Conference Board** maintains and publishes the **Composite Index of Leading Economic Indicators (LEI)**, the **Coincident Economic Index (CEI)**, and the **Lagging Economic Index (LAG)** for the United States, following methodology originally developed by the U.S. Department of Commerce.

### Leading Indicators

**Key Points**

Leading indicators reflect decisions or conditions that precede changes in aggregate output, employment, and income — often because they capture expectations, financial conditions, or early-stage production decisions.

Common components (illustrative, U.S. context):

- **Average weekly hours, manufacturing**: firms adjust hours before hiring/firing.
- **Average weekly initial claims for unemployment insurance**: rises before layoffs show up in aggregate unemployment.
- **Manufacturers' new orders for consumer goods and materials**: signals future production.
- **ISM New Orders Index**: survey-based measure of forward manufacturing demand.
- **Manufacturers' new orders for nondefense capital goods**: proxies future business investment.
- **Building permits for new private housing units**: precedes construction activity by months.
- **Stock prices (e.g., S&P 500)**: forward-looking asset prices capitalize expected future earnings.
- **Leading Credit Index**: captures financial market stress/ease.
- **Interest rate spread (10-year Treasury minus federal funds rate)**: yield curve slope, a classic recession predictor.
- **Average consumer expectations for business conditions**: survey-based sentiment (e.g., University of Michigan, Conference Board Consumer Confidence).

**Example**

An inverted yield curve (short-term rates exceeding long-term rates) has historically preceded U.S. recessions by 6 to 24 months, though the lead time and reliability vary across cycles. [Unverified — the yield curve's predictive lead time is not fixed and has shown weaker signal strength in some recent cycles due to unconventional monetary policy effects on the term premium.]

### Coincident Indicators

**Key Points**

Coincident indicators move together with aggregate economic activity and are often used by the NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee to identify peaks and troughs.

Common components:

- **Nonfarm payroll employment**: broad measure of labor market conditions.
- **Personal income less transfer payments**: real income generated by current production.
- **Industrial production index**: physical output of manufacturing, mining, and utilities.
- **Manufacturing and trade sales**: real final sales activity.

The NBER dating process is qualitative and holistic — it does not rely on a single coincident index or a mechanical rule such as "two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth," but rather examines multiple coincident series jointly. [Unverified — the exact weighting the NBER committee applies to each series is not a published fixed formula and involves judgment.]

### Lagging Indicators

**Key Points**

Lagging indicators confirm cyclical turning points after they have occurred, often because they reflect delayed adjustment processes (contracts, inventories, balance sheets).

Common components:

- **Average duration of unemployment**: rises after a recession has begun and stays elevated after recovery starts.
- **Inventories-to-sales ratio**: firms adjust inventories with a lag relative to demand shifts.
- **Change in unit labor costs**: wage contracts adjust with a lag to output changes.
- **Average prime rate charged by banks**: monetary policy transmission lag.
- **Commercial and industrial loans outstanding**: credit cycle typically lags the real cycle.
- **Consumer price index for services**: services inflation is typically sticky and lags demand shifts.
- **Ratio of consumer installment credit to personal income**: household leverage adjusts with a lag.

### Constructing a Composite Index

Composite indices aggregate multiple component series into a single index using a standardized methodology:

1. **Compute symmetric percentage changes** for each component to avoid asymmetric treatment of increases/decreases:



   $$\Delta x_t = \frac{x_t - x_{t-1}}{0.5(x_t + x_{t-1})}$$
2. **Standardize** each component's changes by dividing by its own historical volatility (to equalize each series' contribution/volatility weight):



   $$\tilde{x}_{i,t} = \frac{\Delta x_{i,t}}{\sigma_i}$$
3. **Weight and aggregate** the standardized changes into a composite growth rate, often equally weighted or weighted by an economic-significance criterion.
4. **Cumulate** the composite growth rates into an index level, typically normalized to 100 in a base period.

### Evaluation Criteria for Selecting Indicators

Following the Moore-Shiskin criteria historically used by the NBER/Conference Board, a good indicator should exhibit:

- **Economic significance**: a plausible theoretical link to the business cycle.
- **Statistical adequacy**: reliable measurement, minimal revisions, adequate sample coverage.
- **Consistent timing**: stable lead/lag relationship across multiple historical cycles.
- **Smoothness**: not excessively volatile (or volatility must be manageable via smoothing).
- **Prompt availability**: published with minimal reporting lag, ideally monthly or higher frequency.

### Signal Extraction and Turning Point Rules

**Key Points**

To avoid false signals from short-term noise, practitioners commonly apply the **"three consecutive declines" rule**: a sustained downturn in the LEI is typically flagged when the index declines for three consecutive months, though this is a heuristic rather than a strict statistical test.

$$\text{LEI signal} = 
\begin{cases}
\text{Downturn warning} & \text{if } \Delta \text{LEI}_t < 0 \text{ for } t, t-1, t-2 \\
\text{No signal} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$$

More formal approaches include:

- **Markov-switching models** (Hamilton, 1989) that probabilistically classify the economy into expansion/recession regimes based on the joint behavior of coincident series.
- **Diffusion indices**: the percentage of component series that are rising, providing a breadth measure independent of magnitude.

$$\text{Diffusion Index}_t = \frac{\text{Number of rising components}}{\text{Total number of components}} \times 100$$

### Worked Numerical Example

**Example**

Suppose a leading index has five equally weighted standardized components with the following month-over-month changes: +0.8, -0.3, +1.2, -0.5, +0.4.

Diffusion index:

$$\text{Diffusion} = \frac{3}{5} \times 100 = 60\%$$

Composite change (simple average):

$$\bar{\Delta} = \frac{0.8 - 0.3 + 1.2 - 0.5 + 0.4}{5} = \frac{1.6}{5} = 0.32$$

A positive composite change alongside a diffusion index above 50% suggests broad-based expansion signals rather than a narrow, single-sector-driven movement.

### Limitations and Critiques

**Key Points**

- **False positives/negatives**: leading indicators have historically signaled recessions that did not materialize ("cried wolf"), notably around certain mid-cycle slowdowns. [Unverified — frequency of false signals varies by index vintage and time period; consult Conference Board historical validation studies for specifics.]
- **Structural instability**: the lead time and reliability of any given indicator can shift as the structure of the economy changes (e.g., declining share of manufacturing reduces the informativeness of manufacturing-based components over time).
- **Revisions**: many underlying data series are revised after initial release, which can alter historical readings of the composite index retroactively.
- **Endogeneity to policy**: financial variables (e.g., yield curve) partly reflect central bank policy reactions themselves, complicating pure "leading" interpretation.
- **Global vs. domestic indicators**: in open economies, foreign demand and capital flow indicators may be as informative as domestic composite indices, which the traditional U.S.-centric framework does not fully capture.

### Applications in Forecasting Practice

**Key Points**

- Central banks and finance ministries use leading indicator composites as one input (alongside structural models and nowcasting) for near-term GDP forecasts.
- **Nowcasting models** (e.g., dynamic factor models, the New York Fed's Nowcast) extend the coincident-indicator logic by extracting a common factor from many mixed-frequency series in real time.
- Private-sector economists use diffusion indices (e.g., ISM Manufacturing PMI, which is itself both a diffusion index and a widely tracked leading indicator) as high-frequency, low-lag substitutes for slower official statistics like GDP.
