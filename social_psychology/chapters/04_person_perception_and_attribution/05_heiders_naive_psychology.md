## Heider's Naive Psychology

### Overview

Heider's naive psychology, introduced by Fritz Heider in his 1958 book *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations*, is the foundational theoretical framework proposing that ordinary people function as intuitive, informal scientists who observe behavior and attempt to infer its underlying causes. Heider argued that everyday people—not just professional psychologists—construct implicit, common-sense theories about why others act as they do, and that these naive causal analyses systematically shape social perception, judgment, and behavior. This framework became the direct theoretical ancestor of modern attribution theory.

### Core Premise: The "Naive Scientist" Metaphor

**Key Points**

- Heider proposed that people are motivated to understand and predict their social environment in order to achieve a sense of control and cognitive stability.
- To do this, ordinary perceivers engage in causal analysis of others' behavior, much as a scientist analyzes data to identify causes, even though this analysis is informal, intuitive, and often biased.
- This everyday causal reasoning was termed "naive psychology" or "commonsense psychology" to distinguish it from formal scientific psychology, while still treating it as a legitimate and important object of psychological study in its own right.
- Heider's central claim was that behavior is perceived as caused by a combination of factors residing in the person (dispositional) and factors residing in the environment (situational), and that perceivers attempt to disentangle these two sources when interpreting actions.

### Personal vs. Impersonal Causality

Heider distinguished between two broad categories of perceived causation:

- **Personal causality**: Behavior attributed to an intentional agent's own disposition, motives, or effort—the person is seen as the origin of the action.
- **Impersonal causality**: Outcomes attributed to environmental or situational forces (e.g., luck, task difficulty, other people's interference) rather than to a person's intentional action.

This distinction became the conceptual seed for the later, more formalized **dispositional vs. situational attribution** dichotomy that dominates modern attribution theory.

### The Analysis of Action: Can, Try, and Intend

**Key Points**

- Heider proposed that perceivers analyze others' behavior using an implicit causal formula involving several component judgments:
  - **Effective personal force**: The combination of a person's power (ability) and their motivation (trying) that determines whether they can produce an outcome.
  - **Ability**: Whether the person has the capacity to perform the action.
  - **Trying**: Composed of two sub-elements—**intention** (what the person wants to achieve) and **exertion** (the effort applied).
  - **Environmental force**: Situational facilitators or obstacles that interact with personal force to determine the actual outcome.
- Heider expressed this relationship informally as: outcome depends on the interaction of personal force (ability × trying) and environmental force (task difficulty, luck, situational constraints).
- This model implies that perceivers must judge not just whether someone acted, but whether they *intended* to act, *tried* to achieve the outcome, and had the *ability* to succeed, before assigning a dispositional attribution.

### Levels of Responsibility Attribution

Heider also proposed a developmental/graded scheme describing how perceivers assign responsibility to an actor for an outcome, later elaborated by researchers such as Shaw and Sulzer. Broadly, responsibility attributions can be organized from lowest to highest perceived personal responsibility:

1. **Association**: The person is merely connected to the outcome in some way, with no causal contribution (e.g., simply being present).
2. **Causality (simple)**: The person caused the outcome but could not have foreseen it.
3. **Foreseeability**: The person could have foreseen the outcome, even if it wasn't intended.
4. **Intentionality**: The person intended the outcome and acted to bring it about.
5. **Justifiability**: The person's intentional action is further evaluated considering situational pressures or justifying circumstances (added in later refinements).

[Unverified] The exact number and labeling of these levels differ slightly across secondary sources and subsequent scholarly refinements of Heider's original ideas, as Heider's own writing was less formally systematized than later interpretive summaries suggest.

### Balance Theory: A Related Contribution

**Key Points**

- Beyond causal attribution, Heider also developed **balance theory**, which addresses how people strive for cognitive consistency in their attitudes toward other people and objects.
- Balance theory models triadic relationships between a perceiver (P), another person (O), and an attitude object or third person (X), using positive (+) or negative (−) sentiment relations.
- A triad is "balanced" when the product of the three relationship signs is positive (e.g., P likes O, O likes X, so P should like X); imbalance creates psychological tension that motivates attitude change to restore balance.
- Although distinct from causal attribution, balance theory shares Heider's core assumption that people seek cognitive consistency and predictability in their understanding of the social world.

### Legacy: Foundation for Modern Attribution Theory

**Key Points**

- Heider's naive psychology is widely regarded as the origin point of formal attribution theory, directly inspiring:
  - **Jones and Davis's Correspondent Inference Theory** (1965), which specifies conditions under which an observed behavior is inferred to correspond to an underlying stable disposition.
  - **Kelley's Covariation Model** (1967), which formalizes Heider's implicit "scientist" metaphor into a systematic model using consensus, distinctiveness, and consistency information.
  - **Weiner's Attribution Theory of Motivation and Emotion**, which extended causal dimensions (locus, stability, controllability) to achievement contexts.
- The person-vs-situation distinction central to Heider's work remains the organizing dichotomy underlying later phenomena such as the **fundamental attribution error** (the tendency to over-attribute others' behavior to disposition while underweighting situational factors) and the **actor-observer asymmetry**.

### Relevance to Person Perception and Attribution

Heider's framework is foundational to the entire chapter topic of person perception and attribution because it established:

- The core assumption that ordinary perceivers actively and continuously engage in causal reasoning about others, rather than passively registering behavior.
- The dispositional/situational distinction that underlies virtually all subsequent attribution research.
- The recognition that perceived intentionality, effort, and ability are not directly observable but must be inferred, making attribution an inherently constructive, inference-laden process susceptible to systematic errors and biases.
- A bridge between cognitive (causal reasoning) and motivational (consistency-seeking via balance theory) accounts of social perception.

### Conclusion

Heider's naive psychology reframed the study of everyday social cognition by proposing that ordinary people act as intuitive causal analysts, constantly parsing others' behavior into personal and impersonal sources of causation using implicit judgments of ability, intention, and effort. Though informal and pre-theoretical relative to later models, Heider's ideas about personal versus impersonal causality, the analysis of action, and cognitive balance directly seeded the more rigorous and testable attribution frameworks that followed, cementing his work as the conceptual bedrock of the entire attribution theory tradition in social psychology.
