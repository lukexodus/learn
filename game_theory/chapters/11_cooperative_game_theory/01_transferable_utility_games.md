## Transferable Utility Games


### Definition and Conceptual Overview

A transferable utility (TU) game is the foundational model of cooperative game theory in which players can form coalitions, each coalition generates some total value, and — critically — that value can be **freely redistributed among the coalition's members** in any way they agree upon, typically via a numeraire good (e.g., money) that enters every player's utility linearly. The "transferable utility" assumption is what allows cooperative game theory to abstract away from individual utility functions and focus purely on the **characteristic function**: a single number assigned to each possible coalition representing the total worth that coalition can guarantee for itself.

This transferability assumption is a substantive modeling simplification — it rules out situations where utility is not freely exchangeable across players (e.g., non-monetary, non-fungible outcomes), which instead require the more general and more complex framework of **non-transferable utility (NTU) games**.

**Key Points**

- A TU game is formally defined by a pair $(N, v)$: a finite player set $N = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ and a **characteristic function** $v: 2^N \to \mathbb{R}$ assigning a real number to every subset (coalition) of players
- Convention: $v(\emptyset) = 0$
- The central question of TU cooperative game theory is not "what should each player do" (as in non-cooperative game theory) but rather **"how should the total value $v(N)$ generated by full cooperation be allocated among the players"**
- Solution concepts (the Core, the Shapley value, the Nucleolus, and others) provide different principled answers to this allocation question, each embodying different fairness or stability criteria

### Formal Structure

**Characteristic function:** $v(S)$ for coalition $S \subseteq N$ represents the total payoff that coalition $S$ can guarantee itself, regardless of what players outside $S$ do (in the pessimistic, "worst-case" interpretation) or, in some formulations, the value $S$ achieves under some specified cooperative arrangement.

**Allocation (imputation):** A payoff vector $x = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ specifying how much each player receives. An allocation is called an **imputation** if it satisfies:

1. **Efficiency (Pareto optimality):** $\sum_{i \in N} x_i = v(N)$ — the full value generated by the grand coalition is exactly distributed, nothing wasted, nothing over-allocated
2. **Individual rationality:** $x_i \geq v(\{i\})$ for every player $i$ — no player accepts less than they could guarantee alone

### Superadditivity

A TU game is **superadditive** if, for any two disjoint coalitions $S, T \subseteq N$:

$$v(S \cup T) \geq v(S) + v(T)$$

Superadditivity formalizes the idea that "cooperation never hurts" — merging two coalitions can never produce less value than they achieved separately (though it might produce exactly the same, if there are no synergies). Superadditivity is a natural assumption that makes the case for full cooperation (the grand coalition $N$) compelling, since under superadditivity, $v(N) \geq \sum_{S \in \mathcal{P}} v(S)$ for any partition $\mathcal{P}$ of $N$ into disjoint coalitions — the grand coalition weakly dominates any fragmented arrangement.

**Convexity (a stronger property):** A game is **convex** if, for all coalitions $S \subseteq T \subseteq N$ and any player $i \notin T$:

$$v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S) \leq v(T \cup \{i\}) - v(T)$$

This says a player's **marginal contribution** to a coalition is weakly increasing as the coalition grows larger — an "increasing returns to coalition size" property. Convex games have particularly nice solution-theoretic properties: their Core is guaranteed to be non-empty and the Shapley value always lies within the Core for convex games.

### Worked Example: A Simple Glove Game

Consider three players. Player 1 has a left glove; Players 2 and 3 each have a right glove. A pair (one left, one right glove) is worth $1; unpaired gloves are worth $0.

Characteristic function:

$$v(\{1\}) = v(\{2\}) = v(\{3\}) = 0$$



$$v(\{1,2\}) = 1, \quad v(\{1,3\}) = 1, \quad v(\{2,3\}) = 0$$



$$v(\{1,2,3\}) = 1$$

This game illustrates an important structural feature: Player 1 is essential to creating any value (no pairing is possible without a left glove), but Players 2 and 3 are **substitutes** for each other (either one, but not both, is needed alongside Player 1). Different solution concepts will allocate the total value $v(N)=1$ differently based on how they weigh this asymmetric bargaining position — the Core, for instance, will assign player 1 a substantial share reflecting their necessity.

### Key Related Concepts Within This Framework

**Marginal contribution:** A player $i$'s marginal contribution to coalition $S$ (with $i \notin S$) is $v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)$ — the additional value $i$ brings to that specific coalition. This concept underlies the Shapley value's construction, which averages marginal contributions across all possible orderings in which the grand coalition could form.

**Essential vs. inessential games:** A game is **inessential** if $v(N) = \sum_{i \in N} v(\{i\})$ — cooperation produces no surplus, so the unique reasonable allocation is simply each player's individual value. Inessential games are trivial from a bargaining perspective, since there is no gains-from-cooperation to allocate.

**Simple games:** A special class of TU games where $v(S) \in \{0, 1\}$ for every coalition, typically used to model voting systems (a coalition either has enough votes to pass a measure, $v(S)=1$, or it doesn't, $v(S)=0$). This connects TU game theory directly to the study of **power indices** (e.g., the Shapley-Shubik index and the Banzhaf index) used to measure voting power in committees and legislatures.

### Comparison: TU Games vs. NTU Games

| Dimension | Transferable Utility (TU) Games | Non-Transferable Utility (NTU) Games |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Value representation | Single real number per coalition ($v(S) \in \mathbb{R}$) | A set of feasible utility vectors per coalition (no single numeraire) |
| Interpersonal transfers | Freely possible (e.g., side payments in money) | Not generally possible; utility is not fungible across players |
| Mathematical tractability | Higher — characteristic function form is compact and well-studied | Lower — requires reasoning about sets of vectors and Pareto frontiers |
| Canonical solution concepts | Core, Shapley value, Nucleolus, Kernel | Generalized Core, Nash bargaining solution, Kalai-Smorodinsky solution |
| Typical applications | Cost-sharing, profit allocation, voting power | Bargaining over non-monetary outcomes, matching markets with heterogeneous preferences |

### Applications

- **Cost allocation problems:** Firms or municipalities jointly building shared infrastructure (e.g., an airport runway, water treatment facility) need to split total cost fairly; the characteristic function represents cost savings (or costs) of every possible sub-coalition of participants
- **Profit-sharing in joint ventures:** Companies forming a consortium need principled rules to divide joint profits based on each party's contribution
- **Voting power analysis:** Simple TU games model legislatures and shareholder voting structures, with power indices derived from the Shapley value framework applied to the 0/1-valued characteristic function
- **Bankruptcy and claims problems:** Dividing an insufficient estate among creditors with competing claims has been extensively modeled using TU game characteristic functions and associated solution concepts (e.g., the Talmud rule, connected to the Nucleolus)
- **Network and supply chain cost allocation:** Multi-party logistics or telecommunications networks use TU game models to fairly allocate shared infrastructure costs across participating firms

[Unverified] The choice of solution concept in practical cost-allocation or profit-sharing applications is often driven by specific institutional or legal fairness criteria that may prioritize different axioms (e.g., stability via the Core vs. the specific additivity and symmetry axioms underlying the Shapley value), and there is no universally "correct" choice independent of the application's normative goals.
