## Plant Anatomy and Morphology


### Definition and Scope

Plant anatomy is the study of internal plant structure at the tissue and cellular level, while plant morphology is the study of external form and structure (organs such as roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, and seeds). Together, they form the structural foundation for understanding plant growth, physiology, crop management decisions, and diagnostic assessment in agricultural systems.

### Plant Cell Structure Relevant to Agriculture

**Key Points**

- The plant cell wall (primarily cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin) provides structural rigidity and distinguishes plant cells from animal cells
- Chloroplasts, containing chlorophyll, are the site of photosynthesis and are concentrated in leaf mesophyll tissue
- The central vacuole regulates turgor pressure, which maintains cell rigidity and is essential for wilting/water stress diagnosis
- Plasmodesmata are cytoplasmic channels connecting adjacent cells, enabling symplastic transport of water, nutrients, and signaling molecules

### Plant Tissue Systems

Plants are organized into three fundamental tissue systems that run throughout the plant body:

#### Dermal Tissue System

The outer protective covering, primarily the epidermis (single layer in most young tissues) and, in woody perennials, the periderm (bark) in older stems and roots. The epidermis is often coated with a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss, and contains specialized structures such as stomata (gas exchange pores) and trichomes (hairs, which may deter herbivory or reduce water loss).

#### Ground Tissue System

Comprises the bulk of the plant body and includes three cell types:

- **Parenchyma**: Thin-walled, metabolically active cells involved in photosynthesis, storage, and healing; the most abundant cell type in most plants
- **Collenchyma**: Living cells with unevenly thickened walls providing flexible structural support, common in growing stems and leaf petioles
- **Sclerenchyma**: Cells with thick, lignified walls providing rigid structural support (fibers and sclereids); typically dead at maturity

#### Vascular Tissue System

The plant's internal transport network:

- **Xylem**: Conducts water and dissolved minerals from roots to shoots; composed of tracheids and vessel elements, which are dead at maturity and lignified for structural strength
- **Phloem**: Conducts sugars (primarily sucrose) and other organic compounds from source tissues (e.g., mature leaves) to sink tissues (e.g., roots, developing fruit); composed of living sieve tube elements and companion cells

**[Inference]** Because xylem transport relies on passive physical forces (transpirational pull and cohesion-tension) rather than active cellular energy, while phloem transport requires active loading via companion cells, phloem transport is generally considered more metabolically costly to the plant, though the two systems function in a coordinated, interdependent manner.

### Root System Morphology

| Root Type | Characteristics | Common Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Taproot system | Single dominant primary root with lateral branches | Dicots: carrot, alfalfa, most trees |
| Fibrous root system | Many similarly-sized roots, no dominant axis | Monocots: grasses, cereals, rice |
| Adventitious roots | Arise from stem or leaf tissue rather than the primary root | Corn (brace roots), strawberry runners |

Root anatomy in cross-section (from outside inward) typically includes: epidermis (often with root hairs for water/nutrient absorption), cortex (storage, often with an endodermis containing the Casparian strip that regulates apoplastic water movement), and the vascular cylinder (stele) containing xylem and phloem.

### Stem Morphology and Anatomy

Stems provide structural support, house the vascular transport system, and position leaves for light capture. Key external features include nodes (points of leaf/bud attachment) and internodes (segments between nodes).

**Monocot vs. dicot stem anatomy** is a key diagnostic distinction:

- **Dicot stems**: Vascular bundles arranged in a ring around a central pith; typically capable of secondary (lateral) growth via a vascular cambium, producing wood in perennial species
- **Monocot stems**: Vascular bundles scattered throughout the ground tissue rather than in a ring; generally lack a vascular cambium and true secondary growth (with some exceptions, such as certain palms)

### Leaf Morphology and Anatomy

Leaves are the primary photosynthetic organs. External morphological classification includes:

- **Simple vs. compound**: A simple leaf has a single undivided blade; a compound leaf is divided into leaflets
- **Venation patterns**: Parallel venation (characteristic of most monocots, e.g., grasses) vs. net/reticulate venation (characteristic of most dicots)
- **Phyllotaxy (leaf arrangement)**: Alternate, opposite, or whorled arrangement along the stem

Internally, a leaf cross-section typically shows: upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll (columnar, densely chloroplast-packed cells optimized for light capture), spongy mesophyll (loosely arranged cells with air spaces facilitating gas diffusion), vascular bundles (leaf veins), and lower epidermis containing most stomata in many species.

### Monocot vs. Dicot Comparison

| Feature | Monocots | Dicots |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seed cotyledons | One | Two |
| Leaf venation | Parallel | Net/reticulate |
| Root system | Fibrous | Taproot |
| Stem vascular bundles | Scattered | Ring arrangement |
| Flower parts | Typically multiples of 3 | Typically multiples of 4 or 5 |
| Secondary growth | Generally absent | Commonly present |
| Examples | Corn, wheat, rice, onion | Beans, tomato, cotton, soybean |

### Flower Morphology

Flowers are the reproductive structures of angiosperms, built from four basic whorls attached to a receptacle:

- **Calyx**: Outermost whorl, composed of sepals; typically protective, often green
- **Corolla**: Composed of petals; often colorful, functioning in pollinator attraction
- **Androecium**: Male reproductive whorl, composed of stamens (filament + anther, which produces pollen)
- **Gynoecium**: Female reproductive whorl, composed of one or more pistils/carpels (stigma, style, ovary containing ovules)

**Key Points**

- **Perfect flowers** contain both male and female structures; **imperfect flowers** contain only one
- **Monoecious** species bear separate male and female flowers on the same plant (e.g., corn); **dioecious** species bear male and female flowers on separate individual plants (e.g., asparagus, hemp)
- Inflorescence types (the arrangement of multiple flowers on a plant) — such as raceme, panicle, spike, umbel, and head — are important taxonomic and diagnostic features in crop identification

### Fruit and Seed Morphology

Botanically, a fruit is a mature, ripened ovary (sometimes with associated accessory tissue), and a seed is a mature, fertilized ovule.

| Fruit Type | Development | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| True/simple fruit | From a single ovary | Tomato, pea pod, grain (caryopsis) |
| Aggregate fruit | From multiple ovaries of one flower | Raspberry, strawberry (technically an accessory fruit) |
| Multiple fruit | From ovaries of multiple flowers fused | Pineapple, fig |
| Accessory fruit | Includes non-ovary tissue (e.g., receptacle) | Apple, strawberry |

Seed anatomy generally includes the seed coat (testa, derived from ovule integuments), the embryo (embryonic root/radicle, shoot/plumule, and cotyledon(s)), and, in many species, an endosperm as a nutrient reserve for germination.

### Plant Growth Habit Classification

- **Growth form**: Herbaceous (non-woody, typically annual/biennial) vs. woody (perennial, secondary growth present)
- **Life cycle**: Annual (completes life cycle in one growing season), biennial (two seasons, typically vegetative growth then reproduction), perennial (multiple years, often with dormancy periods)
- **Growth orientation**: Erect, prostrate/creeping, climbing (via tendrils, twining stems, or adventitious roots), rosette

### Meristems and Plant Growth

Plant growth is localized to meristematic tissue, regions of persistently dividing, undifferentiated cells:

- **Apical meristems**: Located at root and shoot tips, responsible for primary growth (increase in length)
- **Lateral meristems**: Vascular cambium and cork cambium, responsible for secondary growth (increase in girth) in species capable of it
- **Intercalary meristems**: Located at the base of internodes/leaves in many monocots (notably grasses), allowing regrowth after grazing or mowing without relying on apical meristem regeneration

**[Inference]** The presence of intercalary meristems near the soil surface in grasses is a key structural reason grass-family crops (e.g., turfgrass, cereals, pasture species) tolerate grazing, mowing, and defoliation better than many broadleaf dicot species, whose primary growth points are more often removed by cutting.

### Diagnostic Applications in Agriculture

**Key Points**

- Vascular bundle arrangement (monocot vs. dicot) informs herbicide selectivity, since many broadleaf herbicides exploit physiological differences concentrated in dicot growth patterns
- Root system type (taproot vs. fibrous) informs irrigation scheduling, tillage depth decisions, and drought tolerance expectations
- Node/internode identification is essential for accurate crop staging systems (e.g., vegetative stage counting in corn, wheat growth staging)
- Floral morphology (perfect/imperfect, monoecious/dioecious) determines pollination management needs, including whether cross-pollination or pollinator support is required
