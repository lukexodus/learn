## Intercropping and Polyculture


### Definition and Scope

Intercropping is the practice of growing two or more crop species simultaneously on the same land area during the same or overlapping growing period, intentionally designed to exploit interactions between the component species. Polyculture is the broader term for cultivation systems involving multiple species grown together, encompassing intercropping as well as more complex, often perennial-based multi-species systems such as agroforestry and food forests. Both stand in contrast to monoculture, the cultivation of a single crop species across a field.

### Rationale for Intercropping and Polyculture

**Key Points**

- **Resource use complementarity**: Species with differing rooting depths, canopy heights, or growth timing can potentially access and utilize light, water, and nutrients more completely from the same land area than either species grown alone
- **Pest and disease dilution**: Mixed plantings can reduce the density and spatial continuity of a single host species, potentially slowing the spread of host-specific pests and pathogens compared to a continuous monoculture stand
- **Yield risk diversification**: Because different species often respond differently to a given season's weather or pest pressure, growing multiple species together can reduce the variance of total combined yield/return compared to relying on a single crop
- **Weed suppression**: Denser, more complete canopy coverage achieved by combining species with complementary growth habits can reduce the light and resources available to weeds compared to some monoculture arrangements

**[Inference]** Because the potential benefits of intercropping (resource complementarity, pest dilution, risk reduction) depend heavily on the specific combination of species, their relative planting density and spatial arrangement, and local growing conditions, intercropping performance is generally understood in agronomic research as highly context-specific rather than a universally reliable outcome across any arbitrary species pairing.

### Types of Intercropping Arrangements

**Key Points**

- **Row intercropping**: Distinct crop species are planted in separate, alternating or adjacent rows within the same field, allowing some independent management of each species while maintaining physical proximity for interaction
- **Mixed intercropping**: Crop species are interspersed without a distinct row structure, generally maximizing interspecies interaction but complicating differential management (e.g., targeted fertilization or pest control) of individual species
- **Strip intercropping**: Crops are grown in relatively wide strips (often several rows wide), sufficient to allow independent machinery operation for each strip while still enabling edge-effect interactions between adjacent strips
- **Relay intercropping**: A second crop is planted into a still-standing first crop before the first crop's harvest, temporally overlapping the two species' growing periods in a way distinct from same-time-planted arrangements

### Classic and Commonly Studied Combinations

**Example**

The traditional "Three Sisters" system (historically associated with various Indigenous agricultural traditions in the Americas) combines corn, climbing beans, and squash: corn provides a physical structure for the climbing bean vines, the bean component contributes fixed nitrogen to the shared soil environment (as introduced under legume production), and the sprawling squash canopy is often described as providing ground-level shading that may help suppress weed growth.

**Key Points**

- **Cereal-legume intercropping** (e.g., corn-bean, sorghum-cowpea combinations) is widely studied and practiced in various global agricultural traditions, generally combining a nitrogen-demanding cereal with a nitrogen-contributing legume component
- **Grass-legume forage intercropping** (as introduced under forage crop production) combines forage grasses with legumes such as clover, providing both a fertility benefit and generally improved overall forage nutritional quality compared to a pure grass stand
- **Tree-crop agroforestry intercropping** combines perennial tree/shrub species with annual or perennial understory crops, spanning a wide range of specific system designs from widely spaced tree rows with row crops between them (alley cropping) to more complex multi-strata systems

### Agronomic and Management Challenges

**Key Points**

- **Differential management needs**: Component species often have different optimal planting dates, fertility requirements, pest/disease vulnerabilities, and harvest timing, which can create management conflicts difficult to fully resolve within a single combined field operation
- **Herbicide selectivity constraints**: Since most herbicides are developed and labeled for use in a specific crop or narrow group of crops, weed control options in mixed-species plantings are often more limited than in a comparable monoculture, frequently requiring greater reliance on mechanical or cultural weed control methods
- **Mechanization difficulty**: Standard single-crop harvest and cultivation equipment is often poorly suited to intercropped fields, particularly for mixed or row intercropping arrangements, sometimes requiring specialized equipment, increased manual labor, or acceptance of some harvest inefficiency
- **Competition risk**: If species are poorly matched or improperly proportioned, one component can outcompete the other for light, water, or nutrients, resulting in a yield disadvantage for the suppressed species rather than the intended complementary benefit

### Evaluating Intercropping Performance

Agronomic research commonly uses the Land Equivalent Ratio (LER) to assess whether an intercropping arrangement provides a genuine productivity advantage over growing the same species in separate monocultures.

$$LER = \frac{Y_{ab}}{Y_{aa}} + \frac{Y_{ba}}{Y_{bb}}$$

Where $Y_{ab}$ is the yield of species A grown in the intercrop, $Y_{aa}$ is the yield of species A grown in monoculture, $Y_{ba}$ is the yield of species B in the intercrop, and $Y_{bb}$ is the yield of species B in monoculture.

**Key Points**

- An LER greater than 1.0 indicates the intercropped combination produced more combined yield (on an equivalent land-area basis) than would have been achieved by growing the same total area as separate monocultures, suggesting a genuine resource-use efficiency benefit
- An LER at or below 1.0 indicates no net land-use advantage from intercropping over separate monoculture production of the same species and proportions

**[Unverified]** Reported LER values in the agronomic literature vary widely across specific crop combinations, planting ratios, and environments studied; a favorable LER result reported for one specific species pairing and location should not be assumed to generalize automatically to different species combinations, planting densities, or growing regions without further validation.

### Polyculture in Perennial and Agroforestry Systems

**Key Points**

- **Alley cropping**: Rows of trees or shrubs (often selected for timber, fruit, or nut production) are planted with wider alleys between them used for annual or forage crop production, combining a longer-term perennial income stream with shorter-term annual crop production on the same land
- **Silvopasture**: Combines trees, forage production, and livestock grazing within the same land area, potentially providing livestock shade/shelter benefits alongside forage and tree product value
- **Multi-strata home garden/food forest systems**: More complex polycultures combining multiple vertical canopy layers (overstory trees, understory shrubs, ground-level crops), generally associated with subsistence or diversified small-scale production systems rather than large-scale mechanized commodity agriculture

### Practical Considerations for Adoption

**Key Points**

- Intercropping and polyculture systems are generally more labor-intensive to establish and manage relative to comparable monoculture systems, which is a significant practical consideration particularly in regions or operations facing labor cost or availability constraints
- Market and processing infrastructure is typically organized around single-commodity supply chains, which can create marketing challenges for intercropped or polyculture outputs unless specific market channels for mixed or specialty production exist
- Farm-scale adoption of intercropping is generally more common in smaller-scale, diversified, or specialty/organic production contexts than in large-scale, highly mechanized commodity grain production, reflecting the practical mechanization and management constraints discussed above
