## IFC Performance Standard 8 on cultural heritage

### Overview and Regulatory Context

IFC Performance Standard 8 (PS8), *Cultural Heritage*, is one of the eight Performance Standards within IFC's 2012 Sustainability Framework. Cultural heritage encompasses properties and sites of archaeological, historical, cultural, artistic, and religious significance. It also refers to unique environmental features and cultural knowledge, as well as intangible forms of culture embodying traditional lifestyles that should be preserved for current and future generations. PS8 aims to guide companies in protecting cultural heritage from adverse impacts of project activities and supporting its preservation, and it also promotes the equitable sharing of benefits from the use of cultural heritage.

PS8's requirements are grounded in a broader international policy lineage: consistent with requirements in Performance Standard 6 related to ecosystem services and conservation of natural heritage, this Performance Standard aims to ensure that clients protect cultural heritage in the course of their project activities. In addition, the requirements of this Performance Standard on a project's use of cultural heritage are based in part on standards set by the Convention on Biological Diversity. As with the other topic-specific standards, the implementation of the actions necessary to meet the requirements of this Performance Standard is managed through the client's Environmental and Social Management System, the elements of which are outlined in Performance Standard 1.

### Objectives

PS8's stated objectives establish both a protective and an equity dimension:

- To protect cultural heritage from the adverse impacts of project activities and support its preservation, regardless of whether or not it has been legally protected or previously disturbed.
- To promote the equitable sharing of benefits from the use of cultural heritage.

The regardless-of-legal-protection principle is significant: PS8's scope is not limited to officially designated heritage sites, but extends to cultural heritage that may be undocumented, unprotected by national law, or already partially disturbed.

### Siting, Design, and the Assessment Trigger

PS8 places the primary responsibility for avoidance on project siting and design decisions made early in the project lifecycle. The client is responsible for siting and designing a project to avoid significant adverse impacts to cultural heritage. The environmental and social risks and impacts identification process should determine whether the proposed location of a project is in areas where cultural heritage is expected to be found, either during construction or operations — linking PS8 directly to PS1's overarching assessment process rather than treating heritage screening as a separate, siloed exercise.

Qualified personnel should be retained to carry out such analysis as part of the Assessment, and in cases where cultural heritage is deemed a significant issue, a focused assessment may be necessary even if a full-scale social and environmental impact assessment is not required.

### The Mitigation Hierarchy for Cultural Heritage

Where the client has encountered tangible cultural heritage that is replicable and not critical, the client will apply mitigation measures that favor avoidance. Where avoidance is not feasible, PS8 sets out a graduated response hierarchy:

1. **Minimize** adverse impacts and implement restoration measures, in situ, that ensure maintenance of the value and functionality of the cultural heritage, including maintaining or restoring any ecosystem processes needed to support it.
2. **Restore elsewhere**, where in-situ restoration is not possible, restoring the functionality of the cultural heritage in a different location, including the ecosystem processes needed to support it.

**"Replicable" cultural heritage** is a defined technical term: replicable cultural heritage is defined as tangible forms of cultural heritage that can themselves be moved to another location or that can be replaced by a similar structure or natural features to which the cultural values can be transferred by appropriate measures. Archeological or historical sites may be considered replicable where the particular eras and cultural values they represent are well represented by other sites and/or structures.

By contrast, best international practice recommends that cultural heritage be left undisturbed if at all possible, and sites containing cultural heritage should not be excavated or otherwise disturbed unnecessarily — establishing a strong presumption in favor of in-situ preservation across the standard.

### Critical Cultural Heritage

PS8 distinguishes "critical" cultural heritage from ordinary replicable heritage, applying a heightened protection standard. Critical cultural heritage is treated with the same rigor as the avoidance-first principle applied to critical habitat under PS6, and — as established under PS7 for Indigenous Peoples specifically — priority is given to avoidance of impacts on cultural heritage that is critical to a community's identity and/or cultural, ceremonial, or spiritual aspects of life; where such impacts are unavoidable, the client is required to obtain FPIC of the Affected Communities (a requirement operating jointly through PS7 and PS8 for Indigenous Peoples, and consistent with PS8's own provisions for non-Indigenous communities with comparable critical cultural heritage relationships).

### Chance Find Procedures

A distinctive and highly practical PS8 mechanism governs previously unknown cultural heritage discovered during construction or operations. In such cases, as part of the client's ESMS, the client will develop provisions for managing chance finds through a chance find procedure which will be applied in the event that cultural heritage is subsequently discovered.

**Definition:** A chance find procedure is a project-specific procedure that outlines the actions to be taken if previously unknown cultural heritage is encountered.

**Core discovery protocol:** The client will not disturb any chance find further until an assessment by competent professionals is made and actions consistent with the requirements of this Performance Standard are identified. This "stop work and assess" principle is the operational heart of the chance find procedure and is echoed in comparable frameworks used by other development finance institutions (for example, the chance find procedure concept also appears in EBRD Performance Requirement 8 and in national cultural heritage law).

**Integration into management systems:** The procedure should be incorporated in the Management Program and implemented through the client's Environmental and Social Management System, and for ground-disturbing projects, depending on the project location, it may be appropriate to develop a chance find procedure that addresses and protects cultural heritage finds made during a project's construction and/or operation phases.

**Scope of application:** A chance find procedure is applicable to all activities conducted by personnel, including contractors, that have the potential to uncover a heritage item or site — meaning the obligation extends through the client's supply chain and contracted workforce, not solely the client's own employees.

### Preservation vs. Removal of Archaeological Material

Where preservation cannot be achieved in situ, PS8 sets strict conditions for removal rather than leaving it unregulated. Archaeological material should preferably be preserved in situ, since removal is likely to result in irreparable damage to cultural heritage values, and where removal of materials is necessary, it should be conducted by appropriate, locally licensed, experts — reinforcing the standard's general presumption against disturbance and its insistence on qualified, credentialed handling when disturbance is unavoidable.

### Consultation Requirements

Where a project may affect cultural heritage, the client will consult with Affected Communities within the host country who use, or have used within living memory, the cultural heritage for long-standing cultural purposes. Because heritage significance is not always formally documented, consultation performs an identification function as well as a mitigation-planning function: since cultural heritage is not always documented, or protected by law, consultation is an important means of identifying it, documenting its presence and significance, assessing potential impacts, and exploring mitigation options.

Relevant consultation groups identified in Guidance Note 8 include historical or traditional users and owners of cultural heritage, among others whose relationship to the heritage in question warrants engagement. This consultation process operates under the general community engagement requirements set out in paragraphs 25 through 33 of Performance Standard 1, again reinforcing PS8's dependence on the PS1 architecture rather than establishing a fully separate engagement track.

### Commercial Use and Benefit-Sharing

Where a project proposes to use cultural heritage for commercial purposes, PS8 requires informed disclosure and, in relevant circumstances tied to Indigenous Peoples' knowledge, fair and equitable sharing of benefits from commercialization of such knowledge, innovation, or practice, consistent with the customs and traditions of the communities concerned. Practitioners conducting due diligence should assess whether the client has identified proposed project use of cultural resources, and, if so, whether the client has informed affected communities of their rights and arranged for shared benefits.

### Interaction with Other Performance Standards

- **PS1** — supplies the ESMS architecture, the risk identification trigger for heritage screening, and the community engagement process (paragraphs 25–33) underlying PS8 consultation.
- **PS6** — PS8's biodiversity/ecosystem-services linkage draws on PS6's conservation framework, and both standards reference the Convention on Biological Diversity as a policy foundation, particularly where cultural heritage is embedded in ecosystem processes (e.g., sacred natural sites).
- **PS7** — where cultural heritage affected is that of Indigenous Peoples and is "critical," PS7's FPIC requirement governs the consent dimension jointly with PS8's protection and benefit-sharing requirements.

### Practical Example

**Example:** A linear road-construction project in a region with known but undocumented archaeological potential begins earthworks, and excavation crews unexpectedly uncover ceramic fragments and what appear to be burial remains at one site along the alignment.

- **Pre-construction screening:** The ESIA had flagged the general region as having elevated archaeological potential (based on regional survey data), triggering the requirement to prepare a chance find procedure as part of the ESMS, even though a full dedicated archaeological survey of the entire alignment was not conducted given the project's routing through previously disturbed agricultural land.
- **Discovery response:** Consistent with the chance find procedure, the excavation crew halts work in the immediate area upon discovery and secures the site to prevent further disturbance, without collecting or moving any material.
- **Professional assessment:** A locally licensed archaeologist is retained to assess the find's significance, age, and cultural affiliation, consulting with descendant or historically associated communities as part of the assessment given the presence of what appear to be human remains.
- **Consultation:** Local community representatives with living-memory or ancestral association to the area are consulted regarding appropriate treatment of the find, recognizing the heightened sensitivity of burial remains as a cultural heritage category.
- **Determination:** Based on the assessment, the find is determined not to meet "critical" cultural heritage criteria but does warrant careful, licensed removal and relocation (an example of "replicable" tangible heritage) to a location agreed with the consulted community, given that continued road construction at the exact location cannot be avoided through redesign at this stage.
- **Documentation and ESMS integration:** The chance find event, assessment findings, consultation record, and disposition of the material are documented and folded into the project's ongoing ESMS records and management program, and the chance find procedure remains active for the balance of the construction period given demonstrated archaeological potential.

### Key Points

- PS8's protection obligation applies regardless of whether cultural heritage has been legally protected or previously disturbed — a notably broad scope trigger.
- Siting and design to avoid significant adverse impacts is the client's primary responsibility, assessed through the PS1 risk identification process.
- The mitigation hierarchy for non-critical, replicable cultural heritage favors avoidance, then in-situ minimization/restoration, then restoration at an alternative location.
- Critical cultural heritage receives heightened protection: priority is given to avoidance, and unavoidable significant impacts require FPIC of Affected Communities.
- Chance find procedures are a mandatory ESMS component for projects where cultural heritage discovery is plausible, built around a strict "stop, assess, then act" sequence, and applicable to contractors as well as the client's own workforce.
- Archaeological material should preferably remain in situ; where removal is unavoidable, only appropriately licensed local experts should conduct it.
- Consultation serves a dual role: identifying undocumented heritage and shaping mitigation options, operating under PS1's broader community engagement requirements.
- Commercial use of cultural heritage (particularly Indigenous traditional knowledge) requires rights disclosure and equitable benefit-sharing, overlapping directly with PS7.
