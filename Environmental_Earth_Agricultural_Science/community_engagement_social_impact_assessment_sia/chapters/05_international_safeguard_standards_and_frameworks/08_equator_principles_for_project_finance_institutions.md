## Equator Principles for project finance institutions


### Overview and Regulatory Context

The Equator Principles (EP) are a risk management framework adopted by financial institutions for determining, assessing, and managing environmental and social risk in projects. They have been adopted by over 100 international financial institutions across dozens of countries, covering the majority of international project finance debt within developed and emerging markets. Financial institutions that implement the EPs are known as Equator Principles Financial Institutions (EPFIs).

Launched in June 2003 by an initial group of international banks, the Equator Principles draw directly from IFC's Environmental and Social Performance Standards and the World Bank Group's Environmental, Health, and Safety Guidelines. Based on the International Finance Corporation (IFC) Performance Standards and World Bank Environmental Health and Safety Guidelines, the Equator Principles have been widely used since their inception in 2003 by financial institutions for the assessment and management of the impact of large-scale development projects on the environment and society. This lineage means the Equator Principles function as the private commercial-lending mechanism through which the IFC Performance Standards (including PS1, PS4, PS5, PS7, and PS8, covered elsewhere in this chapter) become binding requirements across a large share of global project finance, even for lenders that are not themselves IFC.

### Version History

The Equator Principles have evolved through four iterations since 2003, each adopted following extensive consultation and a vote by member institutions:

| Version | Effective | Key Development |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EP (original) | June 2003 | Initial framework established, drawing on IFC standards |
| EP2 | 2006 | Refinements following IFC's own Performance Standards update |
| EP3 | June 4, 2013 | Broadened scope to include project-related corporate loans and bridge loans; expanded public reporting requirements; explicit references to human rights and climate change, including greenhouse gas emissions reporting |
| EP4 | October 1, 2020 | Extended scope to five financial products; new "Designated Countries" review standard (removing "deemed compliance"); strengthened human rights, Indigenous Peoples, and climate change provisions |

EP4 was adopted by the EP Association on November 18, 2019, with an original effective date of July 1, 2020 that was extended to October 1, 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic, and it applies to transactions mandated on or after that date (earlier-mandated transactions continue to apply the prior EP3 version).

### Scope of Application

EP4 applies globally, to all industry sectors and to five financial products:

1. Project Finance Advisory Services
2. Project Finance
3. Project-Related Corporate Loans
4. Bridge Loans
5. Project-Related Refinance and Project-Related Acquisition Finance

The EP4 scope of application has been extended to five financial products with the inclusion of Project-Related Finance and Project-Related Acquisition Finance — a notable expansion over EP3's narrower product coverage.

**Financial thresholds (EP4):** EP4 applies to Project-Related Corporate Loans of USD 50 million or more (whereas EP3 applied to loans of USD 100 million or more) and which are of two years or more in duration — a materially lower threshold that draws more transactions into EP4's scope. Over the framework's history, capital cost thresholds at which the Equator Principles apply have also been progressively reduced, from an original $50 million project capital cost threshold down to $10 million.

**Expanded definition of "Project":** Non-contiguous developments over one or more geographic areas, including expansions and upgrades to existing developments, are now included in the definition of "Project" under EP4 — closing a prior gap where phased or geographically dispersed developments might have escaped classification as a single project.

### The Ten Principles

The Equator Principles are organized as ten sequential principles that structure the EPFI's due diligence and ongoing oversight process:

**Principle 1 — Review and Categorisation:** EPFIs have to review and categorise the proposed project into three categories. Project categorization under the EP is in line with the recommendations of the IFC's environmental and social categorization process:

- **Category A** — projects with potential significant adverse environmental and social risks and/or impacts that are diverse, irreversible, or unprecedented.
- **Category B** — projects with potential limited adverse environmental and social risks and/or impacts that are few in number, generally site-specific, largely reversible, and readily addressed through mitigation measures.
- **Category C** — projects with minimal or no adverse environmental and social risks and/or impacts.

This categorization explicitly incorporates risks related to human rights, climate change, and biodiversity, not solely conventional environmental/social risk categories.

**Principle 2 — Environmental and Social Assessment:** For Category A and, as appropriate, Category B Projects, the client conducts an Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA) or other Assessment addressing relevant environmental and social risks; for lower-risk projects, a limited or focused assessment may be appropriate, applying applicable risk management standards relevant to the risks or impacts identified during categorisation. The client is expected to include assessments of potential adverse Human Rights impacts and climate change risks as part of the ESIA or other Assessment, with these included in the Assessment Documentation.

**Principle 3 — Applicable Environmental and Social Standards:** The Assessment process evaluates compliance with the IFC Performance Standards and, where applicable, host country laws and World Bank Group EHS Guidelines, with the more stringent standard generally governing.

**Principle 4 — Environmental and Social Management System and Equator Principles Action Plan:** Category A and B projects require an ESMS (consistent with IFC PS1) and, where gaps exist relative to applicable standards, an Equator Principles Action Plan (EPAP) describing corrective measures and timelines.

**Principle 5 — Stakeholder Engagement:** Consultation with Affected Communities is required for Category A and B projects, in a structured, culturally appropriate, and ongoing manner throughout the project life-cycle, mirroring the IFC PS1/World Bank ESS10 engagement architecture.

**Principle 6 — Grievance Mechanism:** For Category A and, as appropriate, Category B projects, the client establishes a grievance mechanism to receive and facilitate resolution of concerns from Affected Communities.

**Principle 7 — Independent Review:** Developers (and Contractors, where applicable) must retain an independent environmental and social consultant to review the ESIA process, including documentation for the ESMPs, environmental and social management systems (ESMS), and stakeholder engagement for Category A and B projects, ensuring proper due diligence and compliance with the Equator Principles.

**Principle 8 — Covenants:** Financing documentation requires the client to regularly demonstrate compliance with the proposed Action Plan as well as with relevant laws, regulations, and permits, through covenants and other conditions in the loan documentation.

**Principle 9 — Independent Monitoring and Reporting:** For the most sensitive projects, an independent consultant is mandated by the lenders to review and monitor the project's E&S documentation and performance, during the planning, design, construction, and operation phases of the project — extending independent oversight beyond the pre-financing review stage into ongoing project life.

**Principle 10 — Reporting and Transparency:** Developers (and Contractors, where applicable) must disclose a summary of the ESIA for each project online, including human rights impacts and climate change risks, and EPFIs must publicly report on their EP portfolio, broken down by category and other metrics, on an annual basis.

### EP4's Human Rights and Climate Change Provisions

EP4 substantially strengthened due diligence expectations in two areas relevant to social impact assessment practice:

**Human rights:** The client should refer to the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs) when assessing Human Rights risks and impacts, and EPFIs commit to fulfilling their responsibility to respect Human Rights in line with the UNGPs by carrying out human rights due diligence commensurate with the nature and risks of the project. In limited high-risk circumstances, it may be appropriate for the client to complement its Assessment Documentation with specific human rights due diligence beyond the standard ESIA.

**Climate change:** The Climate Change Risk Assessment should be aligned with Climate Physical Risk and Climate Transition Risk categories of the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD), and EPFIs require reporting on greenhouse gas emission levels as part of Principle 10's disclosure obligations.

**Impact avoidance hierarchy:** Impacts on Project-affected ecosystems, communities, and the climate should be avoided where possible; if these impacts are unavoidable, they should be minimised and mitigated, and where residual impacts remain, clients should provide remedy for human rights impacts or offset environmental impacts as appropriate — a formulation that extends the classic mitigation hierarchy to explicitly include human rights remedy alongside environmental offsetting.

**"Designated Countries" and removal of deemed compliance:** A significant EP4 change concerns projects in high-income OECD nations, historically treated under a "deemed compliance" assumption based on the robustness of domestic regulatory regimes. Key differences from the June 2013 version (EP3) relate to the scope of transactions covered and new requirements in relation to projects in high-income OECD nations, human rights, impacts on Indigenous Peoples, and climate change — with EP4 removing automatic deemed compliance and introducing dedicated review standards for these Designated Countries, including Indigenous Peoples' rights considerations that had previously been under-addressed in developed-country contexts.

### EPFI Reporting Obligations

Principle 10 anchors a transparency regime with obligations at both the project and institutional level:

- **Project-level disclosure** — a summary of the ESIA for each project is disclosed online, including human rights impacts and climate change risks.
- **Institutional-level reporting** — EPFIs publish annual implementation reports; the totals for each product type are broken down by Category (A, B, or C) and further metrics, consistent with Annex B's minimum reporting requirements.
- **Greenhouse gas reporting** — projects meeting defined emissions thresholds require quantification and reporting of GHG emission levels, referencing Annex A's climate change guidance including alternatives analysis.

### Comparative Position within the International Safeguard Architecture

Within this chapter's broader framework comparison, the Equator Principles occupy a distinctive institutional position: they are not a standard-setting body's own direct-lending policy (as IFC PS1–PS8 and World Bank ESS1–ESS10 are for their respective institutions) but rather a voluntary adoption mechanism through which commercial and export-credit lenders commit to applying IFC-derived standards to their own project finance. This has amplified the practical reach of the IFC Performance Standards well beyond IFC's own balance sheet — the Equator Principles' environmental and human rights standards will apply to project expansions and upgrades financed by EPFIs across the global commercial banking sector, not merely to IFC-financed projects.

### Practical Example

**Example:** A commercial bank syndicate, including several EPFIs, is asked to arrange USD 400 million in project finance for a new greenfield liquefied natural gas (LNG) export terminal in a non-OECD coastal country with an adjacent fishing community.

- **Categorization (Principle 1):** Given the scale, irreversibility of some marine and coastal impacts, and potential for significant community displacement, the lead EPFI categorizes the project as Category A.
- **Assessment (Principle 2):** The sponsor commissions a full ESIA addressing marine ecology, coastal community livelihoods (fishing access), air emissions, and a dedicated climate change risk assessment aligned with TCFD physical and transition risk categories, alongside a human-rights screening referencing the UNGPs given potential land acquisition and community relocation.
- **Applicable standards (Principle 3):** Because host country regulations are less stringent than IFC Performance Standards in several areas (notably resettlement compensation and biodiversity offsets), the more stringent IFC PS5 and PS6 requirements govern via gap analysis.
- **ESMS/EPAP (Principle 4):** An Equator Principles Action Plan is negotiated, specifying corrective actions and a compliance timeline where the sponsor's baseline ESMS falls short of PS1 requirements.
- **Stakeholder engagement and grievance mechanism (Principles 5–6):** A Stakeholder Engagement Plan is developed for the fishing community and other affected parties, alongside a dedicated grievance mechanism addressing concerns about fishing-ground access restrictions during construction.
- **Independent review (Principle 7):** An independent environmental and social consultant reviews the ESIA, ESMP, and stakeholder engagement documentation before financial close, verifying adequacy against the Equator Principles.
- **Covenants (Principle 8):** Loan documentation includes covenants requiring ongoing compliance with the EPAP and applicable laws, with EP4's updated Guidance Note on Incorporating Environmental and Social Considerations into Loan Documentation informing the specific contractual language.
- **Independent monitoring (Principle 9):** Given Category A status, an independent consultant is retained by the lender syndicate to monitor E&S performance through construction and into early operations.
- **Reporting (Principle 10):** A summary ESIA, including climate and human rights findings, is disclosed publicly online, and each participating EPFI reports the transaction in its annual Equator Principles implementation report, categorized as Category A.

[Inference: the LNG terminal scenario is illustrative rather than drawn from a documented case; the sequencing across the ten principles reflects EP4's documented structure and standard EPFI implementation practice as described in publicly available EP4 guidance materials and EPFI implementation reports.]

### Key Points

- The Equator Principles are a voluntary framework adopted by commercial/export-credit lenders (EPFIs), built on the IFC Performance Standards and World Bank Group EHS Guidelines, giving those standards far broader market reach than IFC's own direct financing.
- EP4 (effective October 1, 2020) is the current iteration, following EP (2003), EP2 (2006), and EP3 (2013); each revision has broadened scope and deepened environmental/social/human rights/climate rigor.
- The ten principles run sequentially from categorization (Principle 1) through assessment, standards-setting, ESMS/Action Plan, stakeholder engagement, grievance mechanism, independent review, covenants, independent monitoring, to public reporting (Principle 10).
- Categorization (A/B/C) mirrors IFC's own categorization process and drives the intensity of assessment, consultation, and independent oversight required.
- EP4's defining substantive changes are strengthened human rights due diligence (referencing the UNGPs), climate change risk assessment aligned with TCFD categories, expanded financial product scope, lower loan thresholds, and removal of automatic "deemed compliance" for Designated (high-income OECD) Countries.
- Stakeholder engagement (Principle 5) and grievance mechanism (Principle 6) requirements directly parallel — and are informed by — IFC PS1 and World Bank ESS10, making Equator Principles application a key practical vector through which SIA and community engagement obligations reach commercial project finance.
- Independent review and monitoring (Principles 7 and 9) introduce third-party verification not present in a project sponsor's self-assessment alone.
