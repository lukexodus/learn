## IFC Performance Standard 7 on Indigenous Peoples

### Overview and Regulatory Context

IFC Performance Standard 7 (PS7), *Indigenous Peoples*, is one of the eight Performance Standards within IFC's 2012 Sustainability Framework. Indigenous peoples may be particularly vulnerable to the adverse impacts associated with project development, including risk of impoverishment and loss of identity, culture, and natural resource-based livelihoods. PS7 seeks to ensure that business activities minimize negative impacts, foster respect for human rights, dignity and culture of indigenous populations, and promote development benefits in culturally appropriate ways.

The 2012 revision of PS7 marked a significant departure from its predecessor: for the first time, the Performance Standard includes a requirement of free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) from indigenous peoples, whereas the 2006 Performance Standard on indigenous peoples required only "free, prior, informed consultation." The new Performance Standards went into effect in January 2012 and are applicable to a greater number of the IFC's investments than the 2006 Performance Standards, which only applied to project finance — and because Equator Principles Financial Institutions apply the IFC standards, this expansion extends PS7's reach across much of the world's project financing.

Because of its international recognition, IFC PS7 is widely used as a reference for good practice in Indigenous Peoples engagement, cultural protection, and social risk management well beyond IFC's own direct financing.

### Identifying Indigenous Peoples

PS7 does not rely on a single legal definition but instead uses an identity-and-attachment framework. Indigenous people can be defined as a distinct social and cultural group possessing the following characteristics in varying degrees:

- Self-identification as members of a distinct indigenous cultural group, and recognition of this identity by others.
- Collective attachment to geographically distinct habitats or ancestral territories in the project area, and to the natural resources in these habitats and territories.
- Customary cultural, economic, social, or political institutions that are separate from those of the dominant society or culture.
- A distinct language or dialect, often different from the official language of the country or region.

A key element of IFC PS7 is accurate identification of Indigenous Peoples and their relationship to land and resources, and unlike general social impact assessments, IFC PS7 places strong emphasis on collective rights and cultural systems, not only individual household impacts — making it one of the most sensitive and complex standards within the IFC framework.

### Structured Risk-Management Approach

IFC PS7 follows a structured approach to managing impacts: projects must first consider whether Indigenous Peoples and their territories can be avoided entirely through redesign or relocation of project components — applying the mitigation hierarchy (established in PS1) with particular rigor given the heightened vulnerability and collective-rights dimensions at stake.

Where impacts cannot be avoided, PS7 requires:

- Informed consultation and participation with Indigenous Peoples throughout the project process is a core requirement and may include Free, Prior and Informed Consent under certain circumstances.
- Development of action plans (Indigenous Peoples Plans or equivalent) to address adverse impacts with the informed consultation and participation of affected Indigenous communities.
- Engagement mechanisms tailored to the vulnerability of the Affected Communities of Indigenous Peoples and to their specific characteristics and expressed needs.
- Where any of several defined high-risk circumstances apply, the client will engage external experts to assist in the identification of the project risks and impacts.

### Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC)

FPIC is the standard's most consequential and most debated requirement, and it is a central concept in IFC PS7. There is no universally accepted definition of FPIC; for the purposes of Performance Standards 1, 7 and 8, "FPIC" has the specific meaning defined within PS7 itself.

**Relationship to ICP:** FPIC builds on and expands the process of informed consultation and participation (ICP) described in Performance Standard 1 and will be established through good faith negotiation between the client and the Affected Communities of Indigenous Peoples.

**Documentation requirements:** The client will document (i) the mutually accepted process between the client and Affected Communities of Indigenous Peoples, and (ii) evidence of agreement between the parties as the outcome of the negotiations.

**Not requiring unanimity:** FPIC does not necessarily require unanimity and may be achieved even when individuals or groups within the community explicitly disagree — a technical nuance that distinguishes PS7's FPIC standard from a strict veto-by-any-individual model.

**Qualities of valid consultation:** FPIC consultation that is free of intimidation or coercion provides timely disclosure of information and is relevant, understandable, and accessible, and consultation should continue through the entire life of the project and not only during the early stages.

**Circumstances triggering mandatory FPIC**, per PS7, generally include:

1. Adverse impacts on land and natural resources subject to traditional ownership or under customary use.
2. Relocation of Indigenous Peoples from traditional or customary lands.
3. Significant impacts on critical cultural heritage essential to the identity and/or cultural, ceremonial, or spiritual aspects of Indigenous Peoples' lives — where significant project impacts on critical cultural heritage are unavoidable, the client will obtain the FPIC of the Affected Communities of Indigenous Peoples.
4. Use of the cultural heritage — including knowledge, innovations, or practices — of Indigenous Peoples for commercial purposes; here the client must inform Affected Communities of their rights under national law, the scope and nature of the proposed commercial development, and its potential consequences, and must obtain their FPIC, while also ensuring fair and equitable sharing of benefits from commercialization consistent with the community's customs and traditions.

FPIC is required in specific high-impact scenarios, particularly relocation from traditional lands, and is widely referenced in international ESG and human rights frameworks well beyond the IFC context.

### Cultural Heritage, Benefit-Sharing, and Land

PS7 provisions extend into territory shared with PS8 (Cultural Heritage) but with an Indigenous-Peoples-specific lens:

- **Critical cultural heritage** — priority is given to avoidance of impacts on cultural, ceremonial, or spiritual sites/practices central to Indigenous Peoples' lives; unavoidable significant impacts trigger the FPIC requirement described above.
- **Commercialization of traditional knowledge** — any project use of Indigenous Peoples' cultural heritage (knowledge, innovations, practices) for commercial purposes requires disclosure of rights, scope, and consequences, plus FPIC and fair and equitable benefit-sharing consistent with the community's customs and traditions.
- **Land and natural resource rights** — traditional ownership or customary use of land and resources is a primary trigger category for FPIC, reflecting PS7's recognition that Indigenous land relationships are frequently collective, customary, and not necessarily reflected in formal title systems.

### Interaction with Other Performance Standards

- **PS1** — supplies the ESMS and the baseline ICP process that FPIC builds upon and expands.
- **PS5** — where project-related land acquisition or resettlement affects Indigenous Peoples, PS5's compensation and livelihood-restoration requirements apply in addition to PS7's consent and cultural-protection requirements; the two standards operate concurrently rather than as alternatives.
- **PS8** — critical cultural heritage impacts on Indigenous Peoples sit at the intersection of PS7 and PS8, with PS7's FPIC requirement governing the Indigenous-specific consent dimension.
- **PS4** — conflict-sensitivity and community security considerations apply with particular weight in Indigenous contexts, especially where security forces interact with Indigenous territories.

### International Law Context

PS7 operates alongside, but is distinct from, international human rights instruments. Members of indigenous peoples are entitled to the enjoyment of all internationally recognised human rights — including those specific to their indigenous identity — in a condition of full equality with all other human beings, and the legal standards defining the nature and content of such rights are substantially articulated in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP). UNDRIP is, in principle, a declaration of non-binding character, though it includes several key provisions considered to correspond to existing State obligations under customary international law. ### Implementation Track Record and Critique

PS7's real-world application has been the subject of significant independent scrutiny. In eight years following the 2012 standard's introduction, IFC applied its Indigenous Peoples performance standard in 29 projects out of a portfolio of 2,116, and no more than four have resulted in a documented FPIC process accepted by communities, according to a 2020 report examining implementation gaps. The same reporting notes that projects that bypassed FPIC have triggered ombudsman complaints, public protests, and legal proceedings, and that the report's authors argue IFC needs a stronger, clearer approach to PS7. ### Practical Example

**Example:** A proposed open-pit mining concession overlaps with land traditionally used by an Indigenous community for subsistence hunting, seasonal grazing, and a set of ceremonial sites.

- **Identification:** A social baseline study applies PS7's identity criteria (self-identification, collective territorial attachment, customary institutions, distinct language) and confirms Indigenous Peoples status for the community, engaging external anthropological experts given the complexity of the customary land-use pattern.
- **Avoidance assessment:** Project design is reviewed to determine whether the pit footprint, haul roads, or waste-rock facilities can be relocated to avoid the ceremonial sites entirely; partial avoidance is achieved by rerouting the haul road.
- **FPIC trigger:** Because the mine plan still requires physical relocation of several households from customary land and unavoidable impact on one ceremonial site, FPIC is triggered under both the relocation and critical-cultural-heritage criteria.
- **FPIC process:** The client documents a mutually agreed negotiation process with community representative structures, including timelines, decision-making protocols recognized by the community, and accessible, non-coercive information disclosure in the community's language; the process continues iteratively rather than being a single meeting.
- **Outcome documentation:** Agreement is documented in a way that captures both the process followed and evidence of the outcome, while the record also notes that a minority of households expressed continued objection — consistent with PS7's clarification that FPIC does not require unanimity.
- **Benefit-sharing:** A negotiated benefits package (local employment targets, community development fund, access rights to a redesignated cultural site) is documented as part of the FPIC outcome.
- **Ongoing engagement:** Consultation mechanisms remain active through construction and operations phases rather than concluding once initial consent is documented.

[Inference: the mining scenario is illustrative rather than drawn from a specific documented case; the sequencing (identification → avoidance → FPIC trigger assessment → negotiated process → documentation → benefit-sharing → ongoing engagement) reflects PS7's documented structure and IFC Guidance Note 7's elaboration of good-practice implementation.]

### Key Points

- PS7's defining 2012 innovation over its 2006 predecessor is the introduction of a formal FPIC requirement, moving beyond consultation alone.
- Indigenous Peoples identification rests on a multi-factor identity test (self-identification, collective territorial attachment, customary institutions, distinct language), applied "in varying degrees" rather than as a rigid checklist.
- PS7 emphasizes collective rights and cultural systems, distinguishing it from individual-household-focused social impact assessment approaches used elsewhere in the Performance Standards.
- FPIC builds on PS1's ICP process but adds a negotiated-agreement and documentation requirement; it does not require unanimity.
- Four defined circumstances trigger mandatory FPIC: land/resource impacts, relocation, critical cultural heritage impacts, and commercial use of traditional knowledge.
- PS7 operates concurrently with PS5 (resettlement) and PS8 (cultural heritage) rather than replacing their requirements when Indigenous Peoples are affected.
- Independent monitoring reports have raised significant concerns about the gap between PS7's design and its documented real-world implementation; practitioners should treat implementation statistics as reported findings requiring independent verification.
