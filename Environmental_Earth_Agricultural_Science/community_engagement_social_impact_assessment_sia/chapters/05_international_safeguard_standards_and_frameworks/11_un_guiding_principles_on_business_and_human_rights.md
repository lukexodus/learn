## UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights


### Overview and Regulatory Context

The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs) are a set of guidelines for States and companies to prevent, address and remedy human rights abuses committed in business operations. They were proposed by UN Special Representative on business and human rights John Ruggie, and endorsed by the UN Human Rights Council in June 2011 — specifically, on 16 June 2011, the United Nations Human Rights Council unanimously endorsed the UN Guiding Principles for Business and Human Rights, making the framework the first corporate human rights responsibility initiative to be endorsed by the United Nations.

The UNGPs consist of 31 principles implementing the UN "Protect, Respect and Remedy" Framework on the issue of human rights and transnational corporations and other business enterprises. The Guiding Principles provided the first global standard for preventing and addressing the risk of adverse impacts on human rights linked to business activity, and they continue to provide the internationally accepted framework for enhancing standards and practices regarding business and human rights.

The UNGPs are not a treaty and do not themselves create new binding international legal obligations; rather, they articulate an authoritative framework built on existing standards. They emerged from a structured process: the principles grew out of a six-year consultative process which began in 2005, when UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan appointed Harvard University Professor John Ruggie as his Special Representative for Business and Human Rights.

### Relationship to This Chapter's Other Frameworks

Within this chapter's comparative landscape, the UNGPs function as the foundational human-rights reference that other frameworks explicitly incorporate rather than duplicate. As covered elsewhere in this chapter, IFC PS7 references human rights obligations existing independently of state duties, and Equator Principles EP4 requires that the client should refer to the UNGPs when assessing Human Rights risks and impacts, with EPFIs committing to fulfill their responsibility to respect Human Rights in line with the UNGPs by carrying out human rights due diligence. This makes the UNGPs a cross-cutting reference point that underlies, rather than competes with, the project-specific safeguard standards (IFC PS1–PS8, World Bank ESS1–ESS10, Equator Principles) already documented in this chapter.

### The Three Pillars ("Ruggie Framework")

The UNGPs encompass three pillars outlining how states and businesses should implement the framework, often referred to as "the Ruggie Framework":

**Pillar I — The State Duty to Protect Human Rights**

States must protect against human rights abuse within their territory and/or jurisdiction by third parties, including business enterprises, through appropriate policies, regulation, and adjudication. States should consider a smart mix of measures — national and international, mandatory and voluntary — to foster business respect for human rights.

**Pillar II — The Corporate Responsibility to Respect Human Rights**

Business enterprises should act with due diligence to avoid infringing on the rights of others and address adverse impacts with which they are involved. This pillar is independent of a state's own human rights obligations — a company's responsibility to respect human rights exists regardless of whether the host state is effectively fulfilling its own protective duty.

**Pillar III — Access to Remedy**

There is a need for greater access by victims to effective remedy, both judicial and non-judicial, for victims of business-related human rights abuse.

### Structure of the 31 Principles

The Guiding Principles are organized sequentially, mapping to the three pillars: the first 10 principles apply to the first pillar, with the first two principles serving as Foundational Principles, followed by principles elaborating the state duty to protect (approximately Principles 1–10), the corporate responsibility to respect (approximately Principles 11–24), and access to remedy (approximately Principles 25–31).

### The Corporate Responsibility to Respect (Pillar II) in Depth

Pillar II is the component most directly operational for businesses and, correspondingly, most relevant to community engagement and SIA practice, since it is where project-level human rights impacts on affected communities are addressed.

**Policy commitment:** Under the UNGPs, companies should express their commitment to respect human rights through a policy statement — a foundational, board-level or equivalent senior commitment that anchors subsequent due diligence activity.

**Human rights due diligence (HRDD):** This is the central operational mechanism of Pillar II. Human rights due diligence is defined as an ongoing risk management process undertaken in order to identify, prevent, mitigate and account for how a company addresses its adverse human rights impacts. It includes four key steps: assessing actual and potential human rights impacts; integrating and acting on the findings; tracking responses; and communicating about how impacts are addressed.

This four-step structure is explicitly set out in Guiding Principle 17, and Principles 18 through 21 elaborate its essential components in further detail. Human rights due diligence can be included within broader enterprise risk management systems, provided that it goes beyond simply identifying and managing material risks to the company itself, to include risks to rights-holders — a critical distinction that separates HRDD from conventional corporate risk management, which is typically oriented toward risks to the company rather than risks to affected people.

**Impact categorization:** Human rights risks are understood to be the business enterprise's potential adverse human rights impacts. Potential impacts should be addressed through prevention or mitigation, while actual impacts — those that have already occurred — should be a subject for remediation. This directly parallels the mitigation hierarchy structure found in IFC PS1 (avoid → minimize → mitigate → compensate), but applies it specifically to human rights impacts rather than broader environmental and social risk.

**Consultation and assessment:** In assessing human rights impacts, business enterprises look for both actual and potential adverse impacts, and where meaningful consultation with potentially affected groups and other relevant stakeholders is not possible, business enterprises should consider reasonable alternatives such as consulting credible, independent expert resources, including human rights defenders and others from civil society. The assessment of human rights impacts informs subsequent steps in the human rights due diligence process.

**Integration across the business:** The horizontal integration across the business enterprise of specific findings from assessing human rights impacts can only be effective if its human rights policy commitment has been embedded into all relevant business functions — meaning HRDD is not effective as a compliance-only exercise but requires findings to genuinely inform decisions across procurement, operations, security, and community relations functions.

### Salient Human Rights Issues

A key implementation concept the UNGPs give rise to (though the term itself is more fully developed in subsequent OHCHR and practitioner guidance than in the 31 Principles' text) is the concept of salient human rights issues — the concept of salient human rights issues is key to implementation of the UNGPs and human rights due diligence, referring to the human rights at risk of the most severe negative impact through a company's activities and business relationships. This concept helps companies prioritize HRDD effort proportionate to severity (scale, scope, and irremediable character) rather than treating all potential human rights issues as equally urgent.

### Access to Remedy (Pillar III)

Pillar III addresses the gap that can arise when both state judicial systems and company-level mechanisms fail to provide accessible, effective recourse to people harmed by business activity. It encompasses:

- **State-based judicial mechanisms** — courts and formal legal process.
- **State-based non-judicial mechanisms** — such as National Contact Points under the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, national human rights institutions, and other administrative bodies.
- **Non-state-based grievance mechanisms** — operational-level grievance mechanisms established directly by business enterprises, which the UNGPs encourage as an early-stage, accessible complement to formal remedy channels. This directly parallels the grievance redress mechanisms required under IFC PS1, World Bank ESS10, and Equator Principles Principle 6 — the UNGPs provide the underlying rights-based rationale for why such mechanisms matter, while the safeguard standards operationalize them at the project level.

### Corporate Human Rights Due Diligence: Independent Responsibility

The Guiding Principles clarify that all business enterprises have an independent responsibility to respect human rights, and that in order to do so they are required to exercise human rights due diligence to identify, prevent, mitigate and account for how they address impacts on human rights. Human rights due diligence is a way for enterprises to proactively manage potential and actual adverse human rights impacts with which they are involved, and critically, the prevention of adverse impacts on people is the main purpose of human rights due diligence — it concerns risks to people, not risks to business. This people-centered framing distinguishes HRDD's stated purpose from conventional enterprise risk management, even where the two processes are operationally integrated.

### Global Adoption and Convergence

Since their endorsement, the Guiding Principles have driven a convergence in standards on business and human rights across the international arena, reinforcing their position as the authoritative global standard on business and human rights. This convergence effect is visible in the frameworks already covered in this chapter:

- **IFC PS7** builds human rights due diligence concepts into its Indigenous Peoples requirements.
- **Equator Principles EP4** explicitly directs clients to refer to the UNGPs for human rights risk and impact assessment.
- National governments have adopted National Action Plans implementing the UNGPs; the UK was the first country in the world to publish a National Action Plan for business and human rights, and the UNGPs and UN Framework were significant factors in the European Commission's 2011 adoption of a new CSR definition — "the responsibility of enterprises for their impacts on society" — replacing an earlier definition that limited CSR to voluntary activities.
- Some jurisdictions have moved toward mandatory human rights due diligence legislation, building on the voluntary UNGP framework with binding legal requirements. ### Practical Example

**Example:** A multinational agribusiness company sources palm oil from smallholder and plantation suppliers in a region where land disputes and reports of labor rights concerns have been documented by civil society organizations.

- **Policy commitment:** The company adopts a board-approved human rights policy statement committing to respect internationally recognized human rights across its own operations and supply chain, referencing the UNGPs explicitly.
- **Impact assessment:** The company conducts a human rights impact assessment (HRIA) of its supply chain, engaging directly with affected smallholder communities and, where direct consultation is constrained by access or safety concerns in certain areas, supplementing with input from credible independent civil society organizations and human rights defenders familiar with the region.
- **Salience prioritization:** Based on severity analysis, the company identifies land tenure disputes and forced/child labor risk in its supply chain as salient human rights issues warranting priority attention over lower-severity administrative concerns.
- **Integration and action:** Findings are embedded into sourcing policy (supplier codes of conduct), procurement decision criteria, and field-level community relations practice — not confined to a standalone sustainability report.
- **Tracking:** The company establishes ongoing supplier audits and community feedback channels to track whether mitigation measures (e.g., supplier corrective action plans on land tenure documentation) are effective over time.
- **Communication:** Annual public reporting communicates how salient human rights impacts are being addressed, consistent with the UNGP Reporting Framework's four-step disclosure structure.
- **Remedy:** An operational-level grievance mechanism is established at the plantation and supply-chain level, allowing affected community members and workers to raise concerns directly, complementing (not replacing) their access to formal judicial or National Contact Point remedy channels.

### Key Points

- The UNGPs (2011) are the first UN-endorsed global standard on business and human rights, authored by John Ruggie and structured around the Protect-Respect-Remedy framework.
- Three pillars — State Duty to Protect, Corporate Responsibility to Respect, and Access to Remedy — organize the 31 Guiding Principles.
- The corporate responsibility to respect human rights exists independently of whether the host state fulfills its own protective duty — a company cannot rely on weak state enforcement as justification for inaction.
- Human rights due diligence (HRDD) is the operational core of Pillar II: assess impacts → integrate and act on findings → track responses → communicate how impacts are addressed (Guiding Principle 17).
- HRDD is people-centered, concerning risks to rights-holders rather than risks to the company — a key distinction from conventional enterprise risk management even when the two are integrated.
- Salient human rights issues (those at risk of the most severe impact) provide the prioritization lens for HRDD effort.
- Access to remedy encompasses state-based judicial and non-judicial mechanisms alongside company-level operational grievance mechanisms — directly underpinning the grievance mechanism requirements found in IFC PS1, World Bank ESS10, and Equator Principles Principle 6.
- The UNGPs function as the foundational cross-cutting human rights reference incorporated by name into other frameworks in this chapter, particularly IFC PS7 and Equator Principles EP4.
