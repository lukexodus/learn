## GRI 413 disclosures on local communities

### Overview and Regulatory Context

GRI 413: Local Communities 2016 contains disclosures for organizations to report information about their impacts related to local communities, and how they manage these impacts. It is a Topic Standard within the GRI Standards system, issued by the Global Sustainability Standards Board (GSSB), with an effective date of 1 July 2018.

Unlike the safeguard frameworks covered elsewhere in this chapter (IFC PS1, World Bank ESS10, Equator Principles, AA1000SES), GRI 413 is not a project-financing condition or a stakeholder engagement design methodology — it is a sustainability reporting disclosure standard. It governs what an organization discloses publicly about its community impacts and management approach, rather than prescribing how a specific project's engagement or impact assessment process must be conducted. This positions GRI 413 as the disclosure/transparency layer that typically sits downstream of the operational processes established under PS1, ESS10, or AA1000SES.

### Definition of "Local Communities"

GRI 413 relies on a deliberately broad definitional scope. In the GRI Standards, local communities are defined as individuals or groups of individuals living or working in areas that are affected or that could be affected by the organization's activities. The local community can range from those living adjacent to the organization's operations to those living at a distance.

This breadth mirrors the affected-party concept found in IFC PS1 and World Bank ESS10, but GRI 413 does not itself define categories (affected/interested/vulnerable) the way ESS10 does — it leaves community identification methodology to the reporting organization, to be described as part of its management approach disclosure.

### Scope of Impact

GRI 413 anchors its rationale in a documented impact premise: an organization's activities and infrastructure can have significant economic, social, cultural, and/or environmental impacts on local communities. Where possible, organizations are expected to anticipate and avoid negative impacts, and to identify ways to enhance positive impacts — establishing an underlying avoid-then-mitigate logic conceptually consistent with the mitigation hierarchy found in IFC PS1.

### Structure of the Standard

GRI 413 is structured in two main sections, designed to work alongside the cross-cutting GRI Universal Standards rather than stand alone:

1. **Management approach disclosures** — reported using Disclosure 3-3 in GRI 3: Material Topics 2021 (in the current architecture) or the legacy GRI 103: Management Approach standard. An organization that has determined local communities to be a material topic is required to report how it manages the topic using this disclosure; GRI 413's own Section 1 requirement is designed to supplement — and not replace — that management approach disclosure.
2. **Topic-specific disclosures** — the substantive local-communities disclosures unique to GRI 413: Disclosure 413-1 and Disclosure 413-2.

### Disclosure 413-1: Operations with Local Community Engagement, Impact Assessments, and Development Programs

Disclosure 413-1 requires organizations to report the percentage of operations with implemented local community engagement, impact assessments, and/or development programs, including the use of specified mechanism types. Reported mechanism categories commonly include:

- Social impact assessments, including gender impact assessments, based on participatory processes
- Environmental impact assessments and ongoing monitoring
- Public disclosure of results of environmental and social impact assessments
- Local community development programs based on local communities' needs
- Stakeholder engagement plans based on stakeholder mapping
- Broad-based local community consultation committees and processes that include vulnerable groups
- Works councils, occupational health and safety committees, and other worker representation bodies to deal with impacts
- Formal local community grievance processes

**Denominator guidance:** When compiling the information specified in Disclosure 413-1, the reporting organization should use data on the total number of operations (as reported elsewhere in the organization's General Disclosures) to establish the denominator against which the reported percentage is calculated — meaning a coherent, consistent operations count underpins the percentage figure's comparability.

### Disclosure 413-2: Operations with Significant Actual and Potential Negative Impacts on Local Communities

Disclosure 413-2 requires organizations to identify and describe operations with significant actual and potential negative impacts on local communities, and — critically — this disclosure informs stakeholders about an organization's awareness of its negative impacts on local communities, and enables the organization to better prioritize and improve its organization-wide attention to local communities.

**Scope boundary:** This disclosure is focused on significant actual and potential negative impacts related to an organization's operations and not on community investments or donations, which are addressed by a separate standard, GRI 201: Economic Performance 2016 — a deliberate separation preventing organizations from offsetting disclosure of negative impacts with positive community-investment framing.

**Required content for each significant impact**, the reporting organization should, for each of the significant actual and potential negative economic, social, cultural, and/or environmental impacts on local communities and their rights, describe the operation and the specific impact.

**Vulnerability and risk factors:** When compiling Disclosure 413-2, the reporting organization should report the vulnerability and risk to local communities from potential negative impacts due to factors including:

- The degree of physical or economic isolation of the local community.
- The level of socioeconomic development, including the degree of gender equality within the community.
- The state of socioeconomic infrastructure, including health and education infrastructure.

This vulnerability-factor list is conceptually aligned with the differentiated vulnerable-groups treatment found across IFC PS4, PS5, and PS7, though GRI 413 frames it as a disclosure content requirement rather than an operational management obligation.

### Omission and Reasons for Non-Reporting

GRI 413 incorporates the general GRI omission mechanism rather than a unique provision of its own. If the organization cannot report the required information about an item specified in a disclosure because the item (e.g., committee, policy, practice, process) does not exist, it can comply with the requirement by reporting this to be the case, and the organization can explain the reasons for not having this item, or describe any plans to develop it. Notably, the disclosure does not require the organization to implement the item (e.g., developing a policy), but to report that the item does or does not exist — meaning GRI 413 is fundamentally a transparency requirement, not a performance mandate compelling specific community engagement practices.

### Relationship to Other GRI Standards and External Frameworks

GRI 413 does not operate in isolation. It is designed to be used alongside:

- **GRI 3: Material Topics 2021** (or legacy GRI 103) for the management approach narrative.
- **GRI 201: Economic Performance 2016** — the dividing line for community investment/donation disclosures, kept separate from 413-2's negative-impact focus.
- **GRI 411: Rights of Indigenous Peoples** — organizations with indigenous-lands impacts are directed to report certain matters there rather than duplicating within 413.
- **GRI 416: Customer Health and Safety** — a cross-reference boundary where health/safety impacts on communities as consumers, rather than as local residents, are addressed elsewhere.

GRI 413's development also drew directly on the international safeguard architecture covered in this chapter. The IFC's Guidance Notes: Performance Standards on Environmental and Social Sustainability, 2012 is cited as an informing reference for the standard, alongside instruments such as the OECD Risk Awareness Tool for Multinational Enterprises — situating GRI 413 as a disclosure-oriented codification of concepts already embedded in the IFC Performance Standards framework, rather than a competing methodology.

### Comparative Position within the International Safeguard Architecture

| Dimension | GRI 413 | IFC PS1 / ESS10 / Equator Principles | AA1000SES |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary function | Public disclosure/reporting | Operational risk management, financing condition | Engagement process design |
| Trigger | Materiality determination + voluntary GRI-aligned reporting | Development finance/project finance transaction | Voluntary organizational adoption |
| Core deliverable | Percentage/narrative disclosures in a sustainability report | ESMS, SEP, ESIA, RAP, grievance mechanism | Four-stage engagement plan/process |
| Consequence of gaps | Reputational/reporting-integrity; omission permitted with explanation | Can affect loan covenants, disbursement | Assurance credibility |

Practitioners often use GRI 413 as the external reporting vehicle for engagement and impact-assessment activity that was originally designed and executed to satisfy IFC PS1/PS4/PS5/PS7 requirements or structured using AA1000SES methodology — the underlying activity (impact assessment, SEP, grievance mechanism) feeds both the operational safeguard compliance file and the public GRI disclosure.

### Practical Example

**Example:** A mining company with operations in three countries prepares its annual GRI-referenced sustainability report and needs to compile Disclosure 413-1 and 413-2 for its flagship open-pit operation, which is also subject to IFC PS1/PS4/PS5 requirements under project financing.

- **413-1 compilation:** The company reports that 100% of its flagship operation has implemented local community engagement mechanisms, citing its Stakeholder Engagement Plan (originally developed to satisfy IFC PS1), a participatory social impact assessment conducted during permitting, and a formal community grievance mechanism (the same GRM required under PS1/PS4). The denominator for the percentage calculation uses the total operations count reported in the company's general disclosures.
- **413-2 compilation:** The company identifies the flagship operation as having significant actual and potential negative impacts — specifically, resettlement-related displacement (already documented under its PS5 Resettlement Action Plan) and dust/noise impacts on a nearby village. For each impact, it describes the operation and the specific impact, and reports vulnerability factors: the affected village's physical isolation (limited road access), lower socioeconomic development relative to the regional average, and constrained health infrastructure — drawing directly on baseline data already collected for the PS1 social baseline assessment.
- **Omission handling:** For a smaller, lower-risk operation without a formal impact assessment process, the company reports this absence transparently under the GRI omission mechanism, explaining that a focused assessment is planned but not yet completed, rather than fabricating compliance.
- **Cross-referencing:** The report notes that community investment and philanthropic programs at the same operation are reported separately under GRI 201, keeping the negative-impact-focused 413-2 disclosure analytically distinct from voluntary community investment reporting.

[Inference: the mining company scenario is illustrative rather than drawn from a documented case; the described reuse of PS1/PS4/PS5-derived data for GRI 413 compilation reflects standard sustainability-reporting practice and the documented informing relationship between GRI 413 and IFC's Performance Standards Guidance Notes.]

### Key Points

- GRI 413 is a public disclosure standard, not an operational engagement or risk-management framework — it reports on impacts and management approach rather than prescribing how engagement must be conducted.
- "Local communities" is defined broadly, ranging from those living adjacent to operations to those living at a distance who are or could be affected.
- The standard requires two topic-specific disclosures: 413-1 (percentage of operations with implemented engagement/assessment/development mechanisms) and 413-2 (operations with significant negative impacts, including vulnerability/risk factor reporting).
- 413-2 explicitly excludes community investment/donation reporting, which is directed instead to GRI 201.
- GRI's omission mechanism allows organizations to report the absence of a mechanism rather than compelling its implementation — GRI 413 mandates transparency, not specific practice.
- GRI 413 was informed by, and is commonly fed by data generated under, the IFC Performance Standards framework — particularly PS1's social baseline assessments, PS4/PS5 impact documentation, and PS1-derived Stakeholder Engagement Plans and grievance mechanisms.
- Practitioners frequently treat GRI 413 as the downstream public-reporting layer for engagement and assessment work originally undertaken to satisfy IFC, World Bank, or Equator Principles safeguard requirements.
