## Distinguishing rights-holders from general stakeholders

### Overview and Conceptual Foundation

The distinction between rights-holders and general stakeholders is a foundational analytical move in contemporary community engagement and Social Impact Assessment (SIA) practice. Both terms describe people or groups with a connection to a project, but they rest on fundamentally different bases of claim, and conflating them produces engagement processes that treat a fence-line resident with a customary land right the same as a distant shareholder with a financial interest — a category error with real consequences for prioritization, consent requirements, and remedy design.

The distinction has become more analytically prominent as human-rights-based approaches to business (particularly the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, covered elsewhere in this chapter) have gained traction alongside the conventional stakeholder theory tradition that dominated corporate social responsibility practice through the 1990s and 2000s.

### Defining "Stakeholder"

Stakeholder is the broader, more inclusive term, generally defined as any individual, group, or organization that can affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by an organization's decisions or activities. This is an interest-based or influence-based category, encompassing:

- Those with a **material interest** (financial, operational, reputational) in the project's outcome.
- Those with **influence** over the project (regulators, financiers, industry associations, media).
- Those **affected** by the project, positively or negatively, whether or not they hold any formal legal claim or entitlement related to that effect.

Stakeholder theory's classical formulations (e.g., Freeman's foundational corporate-strategy framing) were developed primarily to describe who a business should account for in its decision-making from a management and strategic-risk perspective, not from a legal or moral-entitlement perspective. This origin matters: a stakeholder-only lens tends to prioritize by influence and proximity to business risk, which does not reliably track who holds the strongest legitimate claim to be heard, protected, or compensated.

### Defining "Rights-Holder"

Rights-holder is a narrower, claim-based term. A rights-holder is a person or group whose human rights, legal entitlements, or recognized customary rights are or may be affected by a project's activities — the claim rests on an entitlement recognized in international human rights law, national law, or customary/traditional systems, rather than on interest, influence, or mere proximity.

Rights-holders include, among others:

- Individuals and communities whose **internationally recognized human rights** (e.g., rights to life, health, adequate housing, water, livelihood, cultural practice, non-discrimination) may be affected by project activities.
- **Indigenous Peoples** holding collective rights to land, resources, self-determination, and cultural continuity — the category explicitly addressed by IFC Performance Standard 7 and instruments such as UNDRIP, covered elsewhere in this chapter.
- **Workers**, who hold labor rights recognized in national law and international labor standards.
- **Land users and titleholders** — including customary and non-titled users — whose entitlements are addressed under frameworks such as IFC Performance Standard 5.
- Communities holding recognized or customary rights to **cultural heritage**, addressed under IFC Performance Standard 8 and, for Indigenous Peoples specifically, PS7.

The critical distinguishing feature is that a rights-holder's claim exists independently of the project and independently of whether the company or financing institution recognizes it — it derives from a pre-existing entitlement, not from the project's decision to engage or not engage.

### The Critical Asymmetry: Not All Stakeholders Are Rights-Holders

Every rights-holder is, by definition, also a stakeholder — a person whose human rights are affected by a project is necessarily affected by it. However, the converse does not hold: many stakeholders are not rights-holders in the technical sense. A shareholder demanding better financial disclosure, a competitor tracking market entry, an industry association monitoring sector reputation, or a media outlet covering the project are all legitimate stakeholders whose interests and influence may matter to project management — but none of them hold a rights-based claim against the project's human-rights or land/resource impacts in the way an affected community member or displaced land user does.

This asymmetry is the practical payoff of the distinction: it prevents dilution of prioritization. If every interested party is treated identically to a person whose water source, home, or livelihood is directly at stake, engagement resources and remedy mechanisms risk being allocated by influence and vocal presence rather than by severity of actual or potential harm.

### Why the Distinction Matters in Practice

**1. Prioritization under resource constraints.** SIA and community engagement work rarely has unlimited time, budget, or access. Sorting the full stakeholder universe into "stakeholders" and "rights-holders" allows practitioners to ensure that people with the most severe, direct, and legitimate claims are not crowded out by louder or more accessible interest groups.

**2. Different obligations attach to each category.** As documented elsewhere in this chapter, IFC Performance Standard 1 distinguishes broad stakeholder engagement from the specific, heightened requirements owed to Affected Communities; World Bank ESS10 similarly separates affected parties from other interested parties; and IFC Performance Standard 7's Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) requirement applies specifically to Indigenous Peoples as rights-holders — not to the general stakeholder universe. Consultation intensity, consent thresholds, and remedy obligations are calibrated to rights-holder status, not general stakeholder status.

**3. Legitimacy of claim versus legitimacy of interest.** A rights-holder's claim carries a different moral and often legal weight than a stakeholder's interest. A company may reasonably decline to accommodate a stakeholder's preference (e.g., a competitor's objection to project approval) while being unable to similarly disregard a rights-holder's legitimate claim (e.g., a resettled community's right to compensation at replacement cost) without violating recognized standards.

**4. Grievance mechanism design.** Effective operational-level grievance mechanisms, as encouraged under the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, are typically designed with particular attention to accessibility for rights-holders — since it is rights-holders whose access to remedy the UNGPs' Pillar III is specifically concerned with, even though a well-designed mechanism may also receive general stakeholder input.

**5. Avoiding "stakeholder-washing" of rights claims.** Treating rights-holders as merely another stakeholder category among many — to be balanced against shareholder or operational convenience through ordinary stakeholder-management trade-offs — risks understating the seriousness of the underlying claim. Human rights and land/resource entitlements are not simply one interest to be weighed against others in a purely commercial calculus; this is a key reason the UNGPs and IFC's Performance Standards deliberately use rights-based language for the categories they intend to protect most robustly.

### Overlapping and Contested Terrain

The distinction is analytically clear but can be operationally contested in specific cases:

- **Disputed entitlement status.** Whether a given individual or group holds a recognized right (particularly customary or informal land rights, as addressed under IFC PS5) is often a matter requiring careful due diligence rather than self-evident classification — misclassifying a rights-holder as a mere stakeholder can result in a serious compliance and harm-prevention failure.
- **Evolving rights recognition.** National law may lag behind international human rights standards in recognizing certain claims (e.g., customary land tenure, Indigenous self-determination), meaning practitioners applying international safeguard standards (IFC PS7, UNGPs) may need to identify rights-holders whose status is not yet reflected in domestic legal recognition.
- **Organizations acting on behalf of rights-holders.** NGOs, community-based organizations, and civil society groups are typically stakeholders (as advocates and interested parties) even when they are not themselves rights-holders — though they may represent or amplify rights-holders' voices, and distinguishing their own stakeholder status from the rights-holder status of those they represent avoids conflating representation with the underlying claim.
- **Workers as a hybrid case.** Workers are simultaneously stakeholders (with an interest in employment continuity) and rights-holders (holding labor rights under national and international labor standards) — illustrating that rights-holder status is an additional, claim-based layer that can sit alongside, rather than replace, stakeholder status.

### Methodological Implications for SIA Practice

A rigorous SIA or community engagement process should build the rights-holder/stakeholder distinction into its methodology from the outset:

1. **Separate identification tracks.** Stakeholder mapping (influence/interest-based) and rights-holder identification (claim-based, requiring land tenure research, human rights screening, and Indigenous Peoples identification criteria) should be conducted as related but distinct analytical exercises, not collapsed into a single undifferentiated list.
2. **Differentiated engagement intensity.** Consultation and disclosure obligations should scale with rights-holder status — reflecting the proportionality principles found in IFC PS1 and World Bank ESS10, where rights-holders (particularly those facing displacement, Indigenous Peoples, or those with critical cultural heritage claims) warrant the most intensive, ongoing, and in some cases consent-based engagement.
3. **Documentation of the classification basis.** Because rights-holder status carries legal and procedural consequences (e.g., triggering FPIC under PS7, or replacement-cost compensation under PS5), the basis for classifying a given individual or group as a rights-holder should be documented and defensible — not asserted informally.
4. **Avoiding both under- and over-inclusion.** Under-inclusion (failing to recognize a legitimate rights-holder) risks serious harm and compliance failure; over-inclusion (labeling every interested party a rights-holder) dilutes the category's practical and moral force and can create unrealistic expectations about consent thresholds that do not, in fact, apply to general stakeholders.

### Practical Example

**Example:** A proposed hydropower dam project's stakeholder universe includes: (a) downstream fishing communities who depend on river flow for their livelihoods, (b) an Indigenous community with ancestral territory partially within the reservoir footprint, (c) a national environmental NGO advocating against large dams generally, (d) a regional business chamber supporting the project for economic development reasons, (e) construction contractors' workers, and (f) institutional investors evaluating the project bond.

- **General stakeholders (not rights-holders in this context):** The environmental NGO (an advocate with organizational interest, but not itself bearing a direct rights claim from this specific project), the regional business chamber (an interest-based stakeholder), and institutional investors (financial stakeholders). These groups warrant engagement — disclosure, consultation on concerns, responsiveness to input — but their claims are interest-based, not entitlement-based.
- **Rights-holders:** The downstream fishing communities (whose livelihood and, potentially, water-access rights are directly and materially affected — triggering IFC PS4/PS5-type consideration), the Indigenous community (triggering IFC PS7's heightened consultation and potential FPIC requirement given territorial and resettlement impacts), and the construction workers (labor rights under PS2 and applicable national law).
- **Practical consequence of the distinction:** The project's Stakeholder Engagement Plan includes all six groups, but the engagement intensity, disclosure timing, and consent thresholds differ sharply — the Indigenous community receives FPIC-standard negotiation per PS7, the fishing communities receive PS5-aligned compensation/livelihood-restoration consultation, while the NGO and business chamber receive standard informational disclosure and consultation opportunities without a parallel consent or compensation obligation.
- **Where classification required scrutiny:** Initial stakeholder mapping treated the fishing communities as general "interested parties" until a dedicated land- and resource-use due diligence exercise confirmed their livelihood dependency constituted a rights-based claim under PS5's economic displacement provisions — illustrating why rights-holder classification often requires deliberate investigation rather than being apparent from an initial stakeholder list.

### Key Points

- Stakeholders are defined by interest or influence (affecting or being affected by, or perceiving themselves affected by, project decisions); rights-holders are defined by a recognized entitlement in human rights law, national law, or customary/traditional systems.
- Every rights-holder is a stakeholder, but not every stakeholder is a rights-holder — this asymmetry is the practical basis for differentiated prioritization.
- Rights-holder categories relevant to SIA/community engagement practice include: communities facing displacement (PS5), Indigenous Peoples (PS7, UNDRIP), workers (PS2, labor law), and holders of cultural heritage claims (PS7/PS8).
- Different consultation intensity, consent thresholds (e.g., FPIC), and remedy obligations attach specifically to rights-holder status under the frameworks covered elsewhere in this chapter (IFC PS1/PS5/PS7, World Bank ESS10, UNGPs).
- Rights-holder status often requires dedicated due diligence (land tenure research, Indigenous identification criteria, human rights screening) to establish — it is not always self-evident from an initial stakeholder list.
- Organizations advocating on behalf of rights-holders (NGOs, civil society groups) are typically themselves stakeholders rather than rights-holders, though they may amplify or represent rights-holder voices.
- Over-inclusion (labeling all stakeholders as rights-holders) dilutes the category's protective force; under-inclusion (failing to recognize genuine rights-holders) risks serious harm and compliance failure — both should be actively avoided through documented, defensible classification.
