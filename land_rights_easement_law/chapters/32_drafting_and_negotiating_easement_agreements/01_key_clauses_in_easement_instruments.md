## Key Clauses in Easement Instruments


### Purpose and Function of Clause Architecture

An easement instrument is the operative legal document that creates, defines, and limits a nonpossessory interest in land. Its clauses collectively answer five questions that determine enforceability and scope: who holds the right, what land is burdened and benefited, what activity is permitted, how the right may change over time, and what happens on breach or termination. Poorly drafted clauses are the leading cause of easement litigation — ambiguity in scope or location clauses in particular generates a disproportionate share of reported disputes.

### Identification of Parties Clause

**Function**

Establishes the grantor (servient estate owner) and grantee (dominant estate owner or, for easements in gross, the individual/entity holding the right directly). Must identify parties with legal precision sufficient to bind successors.

**Key Points**

- Full legal names and, for entities, jurisdiction of organization and entity type
- Recital of the parties' respective property interests (fee owner, leasehold, life estate) since a grantor's authority to burden the land depends on the estate held
- For utility or commercial easements in gross, specify whether the right is personal (non-assignable) or commercial (freely assignable) — this distinction has significant consequences at common law, where easements in gross were historically non-assignable absent explicit language

**Example**

> "This Easement Agreement is entered into by [Grantor Name], a fee simple owner of the Servient Property described in Exhibit A ('Grantor'), and [Grantee Name], a [state] corporation, owner of the Dominant Property described in Exhibit B ('Grantee')."

### Recitals (Background) Clause

**Function**

Non-operative but interpretively significant — recitals establish the parties' shared understanding of purpose, which courts frequently consult when operative language is ambiguous. Under the *contra proferentem* doctrine and general contract-interpretation principles, recitals can resolve disputes about the drafters' intent.

**Key Points**

- State the underlying transaction context (e.g., "Grantor desires to grant Grantee a right of access to install and maintain utility lines")
- Avoid recitals that contradict the operative grant — courts generally give the operative clause precedence, but inconsistency invites litigation
- [Inference] Well-drafted recitals reduce the probability that a court will need to resort to extrinsic evidence, since they memorialize purpose within the four corners of the instrument.

### Granting Clause (Words of Conveyance)

**Function**

The operative clause that actually creates the interest. Must use words of present grant ("grants, conveys, and creates") rather than future or conditional language, which can be construed as a mere license or executory contract to grant rather than an immediate property interest.

**Key Points**

- Specify the estate created: "an easement" (nonpossessory) versus inadvertently using language suggesting a fee or leasehold transfer
- State whether the easement is **appurtenant** (attached to and running with the dominant estate) or **in gross** (personal to the grantee)
- Include express durability language: "perpetual," "for a term of [X] years," or conditions of defeasance

**Example**

> "Grantor hereby grants, conveys, and creates unto Grantee a perpetual, non-exclusive easement appurtenant to the Dominant Property, over, under, and across the Easement Area described in Exhibit C, for the purposes set forth in Section 3 below."

### Legal Description and Easement Area Clause

**Function**

Defines the physical location and dimensions of the burdened land. This is the single highest-litigation-risk clause; imprecise or "floating" descriptions (e.g., "a reasonable path across Grantor's land") produce disputes over exact routing.

**Key Points**

- Preferred: metes-and-bounds description prepared by a licensed surveyor, referenced to a recorded plat or exhibit
- Acceptable alternative for utility easements: centerline description with a specified width (e.g., "15 feet on either side of the centerline described as follows...")
- **Floating easements** (location to be fixed later by use or subsequent agreement) should include a mechanism for fixing the final location — either by reference to "the location as actually constructed" or a defined selection procedure — since indefinite floating easements risk being held void for vagueness in some jurisdictions
- Attach a survey exhibit (Exhibit) and incorporate it by reference

### Purpose and Scope of Use Clause

**Function**

Defines the permitted activities within the easement area. Scope clauses are construed narrowly by most courts — activity not fairly encompassed by the stated purpose, or not reasonably necessary to it, generally falls outside the grant even if it occurs within the physical easement area.

**Key Points**

- Enumerate specific permitted uses (e.g., "ingress, egress, and installation, operation, maintenance, repair, and replacement of underground electric distribution lines")
- Address whether the scope may expand with technological change (relevant for utility and telecommunications easements — courts are split on whether a general "utility easement" covers later-developed technologies like fiber optic cable)
- State whether the easement is **exclusive** (excluding even the servient owner from that use) or **non-exclusive**
- Specify permitted ancillary rights: vehicular access, equipment staging, vegetation clearing, temporary construction rights

### Maintenance and Repair Obligations Clause

**Function**

Allocates responsibility for upkeep of the easement area and any improvements within it (roads, pipelines, drainage structures).

**Key Points**

- Specify which party bears cost and responsibility for routine maintenance versus capital repairs
- Address restoration obligations after maintenance activity (e.g., regrading, reseeding, resurfacing disturbed ground)
- Consider a cost-sharing formula for shared-use easements (e.g., shared driveways) proportional to use or benefited parcels
- Address default consequences if the responsible party fails to maintain — self-help rights, cost recovery, lien rights

### Indemnification and Insurance Clause

**Function**

Allocates liability risk arising from use of the easement, particularly important where the grantee's activity creates hazard exposure on the servient estate (construction, utility work, public access).

**Key Points**

- Mutual or one-directional indemnification, scoped to claims "arising out of or related to" the grantee's exercise of the easement
- Carve-outs for the indemnitee's own negligence or willful misconduct (many jurisdictions void broad indemnification for a party's own gross negligence as against public policy)
- Minimum insurance requirements: commercial general liability limits, additional-insured endorsements naming the servient owner, evidence-of-insurance delivery obligations

### Duration and Termination Clause

**Function**

Fixes the temporal scope of the right and the conditions under which it ends.

**Key Points**

- **Perpetual** easements run with the land indefinitely absent merger, abandonment, or express release
- **Term easements** specify an end date or triggering event
- **Defeasible** easements terminate automatically on a condition (e.g., "until Grantee ceases to operate a public utility on the Dominant Property")
- Address **abandonment** standards — mere nonuse is typically insufficient at common law; most jurisdictions require nonuse plus affirmative intent to abandon, evidenced by conduct
- Include a **termination/release mechanism**: recordable instrument requirements so title records reflect extinguishment

### Maintenance of Priority and Subordination Clause

**Function**

Addresses the easement's priority relative to existing liens, mortgages, and other encumbrances on the servient estate — critical because a foreclosure of a senior lien can extinguish a junior easement.

**Key Points**

- Obtain **subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment (SNDA)** agreements from existing mortgagees where the easement is junior in time, or condition the grant on lender consent
- Title insurance endorsement addressing the easement's insured priority position

### Assignability and Successors-and-Assigns Clause

**Function**

Determines whether the benefit and/or burden transfers automatically to successive owners, and whether the grantee may assign the right to third parties.

**Key Points**

- Appurtenant easements generally run with the land automatically; the clause should confirm this and state that the burden binds "heirs, successors, and assigns" of the servient estate
- Easements in gross require explicit assignability language to be transferable in many jurisdictions (reflecting the historical common-law presumption against alienability of personal easements)
- Address whether **divisibility** (splitting the benefit among multiple grantees, e.g., a utility assigning portions to co-locating providers) is permitted — the *one stock rule* limiting subdivision of easement benefits is relevant here

### Dispute Resolution Clause

**Function**

Specifies the forum and method for resolving disagreements over interpretation, scope, or breach.

**Key Points**

- Governing law and venue selection
- Arbitration versus litigation election; if arbitration, specify rules (AAA, JAMS) and whether binding
- Attorneys' fees provision (prevailing-party fee shifting) — absent this clause, the American Rule leaves each party bearing its own fees in most U.S. jurisdictions

### Recordation Clause

**Function**

Ensures the instrument is recorded in the land records to provide constructive notice to subsequent purchasers and encumbrancers, and to satisfy the "touch and concern" and notice requirements for the burden to run with the land against bona fide purchasers.

**Key Points**

- Specify the recording office and confirm the instrument is in recordable form (acknowledgment/notarization per jurisdictional requirements)
- Cross-reference in both the servient and dominant parcel's chain of title where local practice allows

### Miscellaneous / Boilerplate Provisions

**Key Points**

- Entire agreement clause
- Amendment requirements (writing, signed by both parties, recorded)
- Severability
- Notice provisions (addresses, method, deemed-delivery timing)
- Force majeure, if construction obligations are involved

**Next Steps**

- Exclusivity designation

- Indemnification and insurance requirements
- Duration, defeasance, and abandonment standards

- Dispute resolution and fee-shifting

- Signature blocks with proper acknowledgment for recording
