## Hotspots and Mantle Plumes

### Definition and Core Concept

A **hotspot** is a persistent, localized center of volcanic activity at Earth's surface that is not associated with a plate boundary, producing intraplate volcanism in settings otherwise tectonically quiescent. A **mantle plume** is the proposed deep-mantle mechanism most commonly invoked to explain hotspots: a narrow, buoyant, thermally-driven upwelling of unusually hot mantle material, hypothesized to rise from deep within the mantle (potentially originating near the core-mantle boundary) independently of the shallower, plate-boundary-associated mantle flow responsible for seafloor spreading and subduction. The two terms are related but distinct: "hotspot" describes the observed surface phenomenon, while "mantle plume" describes one proposed (and most widely favored) explanatory mechanism for it.

### The Plume Hypothesis: Origin and Structure

The plume concept was first proposed by W. Jason Morgan in 1971, building on earlier observations by J. Tuzo Wilson (1963) that certain volcanic island chains show a systematic age progression inconsistent with plate-boundary volcanism.

**Key Points**

- A mantle plume is typically modeled as having a broad, bulbous "plume head" trailing a narrower, sustained "plume tail" as it ascends through the mantle.
- The plume head, upon reaching the base of the lithosphere, can spread laterally and is associated with rapid, voluminous eruption of flood basalts (large igneous provinces), while the persistent plume tail sustains longer-term, more localized hotspot volcanism.
- Some plumes are hypothesized to originate at the core-mantle boundary, potentially linked to thermal boundary layer instabilities there, while others may originate at shallower mantle depths; the actual depth of origin for any specific plume remains a subject of ongoing seismic tomography research. *[Unverified: the depth of origin and structural continuity of individual mantle plumes through the mantle is not fully resolved and varies by specific case and research method.]*

### The Hotspot Trail Mechanism

The defining diagnostic feature of a hotspot is the production of a linear or curvilinear chain of progressively older volcanic features as a tectonic plate moves over a comparatively fixed (or slowly moving) mantle plume:

1. The plume remains in a roughly fixed position relative to deeper mantle reference frames (though this fixity itself has been increasingly questioned by paleomagnetic and geodynamic studies).
2. The overlying lithospheric plate continues its normal tectonic motion, carrying any volcanic edifice built above the plume progressively away from the active melting zone.
3. As the old volcanic edifice moves away from the plume, volcanic activity there ceases, and a new volcano begins forming at the plume's current surface location.
4. Repetition of this process over millions of years produces a chain of volcanic islands or seamounts that get systematically older with increasing distance from the currently active hotspot.

**Example**

The Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain is the archetypal example: the Big Island of Hawaii, presently situated directly above the active plume, is geologically young (still actively erupting), while islands and seamounts progressively northwest along the chain (Maui, Oahu, Kauai, and beyond into the submarine Emperor Seamounts) are successively older, with the oldest seamounts in the chain exceeding 80 million years in age. The chain also shows a distinct bend (~47 million years ago) interpreted as recording a change in the Pacific Plate's direction of motion (though the relative contribution of plate motion change versus possible plume motion itself to this bend remains debated among researchers).

### Hotspots Not Associated with Oceanic Island Chains

While the Hawaiian chain is the textbook oceanic example, hotspots also occur beneath continental lithosphere, producing distinctive geological features:

**Key Points**

- **Yellowstone hotspot**: currently situated beneath continental crust in the northwestern United States, associated with a track of progressively older silicic volcanic calderas extending back across the Snake River Plain, interpreted as recording the North American Plate's motion over the stationary hotspot.
- **Iceland**: represents a rare case of a hotspot coinciding with a mid-ocean ridge (the Mid-Atlantic Ridge), where plume-related excess magma supply is thought to explain Iceland's anomalously thick crust and above-sea-level ridge exposure.
- **Réunion hotspot**: associated with the Deccan Traps flood basalt province in India, illustrating the proposed link between an initial voluminous plume-head eruptive phase and subsequent long-lived plume-tail hotspot volcanism (Réunion Island today).

### Distinguishing Hotspot Volcanism from Plate-Boundary Volcanism

| Feature | Hotspot (Intraplate) Volcanism | Plate-Boundary Volcanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Location relative to boundaries | Within plate interiors, away from active boundaries | Along divergent or convergent boundaries |
| Driving mechanism | Proposed deep mantle plume upwelling | Decompression melting (divergent) or flux melting (convergent) |
| Age pattern | Systematic age progression along a linear trail | Broadly continuous along the active boundary |
| Composition | Often ocean-island basalt (OIB), geochemically distinct from mid-ocean ridge basalt | MORB (divergent) or andesitic/arc-related (convergent) |
| Example | Hawaii, Yellowstone, Réunion | Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Andean volcanic arc |

### Debates and Open Questions in Plume Research

**Key Points**

- The classical plume hypothesis, while widely used as an explanatory framework, is not universally accepted as the sole cause of all intraplate volcanism; some researchers have proposed alternative explanations (such as shallow lithospheric extension or fracture reactivation) for certain hotspot-like features. *[Unverified: the relative merits and scope of applicability of plume versus non-plume explanations for specific volcanic provinces remain actively debated in the geodynamics literature.]*
- The assumption that mantle plumes are entirely fixed in position relative to one another and to the deep mantle has been increasingly questioned; paleomagnetic and geochronological studies of hotspot tracks suggest some plumes may exhibit slow relative motion over geologic time, complicating their historical use as an absolute reference frame for plate motion reconstruction.
- Seismic tomography has provided increasing, though not universally conclusive, imaging support for narrow, deep-mantle low-velocity conduits beneath some major hotspots (interpreted as thermal plume conduits), while other proposed hotspots lack similarly clear deep-mantle seismic signatures.

### Common Misconceptions

**Key Points**

- Hotspots are not the same thing as volcanic arcs at subduction zones; hotspot volcanism occurs within plate interiors independent of plate boundary processes, while arc volcanism is directly tied to subduction-related flux melting.
- The mantle plume itself does not move with the tectonic plate above it; it is the plate that moves over the more nearly stationary (though not perfectly fixed) plume, which is what produces the observed age-progressive volcanic trail.
- Not every isolated volcanic feature within a plate interior is necessarily plume-related; some intraplate volcanism has been attributed by some researchers to alternative, non-plume mechanisms such as lithospheric flexure, fracture zone reactivation, or edge-driven convection.
- A hotspot trail's bend (such as the well-known bend in the Hawaiian-Emperor chain) does not automatically prove the plume itself moved; it is more commonly interpreted as recording a change in the overlying plate's direction of motion, though the alternative explanation of plume motion has also been proposed and is debated for this specific case.
