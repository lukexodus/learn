## Innocent Landowner, Bona Fide Prospective Purchaser, and Brownfields Defenses


### Overview

CERCLA's current-owner liability under §107(a)(1) is exceptionally broad — a property owner can be strictly liable regardless of whether it caused, contributed to, or even knew about contamination present when it acquired the property. Recognizing that this rule could paralyze real estate transactions and discourage redevelopment of contaminated urban land ("brownfields"), Congress carved out three interlocking landowner liability protections: the **innocent landowner defense** (originally 1986), the **contiguous property owner defense**, and the **bona fide prospective purchaser (BFPP) defense** (added by the 2002 Brownfields Amendments). All three share a common analytical backbone — the **"all appropriate inquiries" (AAI)** standard — but differ in the timing of acquisition, knowledge requirements, and ongoing obligations.

### Statutory and Regulatory Basis

- **CERCLA §101(35)** — 42 U.S.C. §9601(35): innocent landowner defense definitions
- **CERCLA §101(40)** — 42 U.S.C. §9601(40): bona fide prospective purchaser definition
- **CERCLA §107(b)(3)** — 42 U.S.C. §9607(b)(3): third-party defense (statutory basis for innocent landowner defense)
- **CERCLA §107(q)** — 42 U.S.C. §9607(q): contiguous property owner defense
- **CERCLA §107(r)** — 42 U.S.C. §9607(r): windfall lien provisions applicable to BFPPs
- **Small Business Liability Relief and Brownfields Revitalization Act of 2002** — Pub. L. 107-118: added BFPP defense and codified AAI requirement
- **All Appropriate Inquiries Rule** — 40 C.F.R. Part 312: EPA's regulatory standard for conducting AAI

### The "All Appropriate Inquiries" (AAI) Foundation

#### **Key Points**

All three landowner defenses require the party to have conducted **"all appropriate inquiries"** into the previous ownership, uses, and environmental condition of the property **prior to acquisition**. The AAI standard (40 C.F.R. Part 312) generally requires:

- A **Phase I Environmental Site Assessment** conducted in accordance with an EPA-recognized standard (most commonly **ASTM E1527**, the industry-standard Phase I ESA protocol).
- Review of historical sources: prior ownership and use records, aerial photographs, fire insurance maps, chain-of-title documents, and government records (e.g., environmental cleanup records, permit files).
- Interviews with past and present owners, operators, and occupants.
- A visual site inspection.
- Review of the reasonableness of the purchase price in light of contamination (if known or suspected).
- Commercially reasonable and generally available information about the property must be reviewed.
- The AAI investigation must generally be conducted or updated **within one year prior to acquisition**, with certain components (e.g., title search, environmental lien review, interviews) requiring updating **within 180 days** of acquisition.

#### **[Inference]** Practical Centrality of AAI

Because AAI compliance is a threshold, non-waivable element for all three defenses discussed here, environmental due diligence practice is built substantially around satisfying Part 312's requirements at the time of purchase — a deficient or absent Phase I ESA is typically fatal to any subsequent defense, regardless of how meritorious the underlying facts might otherwise be.

### The Innocent Landowner Defense

#### **Key Points**

- Codified through §101(35), which defines circumstances under which contamination is deemed to have been caused by an act or omission of a **third party** for purposes of the §107(b)(3) statutory defense — effectively making this a specialized application of the third-party defense rather than a wholly separate liability exemption.
- Applies where the current owner:
  1. Acquired the property **after** the disposal of hazardous substances occurred;
  2. **Did not know and had no reason to know** that hazardous substances were disposed of on the property at the time of acquisition, established by conducting AAI;
  3. Exercised **appropriate care** with respect to the hazardous substances found on the property, including taking reasonable steps to stop continuing releases, prevent threatened future releases, and prevent or limit human/environmental exposure;
  4. Provided **full cooperation, assistance, and access** to persons authorized to conduct response actions; and
  5. Complied with **land use restrictions and institutional controls**, and did not impede the effectiveness of institutional controls.
- The defense is generally understood as **unavailable** where the landowner has any familial, contractual, or corporate relationship with a party responsible for contamination — such relationships often preclude qualification as a truly innocent third party.

#### **Example**

A residential developer purchases a formerly agricultural parcel in 2005 without conducting AAI, later discovering pesticide contamination from decades-old agricultural chemical use. Because the developer failed to conduct AAI before purchase, it **cannot** invoke the innocent landowner defense — the "did not know and had no reason to know" element requires the diligence inquiry to have actually been performed, not merely that the landowner happened to be subjectively unaware.

### The Bona Fide Prospective Purchaser (BFPP) Defense

#### **Key Points**

- Added by the 2002 Brownfields Amendments specifically to address a critical gap: the innocent landowner defense is **unavailable** to a purchaser who has **actual knowledge** of contamination before acquiring the property (since such knowledge defeats the "no reason to know" element).
- The BFPP defense allows a purchaser to acquire property **with full knowledge of existing contamination** and still avoid CERCLA liability for pre-existing contamination, provided the purchaser satisfies §101(40)'s requirements:
  1. All disposal of hazardous substances occurred **before** the purchaser acquired the property;
  2. The purchaser conducted **AAI** prior to acquisition;
  3. The purchaser provides **all legally required notices** with respect to discovery or release of hazardous substances;
  4. The purchaser exercises **"appropriate care"** with respect to hazardous substances, including reasonable steps to stop continuing releases, prevent threatened future releases, and prevent/limit exposure;
  5. The purchaser provides **full cooperation, assistance, and access** to persons performing response actions;
  6. The purchaser complies with **land use restrictions and does not impede institutional controls**;
  7. The purchaser complies with **information requests and administrative subpoenas**; and
  8. The purchaser is **not potentially liable** or affiliated with any liable person through a family, contractual, corporate, or financial relationship (other than a relationship created by the instruments conveying title, a contract for sale of goods/services, or a security interest, unless the affiliation is a means of avoiding liability).

#### Distinguishing BFPP from Innocent Landowner

| Element | Innocent Landowner | BFPP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge of contamination at acquisition | Must have **no knowledge** | May have **full knowledge** |
| Statutory basis | §101(35) / §107(b)(3) | §101(40) |
| AAI required | Yes | Yes |
| Ongoing "appropriate care" obligation | Yes | Yes, explicitly enumerated |
| Primary use case | Buyer genuinely unaware of historical contamination | Buyer knowingly purchasing contaminated (often brownfield) property for redevelopment |

#### Windfall Lien Provision (§107(r))

- Even where a BFPP successfully avoids liability, the U.S. government may place a **lien on the property** (a "windfall lien") to the extent that EPA response costs increased the property's fair market value, up to the amount of that value increase.
- This ensures the government can recover some costs from the **increased property value** attributable to its cleanup, without imposing full CERCLA liability on the otherwise-protected BFPP.

### The Contiguous Property Owner Defense

#### **Key Points**

- Codified at §107(q), this defense protects an owner whose property became contaminated **solely by migration** from a source on an adjacent or nearby (contiguous) property, where the owner:
  1. Did not cause, contribute to, or consent to the release;
  2. Is not affiliated with the party responsible for the release;
  3. Conducts **appropriate care** and provides full cooperation to response authorities;
  4. Complies with land use restrictions and institutional controls; and
  5. Conducted **AAI** regarding the property prior to or at the time of acquisition.
- A landowner who **discovers** contamination has migrated onto its property from a neighboring source, and does not initially qualify (e.g., because it learns of the contamination only after acquisition and did not conduct pre-acquisition AAI), may potentially seek protection as a **BFPP instead**, depending on the facts, since the two defenses overlap in covering property affected by third-party contamination.

#### **[Inference]** Relationship to Innocent Landowner and BFPP

The contiguous property owner defense is most useful where a landowner's own property was never the source of contamination and the contaminating release originated entirely off-site — this is a narrower fact pattern than the innocent landowner or BFPP defenses, which contemplate contamination existing **on** the defended property itself (whether from historical on-site disposal predating acquisition, in the innocent landowner case, or known on-site contamination, in the BFPP case).

### Brownfields Program Context

#### **Key Points**

- "Brownfields" are properties where redevelopment or reuse is complicated by the presence or potential presence of contamination.
- The 2002 Brownfields Amendments, beyond creating the BFPP defense, established:
  - **Brownfields grant programs** administered by EPA to fund assessment, cleanup, and revolving loan programs for state and local governments, tribes, and nonprofits.
  - **De micromis and municipal solid waste exemptions**, reducing liability exposure for minimal contributors and generators of ordinary municipal waste, which helps facilitate brownfields transactions by narrowing the PRP universe.
  - Clarification of **state and local government liability protections** for involuntary acquisitions (e.g., through tax foreclosure or eminent domain).
- Many states operate parallel **Voluntary Cleanup Programs (VCPs)** that provide state-law liability protections (often through "no further action" letters or covenants not to sue) to complement federal BFPP protection, encouraging redevelopment of brownfield sites.

#### **Example**

A municipal redevelopment authority wishes to acquire a former dry-cleaning facility site known to have soil and groundwater solvent contamination, intending to convert it into mixed-use housing.

1. The authority commissions a **Phase I ESA** satisfying AAI requirements, which confirms the pre-existing contamination and its origin predates the planned acquisition.
2. Because the authority has **actual knowledge** of the contamination, the **innocent landowner defense is unavailable** (it requires no knowledge).
3. The authority instead structures the acquisition to qualify as a **BFPP**, ensuring it: provides required notices to EPA/state agencies of any newly discovered releases, exercises appropriate care (e.g., securing the site, addressing any immediate exposure pathways), cooperates fully with any ongoing or future response actions, and has no disqualifying affiliation with the dry-cleaning operator.
4. The authority may also enroll the property in the state's **Voluntary Cleanup Program**, potentially layering state liability protection atop the federal BFPP defense and streamlining any remediation needed before redevelopment.
5. The authority remains aware that a **windfall lien** could attach if EPA later incurs response costs that measurably increase the property's value, up to that value increase.

### Comparative Table: The Three Landowner Defenses

| Defense | Statutory Basis | Timing of Disposal | Knowledge Requirement | Key Distinguishing Feature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Innocent landowner | §101(35) / §107(b)(3) | Before acquisition | No knowledge, no reason to know | Third-party defense variant; knowledge defeats defense |
| Bona fide prospective purchaser | §101(40) | Before acquisition | Knowledge permitted (even required to be disclosed) | Designed for knowing purchase of contaminated brownfields |
| Contiguous property owner | §107(q) | N/A — contamination from off-site source | No knowledge of on-site contamination source (since none exists on-site) | Contamination migrates from adjacent property only |
