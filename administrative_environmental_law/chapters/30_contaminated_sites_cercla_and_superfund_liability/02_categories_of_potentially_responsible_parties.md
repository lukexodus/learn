## Categories of Potentially Responsible Parties

### Overview

CERCLA §107(a) (42 U.S.C. §9607(a)) identifies four discrete classes of potentially responsible parties (PRPs) who may be held liable for response costs, natural resource damages, and health assessment costs at a contaminated facility. These categories are the operative liability triggers examined once the threshold elements (facility, release/threatened release, hazardous substance, response costs consistent with the NCP) are established. Each category has developed its own body of interpretive case law defining its scope, and each interacts differently with the strict, joint-and-several, and retroactive liability framework.

### Statutory Basis

- **CERCLA §107(a)(1)–(4)** — 42 U.S.C. §9607(a)(1)–(4): the four PRP categories
- **CERCLA §101(20)** — 42 U.S.C. §9601(20): definition of "owner or operator"
- **CERCLA §101(21)** — 42 U.S.C. §9601(21): definition of "person"
- **CERCLA §101(35)** — 42 U.S.C. §9601(35): innocent landowner defense provisions
- **CERCLA §101(40)** — 42 U.S.C. §9601(40): bona fide prospective purchaser definition (added by the Small Business Liability Relief and Brownfields Revitalization Act of 2002)

### Category 1: Current Owners and Operators (§107(a)(1))

#### **Key Points**

- Any person who **currently** owns or operates the facility at the time cleanup action commences, **regardless of whether that person had any role in causing the contamination**.
- This is the broadest and most strictly applied category — current ownership alone is sufficient.
- "Owner" is generally understood in its ordinary sense (holding title or a possessory interest); "operator" requires actual management or control of facility operations.

#### Operator Liability Standard: *United States v. Bestfoods*

*United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51 (1998), is the controlling Supreme Court decision on operator liability, particularly in the parent-subsidiary context:

- A parent corporation is not automatically liable as an "operator" of its subsidiary's facility merely by virtue of the ownership relationship or normal incidents of ownership (e.g., monitoring subsidiary performance, articulating general policies).
- A parent corporation **can** be directly liable as an operator if it **exercises control over the facility itself** — meaning the parent's own personnel or agents manage, direct, or conduct operations specifically related to pollution, as distinguished from merely directing the affairs of the subsidiary generally.
- The Court also left open (and remanded) the question of **derivative liability** through veil-piercing under traditional corporate law principles, distinguishing this from direct operator liability.

#### **[Inference]** Practical Application

*Bestfoods* is frequently cited in due diligence practice: corporate parents structuring subsidiary relationships to avoid CERCLA operator exposure typically avoid direct involvement in subsidiary environmental/operational decisions and maintain clear corporate formalities, since operator liability turns on actual control exercised, not corporate structure alone.

#### Defenses Specific to Current Owners

- **Innocent landowner defense** (§101(35)) — available where the current owner acquired the property after the disposal occurred, conducted "all appropriate inquiries" (AAI) into prior ownership and uses consistent with good commercial/customary practice at the time of acquisition, and did not know or have reason to know of contamination.
- **Bona fide prospective purchaser (BFPP) defense** (§101(40)) — added by the 2002 Brownfields Amendments; protects a purchaser who acquires contaminated property **after** conducting AAI, even with actual knowledge of contamination, provided the purchaser exercises appropriate care, cooperates with response actions, and complies with land-use restrictions and information requests.
- **Contiguous property owner defense** (§107(q)) — protects an owner of property contaminated solely by a release from an adjacent or nearby property, where the owner did not cause or contribute to the release.

### Category 2: Owners and Operators at the Time of Disposal (§107(a)(2))

#### **Key Points**

- Covers **past** owners or operators who held that status **at the time hazardous substances were disposed of** at the facility.
- Unlike current owners, past owners/operators are liable only if disposal occurred during their period of ownership/operation — mere prior ownership without a nexus to disposal timing is insufficient.

#### Interpretation of "Disposal"

- Courts have split on whether passive migration of contamination (e.g., existing contamination spreading through soil or groundwater without new human activity) constitutes "disposal" under §107(a)(2).
- The majority view, followed in circuits including the Second and Ninth, generally holds that passive soil migration alone does **not** constitute a new "disposal" that would extend liability to an intervening owner during whose tenure only passive spreading (not an affirmative disposal act) occurred; other circuits, including some readings from the Fourth Circuit, have taken a broader view treating certain passive migration as disposal. This remains a genuinely contested and circuit-dependent question. **[Unverified]** — the precise circuit split composition should be confirmed against current case law given ongoing litigation refining this doctrine.

#### **Example**

Company X owned an industrial facility from 1970–1985 and disposed of solvent waste on-site during that period. Company Y purchased the property in 1985 and operated a warehouse with no further waste-generating activity, simply holding the property while historical contamination migrated through the groundwater. Under the majority passive-migration view, Company X is liable under §107(a)(2) as the owner/operator at the time of disposal; Company Y's liability would depend on whether it is deemed a **current** owner under §107(a)(1) (which does not require a disposal-timing nexus) rather than on any theory that groundwater migration during its tenure was itself a "disposal."

### Category 3: Arrangers (§107(a)(3))

#### **Key Points**

- Covers any person who, **by contract, agreement, or otherwise, arranged for disposal or treatment** of hazardous substances owned or possessed by that person, at a facility owned or operated by another party.
- This category reaches generators of waste who never physically handled disposal themselves but contracted with waste haulers, treatment facilities, or disposal sites.

#### Intent Requirement: *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*

*Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599 (2009), narrowed and clarified arranger liability:

- Liability requires that the party **took intentional steps to dispose of a hazardous substance**.
- Selling a **useful, unused product** (in that case, an agricultural chemical) is not "arranging for disposal" merely because the seller knows that some portion will be spilled, leaked, or lost during the buyer's use or transfer — knowledge of an incidental byproduct of a legitimate transaction, without intent to dispose, is insufficient.
- Relevant factors the Court considered included: whether the transaction involved a **useful product** versus **waste**, the **state of the product** at the time of transfer (i.e., whether it retained value), and whether the arrangement reflected **an intent to dispose of a hazardous substance** as opposed to a sale for a legitimate business purpose.

#### **[Inference]** Fault-Like Element in an Otherwise Strict-Liability Statute

*Burlington Northern*'s intent requirement introduces a quasi-fault element into arranger liability that is not present in the other three PRP categories, which remain purely strict liability. This makes arranger-liability analysis more fact-intensive than the current-owner or transporter categories, often turning on characterization of the underlying transaction (sale of product vs. disposal arrangement).

#### Common Arranger Fact Patterns

- A manufacturer contracts with a waste hauler to remove drums of spent solvent for "disposal" at a landfill — classic arranger liability.
- A company sells used, off-spec, or surplus chemicals to a third party who resells or disposes of them — analyzed under *Burlington Northern*'s useful-product test.
- Recycling arrangements — courts examine whether the material had ongoing commercial value and utility (weighing against arranger liability) versus being effectively discarded waste channeled through a nominal "recycling" label.

### Category 4: Transporters (§107(a)(4))

#### **Key Points**

- Covers any person who **accepted hazardous substances for transport** to disposal or treatment facilities, **incineration vessels, or sites selected by the transporter**.
- The key limiting element: the transporter must have had a role in **selecting** the disposal site. A transporter who merely delivers waste to a site chosen entirely by the generator/arranger, with no independent selection role, does not trigger this category's liability rationale in the same way — the statute's structure is designed to hold transporters liable when they exercise discretion over the choice of disposal location.
- Common carriers who transport hazardous substances without any role in destination selection are generally treated as falling outside this liability trigger's core rationale, since they lack the site-selection nexus the provision targets.

#### **Example**

A licensed waste hauler contracts with a generator, and the generator's own environmental staff identifies and directs the waste to a specific licensed TSD facility. The hauler simply transports the waste to the specified location with no independent input into site selection. Because the hauler played no role in selecting the disposal site, transporter liability under §107(a)(4) is unlikely to attach on these facts; liability would instead rest with the generator (potentially as an arranger under §107(a)(3)) and the facility's owner/operator under §107(a)(1)/(2).
