## The Endangerment Finding Repeal, WOTUS Rulemaking, and ESA Rollbacks as a Coordinated Deregulatory Pattern


### Overview

Three ostensibly separate 2025–2026 regulatory actions — the rescission of the Clean Air Act Endangerment Finding, the narrowing of the Clean Water Act's "waters of the United States" (WOTUS) definition, and rollbacks to the Endangered Species Act's (ESA) definition of "harm" and related implementing regulations — share a common structural logic despite arising under different statutes and agencies. Each targets a **foundational interpretive or definitional predicate** (rather than a downstream implementing rule) that determines the scope of an entire regulatory program, and each is justified using a common post-*Loper Bright* interpretive methodology emphasizing statutory text, original meaning, and reduced deference to prior agency scientific or definitional judgments. Understanding this pattern illuminates a general strategy available to future administrations across environmental statutes: identify and revise the single definitional term or finding on which the broadest possible downstream regulatory apparatus depends.

### The Common Structural Logic: Predicate-Level Deregulation

**Key Points**

Each action targets a different type of foundational predicate:

1. **Endangerment Finding (Clean Air Act)**: A scientific/factual finding (GHGs endanger public health and welfare) that serves as the statutory trigger for the CAA's GHG regulatory authority.
2. **WOTUS definition (Clean Water Act)**: A jurisdictional/definitional term ("waters of the United States") that determines which water bodies fall within federal permitting authority under CWA §§402 and 404.
3. **"Harm" definition (Endangered Species Act)**: A definitional term within the statutory prohibition on "take" of a listed species, determining whether habitat modification alone constitutes a violation.

In each case, narrowing or eliminating the predicate **shrinks federal regulatory jurisdiction without repealing the underlying statute itself** — a structurally efficient deregulatory technique, since a single definitional or factual reinterpretation can eliminate permitting or compliance obligations across an entire class of activities and actors, rather than requiring repeal of numerous individual downstream rules one at a time.

### WOTUS: Post-*Sackett* Narrowing

**Key Points**

- **Doctrinal predicate**: *Sackett v. EPA*, 598 U.S. 651 (2023), narrowed CWA jurisdiction to require that a wetland have a "continuous surface connection" to a "relatively permanent" water body, displacing the prior "significant nexus" test derived from Justice Kennedy's concurrence in *Rapanos v. United States* (2006).
- **November 17, 2025 proposed rule**: EPA and the Army Corps proposed a rule to revise the WOTUS definition, published in the Federal Register on November 20, 2025, with a public comment period closing January 5, 2026. Key proposed changes:
  - Defining "relatively permanent" as bodies of water standing or continuously flowing "year-round or at least during the wet season."
  - Defining "continuous surface connection" as having surface water at least during the wet season and abutting (touching) a jurisdictional water.
  - Clarifying that ephemeral waters — those with surface water flowing or standing only in direct response to precipitation — are not jurisdictional because they are not relatively permanent.
  - Removing automatic jurisdiction for "interstate waters" as a standalone category, and expanding exclusions for prior converted cropland, ditches, wastewater treatment systems, and groundwater.
- **September 9, 2026 supplemental notice**: EPA and the Corps issued a supplemental notice of proposed rulemaking (SNPRM) offering additional methods for defining WOTUS, with comments due October 9, 2026 — indicating the rulemaking remains in active development rather than finalized as of the most recent developments.
- **Stated rationale**: The agencies frame the rule as fully implementing the Supreme Court's *Sackett* decision, cutting red tape and providing clarity for industry, energy producers, farmers, and developers, while striking a balance recognizing that states and tribes are best positioned to manage local land and water resources.
- **Practical effect**: A narrower WOTUS definition leaves many wetlands and streams subject to state (rather than federal) jurisdiction, which could constrain pollution prevention efforts by EPA and result in state-by-state variability in practical CWA coverage pending litigation.

### ESA: The "Harm" Definition Rollback

**Key Points**

- **Historical baseline**: Since 1975 (formally codified in 1981), the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service defined "harm" under the ESA's "take" prohibition to include significant habitat modification or degradation that actually kills or injures wildlife — a definition upheld by the Supreme Court in *Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon*, 515 U.S. 687 (1995).
- **April 2025 proposal**: The Fish and Wildlife Service and NOAA proposed rescinding the "harm" definition, published as *Rescinding the Definition of "Harm" Under the Endangered Species Act*, 90 Fed. Reg. 16104 (proposed Apr. 17, 2025), asserting that revising the definition would reduce regulatory burdens and improve administrative efficiency.
- **July 2026 finalization**: The rule was finalized, fully deleting the 1981 "harm" definition without replacing it, on the agency's stated rationale that the definition was an unlawful regulatory intrusion that interfered with private property rights. Going forward, "harm" refers only to an act that actually kills or injures wildlife directly — habitat destruction alone, absent intentional direct injury, is no longer independently prohibited.
- **Related, closely timed action**: One week after the "harm" redefinition, the Fish and Wildlife Service and NOAA finalized a separate rule eliminating automatic ESA protections that previously attached upon a species being listed as "threatened" (as opposed to "endangered"), a change affecting species such as the Florida manatee and pygmy rabbit.
- **Additional coordinated actions in the same period**: An April 2025 executive order declared a national energy emergency and directed agencies to use ESA emergency consultation provisions to the maximum extent permissible to facilitate energy development; the Department of Agriculture separately designated approximately 113 million acres (nearly 60%) of national forests an "emergency" logging zone and rescinded the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule, using emergency designations to bypass standard environmental review under the ESA and NEPA. A March 2026 announcement additionally convened the rarely-used Endangered Species Committee (the "God Squad") to consider an exemption for oil and gas development in the Gulf of Mexico — only the fourth such exemption proceeding since Congress created the committee in 1978.
- **Stated rationale**: Interior Secretary Doug Burgum characterized the prior interpretation as having "turned routine activity into a regulatory trap" and expanded federal authority beyond what Congress intended; the agencies framed the change as returning ESA interpretation "back to its actual text and original intent."
- **Litigation posture**: A coalition of nine environmental groups and two Native American tribes filed suit against the threatened-species protections rollback, arguing the changes violate the ESA's statutory language and undermine its fundamental goals; the Center for Biological Diversity has separately signaled it will challenge the "harm" redefinition, citing *Babbitt v. Sweet Home* as the controlling precedent the new rule must contend with — though commentary notes that if such challenges reach the Supreme Court again, environmental groups face a considerably more conservative Court than the one that decided *Sweet Home* in 1995.

### Shared Interpretive Methodology: Post-*Loper Bright* Textualism

**Key Points**

- Regulatory tracker documentation of the November 2025 ESA rollback proposals explicitly frames the new interpretations as reflecting the "single, best meaning" of the statute under *Loper Bright* — directly invoking the post-*Chevron* interpretive standard as the doctrinal basis for the definitional narrowing.
- This is the same interpretive posture underlying the Endangerment Finding rescission (arguing CAA §202(a) does not permit GHG regulation based on global climate concerns) and the WOTUS revision (implementing *Sackett*'s textual "relatively permanent"/"continuous surface connection" test in place of the broader "significant nexus" standard).
- **Structural implication**: Because *Loper Bright* eliminates judicial deference to an agency's own reasonable interpretation of ambiguous statutory terms, an administration seeking to narrow regulatory scope can argue its new, narrower reading is simply the statute's "best" reading — a legal theory that requires no deference-based justification, unlike the *Chevron*-era approach where an agency might have argued a broader reading was merely "reasonable" and entitled to deference.
- Symmetrically, per commentary noted in the broader deregulatory-wave literature, this same doctrinal tool remains available to future administrations or courts to reject the current narrowing as not, in fact, the "best" reading — the interpretive methodology does not guarantee outcome durability across administrations.

### Broader Implications for Administrative Law Strategy

**Key Points**

1. **Efficiency of predicate-level action**: A single definitional change can achieve deregulatory scope comparable to repealing dozens of downstream implementing rules, making this an efficient tool for an administration operating under time and political-capital constraints — illustrated by the ESA rollback's characterization by the agency's own critics as opening the door to "widespread destruction of habitat" through a change to a single word's regulatory meaning.
2. **Reduced procedural friction relative to formal statutory amendment**: Because these are agency rulemakings (or a rescinded factual finding) rather than legislative amendments, they proceed through notice-and-comment rulemaking rather than requiring congressional action — though this also means they remain fully reversible by a future administration through the same rulemaking process, subject to *State Farm* reasoned-explanation requirements for any reversal.
3. ***Corner Post* interaction**: Because the *Corner Post* statute-of-limitations rule (running from a plaintiff's injury rather than a rule's promulgation date) applies to all three rulemakings once finalized, newly regulated or newly deregulated parties retain extended windows to challenge whichever version of each rule is in effect at any given time — compounding the durability uncertainty already present in predicate-level deregulation.
4. **Common defense structure regulated parties should anticipate**: Industry commentary around the WOTUS proposal explicitly frames the narrower definition as reducing costs and clarifying permitting obligations for energy producers, farmers, ranchers, developers, and landowners — the same beneficiary framing (reduced compliance burden, clarified property rights) recurs across the ESA "harm" rollback and, less directly, the Endangerment Finding repeal's framing around energy costs, suggesting a consistent political and economic narrative underlies the pattern regardless of the specific statute involved.
5. **Species-specific and habitat-specific downstream effects distinguish the ESA action**: Unlike the Endangerment Finding and WOTUS actions, which primarily affect industrial and development permitting processes, the ESA "harm" rollback has a more direct and immediate biological consequence — critics specifically note that habitat loss is the leading cause of extinction, meaning this particular predicate-level change may have less procedurally mediated and more directly observable ecological effects than the other two actions in the pattern.
