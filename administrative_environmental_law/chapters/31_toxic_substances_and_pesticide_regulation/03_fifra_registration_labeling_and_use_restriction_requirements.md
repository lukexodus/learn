## FIFRA Registration, Labeling, and Use Restriction Requirements


### Overview

The Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA) is the principal federal statute governing the sale, distribution, and use of pesticides in the United States. Unlike TSCA's general chemical framework, FIFRA operates through a **product-registration model**: no pesticide may be sold or distributed in the United States unless it is registered with EPA, and the registered product's approved label carries the force of law — using a pesticide inconsistent with its labeling is itself a federal violation. FIFRA's structure reflects a risk-benefit balancing standard distinct from TSCA's post-2016 risk-only approach, and its registration, classification, and enforcement mechanisms form an integrated compliance system that pesticide manufacturers, applicators, and users must all navigate.

### Statutory Basis

- **FIFRA §3** — 7 U.S.C. §136a: pesticide registration requirements
- **FIFRA §2(bb)** — 7 U.S.C. §136(bb): "unreasonable adverse effects on the environment" definition
- **FIFRA §12** — 7 U.S.C. §136j: prohibited acts, including use inconsistent with labeling
- **FIFRA §24** — 7 U.S.C. §136v: state authority to regulate pesticide sale and use
- **FIFRA §3(c)(5)** — 7 U.S.C. §136a(c)(5): registration standard and classification for restricted use

### Registration Requirements

#### **Key Points**

- **§3(a)** establishes the core prohibition: no person may sell or distribute any pesticide in the United States unless it is registered under FIFRA (subject to limited exceptions, such as certain intrastate registrations and experimental use permits).
- To register a pesticide, an applicant must submit data demonstrating that the pesticide, when used in accordance with widespread and commonly recognized practice, will:
  1. Perform its intended function **without unreasonable adverse effects on the environment**, and
  2. When used in accordance with the label's directions, **will not generally cause unreasonable adverse effects on the environment**.
- **"Unreasonable adverse effects on the environment"** (§2(bb)) is defined as **any unreasonable risk to man or the environment, taking into account the economic, social, and environmental costs and benefits** of the pesticide's use — this is an explicit **risk-benefit balancing standard**, in contrast to TSCA's post-Lautenberg risk-only threshold determination.
- Registration requires extensive supporting data across categories including: product chemistry, toxicology (acute, chronic, carcinogenicity, reproductive/developmental), environmental fate (persistence, mobility, degradation), ecological effects (impacts on non-target species, including pollinators, birds, and aquatic organisms), and residue chemistry (for food-use pesticides, informing tolerance-setting under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act).

#### Registration Categories

1. **New active ingredient registration** — the most data-intensive pathway, required when a pesticide's active ingredient has not previously been registered.
2. **Registration of a new product containing an already-registered active ingredient** — generally requires less extensive new data, since the active ingredient's core toxicology and environmental fate profile is already established.
3. **Section 24(c) "Special Local Need" (SLN) registrations** — allow states to register additional uses of a federally registered pesticide to address a specific local pest problem not covered by the federal label, subject to EPA oversight and disapproval authority.
4. **Experimental Use Permits (EUPs)** — allow limited field testing of unregistered pesticides or new uses, subject to restrictions on treated acreage and food/feed use.
5. **Emergency exemptions (§18)** — allow time-limited use of an unregistered pesticide or unregistered use in response to an urgent, non-routine pest situation.

#### Registration Review

- FIFRA requires EPA to **periodically review each registered pesticide** (generally every 15 years) to ensure it continues to meet the registration standard in light of updated science, changed use patterns, and evolving risk assessment methodologies.
- Registration review can result in label amendments, new use restrictions, cancellation of certain uses, or, in some cases, full cancellation of the registration.

### Classification: General Use vs. Restricted Use

#### **Key Points**

Under §3(d), EPA classifies each registered pesticide (or specific use) into one of two categories:

1. **General Use Pesticides (GUP)** — may be applied by the general public without additional certification, based on a determination that the pesticide, when used according to label directions, will not generally cause unreasonable adverse effects on the environment.
2. **Restricted Use Pesticides (RUP)** — may be applied **only by, or under the direct supervision of, a certified applicator**, based on a determination that, absent such restriction, the pesticide may generally cause unreasonable adverse effects on the environment, including injury to the applicator.

#### **[Inference]** Rationale for Restricted Use Classification

RUP classification is typically applied to pesticides presenting elevated acute toxicity, significant applicator exposure risk, or substantial risk to non-target organisms (e.g., certain organophosphates, some rodenticides, and pesticides with notable pollinator toxicity concerns), reflecting a judgment that ordinary consumer-level precautions and labeling are insufficient to manage the risk without requiring trained, certified handling.

#### Applicator Certification

- States (or EPA, in states without an approved certification program) administer **certified applicator** programs, distinguishing between **private applicators** (generally producers applying RUPs on their own or their employer's land) and **commercial applicators** (applying pesticides for hire or in non-agricultural commercial settings).
- Certification typically requires passing a competency examination covering categories such as label comprehension, pest identification, application equipment calibration, and safety procedures.

### Labeling Requirements

#### **Key Points**

- FIFRA labeling is not merely informational — it is a **legally binding regulatory instrument**. The registered label constitutes the enforceable terms under which the pesticide may be lawfully sold, distributed, and used.
- Required label elements generally include:
  - Product name, registration number, and manufacturer/registrant identification
  - **Ingredient statement** disclosing active and inert ingredient percentages
  - **Signal word** reflecting acute toxicity category (**DANGER**, **WARNING**, or **CAUTION**, from most to least severe)
  - **Precautionary statements** addressing hazards to humans, domestic animals, and the environment
  - **Directions for use**, including approved application sites, target pests, application rates, timing, and any restrictions (e.g., re-entry intervals, buffer zones, application method limitations)
  - **First aid statements** and, where applicable, **environmental hazard statements** (e.g., toxicity to bees, aquatic organisms)
  - For RUPs, the statement **"Restricted Use Pesticide"** prominently displayed

#### "Use Inconsistent With Labeling" — §12(a)(2)(G)

- FIFRA §12(a)(2)(G) makes it **unlawful** to use any registered pesticide **in a manner inconsistent with its labeling** — this is one of the most frequently enforced FIFRA violations and applies to end users, not merely manufacturers or distributors.
- "Inconsistent use" includes: applying at a higher rate or more frequently than labeled, applying to a crop or site not listed on the label, ignoring required protective equipment or re-entry interval directions, or applying via a method not specified.
- Limited exceptions exist (7 U.S.C. §136j(a)(2)(G) and related EPA interpretive guidance) — for example, applying a pesticide against a target pest not specified on the label is generally permitted if the application is otherwise consistent with the label's directions and the pest is of a type the labeled use is intended to control, but the framework is narrow and fact-specific.

#### Worker Protection Standard Interaction

- For agricultural pesticide use, EPA's **Worker Protection Standard (WPS)**, promulgated under FIFRA, imposes additional requirements — restricted-entry intervals, personal protective equipment, decontamination supplies, and hazard communication for agricultural workers and pesticide handlers — that operate alongside and often overlap with label-specific directions.

### **Example**

A manufacturer seeks to register a new insecticide active ingredient for use on row crops.

1. The manufacturer conducts and submits the full battery of **registration data**: acute and chronic toxicology studies, environmental fate data showing moderate soil persistence, ecological effects data revealing notable toxicity to honeybees at label application rates, and residue trials supporting a proposed food tolerance.
2. Based on the bee toxicity and applicator exposure data, EPA determines the product should be classified as a **Restricted Use Pesticide**, requiring application only by certified applicators and mandating a **pollinator protection statement** and application-timing restrictions (e.g., prohibiting application during bloom when bees are foraging).
3. EPA approves the **label**, which specifies approved crops, maximum application rate, minimum re-entry interval, required personal protective equipment, and the RUP designation statement.
4. Several years later, during **registration review**, new incident data reveals unexpected runoff into adjacent waterways causing fish kills. EPA amends the label to add a **buffer zone requirement** near water bodies and may require additional environmental fate data as a condition of continued registration.
5. A commercial applicator who applies the product at twice the labeled rate to address a severe pest outbreak, believing this constitutes reasonable pest control judgment, is nonetheless in violation of **§12(a)(2)(G)** — use inconsistent with labeling — regardless of the practical pest-management rationale, because the label's rate directions are legally binding.

### State Authority Under §24

#### **Key Points**

- **§24(a)** preserves state authority to **regulate the sale or use** of federally registered pesticides, provided such regulation does not permit any sale or use prohibited under FIFRA — meaning states may impose **additional restrictions** more stringent than federal requirements, but may not authorize a use that FIFRA or the federal label prohibits.
- **§24(b)** generally preempts states from imposing labeling or packaging requirements **different from** those required under FIFRA, reflecting Congress's intent for a uniform national labeling standard even while preserving state authority over sale and use.
- **§24(c)** SLN registrations allow states to add uses for local pest problems, subject to EPA's ability to disapprove an SLN registration found inconsistent with FIFRA's registration standard.

### Comparative Table: General Use vs. Restricted Use Pesticides

| Feature | General Use Pesticide (GUP) | Restricted Use Pesticide (RUP) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who may apply | General public | Certified applicator, or under direct supervision |
| Basis for classification | No general unreasonable adverse effects expected under label use | May cause unreasonable adverse effects absent restriction (including applicator injury) |
| Label designation | No special designation required | "Restricted Use Pesticide" statement required |
| Typical examples | Common household/lawn pesticides | Certain organophosphates, some rodenticides, high-toxicity agricultural chemicals |
