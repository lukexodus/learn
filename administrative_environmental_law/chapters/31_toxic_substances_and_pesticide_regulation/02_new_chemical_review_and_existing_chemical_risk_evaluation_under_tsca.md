## New Chemical Review and Existing Chemical Risk Evaluation Under TSCA

### Overview

Post-Lautenberg TSCA operates through two parallel but structurally distinct regulatory tracks: **new chemical review** under §5, which governs chemicals not yet on the TSCA Inventory before they may enter commerce, and **existing chemical risk evaluation** under §6, which governs the systematic reassessment of chemicals already in commerce. These tracks share the same underlying risk-only safety standard but differ substantially in procedural triggers, timelines, and the burden EPA bears at each stage. Mastering the distinction between these two tracks — and the specific determinations and deadlines each requires — is essential to understanding how TSCA functions operationally after the 2016 reforms.

### Statutory Basis

- **TSCA §5** — 15 U.S.C. §2604: new chemical / significant new use review
- **TSCA §6(b)** — 15 U.S.C. §2605(b): prioritization and risk evaluation of existing chemicals
- **TSCA §6(a)** — 15 U.S.C. §2605(a): risk management following an unreasonable risk determination
- **TSCA §26** — 15 U.S.C. §2625: scientific standards applicable to both tracks

### New Chemical Review (§5)

#### **Key Points**

- Any person intending to manufacture (including import) a chemical substance **not listed** on the TSCA Chemical Substance Inventory must submit a **Pre-Manufacture Notice (PMN)** to EPA at least **90 days** before commencing manufacture.
- EPA must, within the 90-day review period (extendable in certain circumstances), reach one of **three affirmative determinations** — the statute no longer permits default entry through EPA inaction.
- The review also covers **Significant New Use Notices (SNUNs)**, which apply when an already-listed chemical is proposed for a "significant new use" that EPA has designated via a **Significant New Use Rule (SNUR)** as requiring notice before that use may commence.

#### The Three PMN Determinations

1. **"Not likely to present an unreasonable risk"** — EPA makes this finding and the chemical may proceed to manufacture without restriction. This is the most common outcome for straightforward, well-characterized chemicals with adequate supporting data.
2. **"Presents an unreasonable risk"** — EPA must issue an order under §5(f) restricting the chemical's manufacture, processing, distribution, use, or disposal to the extent necessary to eliminate the unreasonable risk **before** manufacture may proceed. This may take the form of use restrictions, personal protective equipment requirements, or other conditions.
3. **Insufficient information** to permit a reasoned "not likely" evaluation, combined with either:
   - The chemical **may present an unreasonable risk** without regulation, or
   - The chemical is or may be produced in **substantial quantities**, and either enters the environment in substantial quantities or there is significant or substantial human exposure.In this scenario, EPA must issue an order under §5(e) — commonly requiring **additional testing** to fill the data gap and/or imposing interim restrictions pending that testing, before manufacture may proceed.

#### **[Inference]** Practical Frequency of Outcomes

In practice, a substantial share of PMN submissions historically have resulted in some form of EPA order (either §5(e) testing/interim-restriction orders or §5(f) unreasonable-risk orders) rather than an unconditional "not likely" finding, reflecting the more rigorous affirmative-determination requirement Congress imposed in 2016. The exact proportion fluctuates by year and chemical category and should be verified against current EPA PMN statistics for any precise figures.

#### Significant New Use Rules (SNURs)

- EPA can identify a **"significant new use"** of an existing chemical — meaning a use different from those EPA reviewed and found acceptable when the chemical was originally reviewed or listed — and require manufacturers/processors to submit a SNUN before commencing that use.
- SNURs are frequently used as a **risk-management tool** following a new-chemical review: EPA might approve a chemical for a specific, narrow use while requiring a SNUR for any other use, ensuring EPA retains oversight if the chemical's application expands beyond the originally reviewed scope.
- SNUNs are reviewed under the same three-determination framework as PMNs.

### Existing Chemical Risk Evaluation (§6(b))

#### **Key Points**

Existing chemicals — those already on the TSCA Inventory — are addressed through a structured, multi-stage process rather than a transaction-triggered review like PMNs:

1. **Prioritization**
2. **Risk Evaluation**
3. **Risk Management** (if an unreasonable risk is found)

#### Stage 1: Prioritization

- EPA must designate chemicals as either **high-priority substances** (warranting a full risk evaluation because they may present an unreasonable risk) or **low-priority substances** (not warranting a risk evaluation at that time, based on current information).
- The prioritization process considers factors including: hazard and exposure potential, persistence and bioaccumulation, storage near significant sources of drinking water, and the chemical's conditions of use.
- Prioritization decisions are subject to public comment, and a low-priority designation can be revisited if new information emerges.
- EPA is required to maintain an **ongoing minimum number of chemicals** undergoing risk evaluation at any given time, reflecting Congress's intent to institutionalize continuous existing-chemical review rather than relying on ad hoc EPA initiative.

#### Stage 2: Risk Evaluation

- Once designated high-priority, EPA conducts a **risk evaluation** to determine whether the chemical, under its conditions of use, presents an unreasonable risk of injury to health or the environment — **without consideration of costs or other non-risk factors** at this stage, consistent with the Lautenberg Act's bifurcated risk/risk-management structure.
- The risk evaluation must specifically consider risk to **potentially exposed or susceptible subpopulations** identified as relevant to the specific chemical (e.g., pregnant women, children, industrial workers, fenceline communities near facilities using the chemical).
- Risk evaluations must be completed within **three years** of initiation, with a possible **six-month extension**.
- **Conditions of use** — meaning the circumstances under which the chemical is intended, known, or reasonably foreseen to be manufactured, processed, distributed, used, or disposed of — define the scope of what the evaluation must address; EPA generally cannot arbitrarily exclude a known condition of use from the scope of its evaluation.
- **Manufacturer-requested risk evaluations** allow a chemical manufacturer to request that EPA conduct a risk evaluation on a specific chemical (subject to fee payment and EPA capacity constraints), often pursued where a manufacturer prefers a definitive federal evaluation over a patchwork of state-level restrictions.

#### Stage 3: Risk Management

- If the risk evaluation concludes the chemical presents an **unreasonable risk** under one or more conditions of use, EPA must propose and finalize a **risk management rule** under §6(a) within statutorily defined timeframes (generally proposed within one year and finalized within two years of the risk evaluation's completion, subject to possible extension).
- At this risk-management stage — and only at this stage — EPA **may consider costs and other reasonably ascertainable economic consequences** in selecting among regulatory options that adequately address the identified unreasonable risk.
- Available risk management measures include: prohibiting or limiting manufacture/processing/distribution for specific uses, requiring warning labels, restricting concentration levels, requiring recordkeeping, or, in appropriate cases, a full ban.
- If the evaluation concludes the chemical **does not** present an unreasonable risk under its evaluated conditions of use, EPA publishes that finding and no risk management rule follows for those conditions of use (though the chemical could later be re-prioritized if new information emerges).

### **Example**

**New chemical scenario**: A specialty chemical manufacturer develops a novel surfactant not currently listed on the TSCA Inventory, intended for use in industrial cleaning products.

1. The manufacturer submits a **PMN** at least 90 days before intended manufacture, including available toxicological and exposure data.
2. EPA's technical review identifies a data gap regarding the surfactant's aquatic toxicity and estimates that use patterns could result in substantial environmental release through wastewater discharge.
3. Because information is insufficient to support a "not likely" finding, and the chemical may be produced in substantial quantities with substantial environmental release potential, EPA issues a **§5(e) order** requiring the manufacturer to conduct aquatic toxicity testing and imposing interim use restrictions (e.g., limiting concentration in discharge) pending completion of that testing.
4. Once EPA determines the additional data does not indicate unreasonable risk under the restricted conditions, EPA may allow expanded use, potentially coupled with a **SNUR** requiring notice if the manufacturer later seeks to expand into new use categories beyond those reviewed.

**Existing chemical scenario**: A widely used industrial degreaser has been on the TSCA Inventory since before 1976 and has recently drawn concern due to emerging epidemiological data on occupational exposure.

1. EPA's **prioritization** process designates the degreaser as **high-priority**, citing worker exposure concerns and persistence data.
2. EPA initiates a **risk evaluation**, examining all known conditions of use (industrial degreasing, commercial applications, consumer products containing the chemical) and specifically assessing risk to **occupational subpopulations** with elevated exposure.
3. Within the three-year statutory window (potentially extended six months), EPA completes the evaluation and determines the chemical **presents an unreasonable risk** to workers under certain uses, but not to the general consumer population under typical consumer product use.
4. EPA proceeds to **risk management**, proposing a rule that imposes workplace exposure limits and personal protective equipment requirements for occupational uses (where cost and technical feasibility may now be considered), while leaving unrestricted the consumer-product conditions of use found not to present unreasonable risk.

### Comparative Table: New Chemical Review vs. Existing Chemical Risk Evaluation

| Feature | New Chemical Review (§5) | Existing Chemical Risk Evaluation (§6(b)) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger | Manufacturer submits PMN/SNUN before manufacture | EPA prioritization designates chemical as high-priority |
| Timing relative to commerce | Pre-market (chemical not yet in commerce or in a new use) | Post-market (chemical already in commerce) |
| EPA determination options | Three: not likely / unreasonable risk / insufficient information | Two: unreasonable risk found / not found (per condition of use) |
| Cost consideration | Not permitted at the determination stage | Not permitted at risk evaluation stage; permitted at risk management stage |
| Statutory deadline | 90-day initial review period | 3 years for risk evaluation (+ 6-month extension); 1–2 years for risk management rule |
| Default outcome absent EPA action | None — affirmative determination required | N/A — process is EPA-initiated, not transaction-triggered |
| Subpopulation consideration | Considered as part of unreasonable risk analysis | Explicitly required in risk evaluation scope |
