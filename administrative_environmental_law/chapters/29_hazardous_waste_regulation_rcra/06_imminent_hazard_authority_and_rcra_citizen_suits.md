## Imminent Hazard Authority and RCRA Citizen Suits

### Overview

RCRA (Resource Conservation and Recovery Act) contains two distinct but overlapping enforcement mechanisms that allow action against hazardous waste threats outside the ordinary permitting and inspection process: **imminent hazard authority** under §7003 and **citizen suits** under §7002. Both provisions exist to fill gaps left by RCRA's primary regulatory scheme (Subtitle C), which depends on EPA and authorized states identifying violations and pursuing enforcement. §7003 gives EPA (and, since 1984, private citizens) a tool to address hazards regardless of whether they arise from technical regulatory violations. §7002 gives citizens standing to sue both violators and EPA itself, functioning as a private attorney general mechanism.

### Statutory Basis

- **§7003 (42 U.S.C. §6973)** — "Imminent Hazard" authority
- **§7002 (42 U.S.C. §6972)** — "Citizen Suits"

Both sit in RCRA Subtitle G (Miscellaneous Provisions) and apply across the statute's scope — not limited to Subtitle C hazardous waste, but also reaching solid waste under Subtitle D and underground storage tanks under Subtitle I.

### Imminent Hazard Authority (§7003)

#### **Key Points**

- Grants EPA authority to sue any person who has contributed or is contributing to handling, storage, treatment, transportation, or disposal of solid or hazardous waste that **"may present an imminent and substantial endangerment to health or the environment."**
- Applies **regardless of compliance status** — a facility can be fully permitted and still be subject to a §7003 action if its waste handling presents the requisite endangerment.
- Applies to **past and present** waste handlers, including generators who no longer have any ongoing operational control (this reaches inactive and abandoned sites).
- EPA can seek injunctive relief compelling cleanup, monitoring, or other response actions; it is not primarily a penalty mechanism, though associated penalties can attach for noncompliance with resulting orders.
- No requirement to prove actual harm has occurred — the standard is prospective and precautionary.

#### The "May Present an Imminent and Substantial Endangerment" Standard

Courts have construed this phrase expansively:

- **"May present"** — does not require certainty; a *risk* or *potential* of endangerment suffices. This is a lower evidentiary bar than proving actual, ongoing contamination.
- **"Imminent"** — refers to the nature of the *risk*, not the timing of the *harm*. A hazard can be "imminent" even if the harm it might cause is years away, so long as the risk-creating conditions currently exist. This interpretation flows from cases like *United States v. Price* (3d Cir. 1982) and is now well-settled.
- **"Substantial"** — endangerment must be significant, though not necessarily catastrophic; courts weigh the severity, gravity, and probability of harm cumulatively rather than requiring a single dispositive threshold.
- **"Endangerment to health or the environment"** — construed broadly, covering groundwater contamination risk, exposure pathways, and ecological harm, not merely direct human injury.

**Illustrative Case: *United States v. Vertac Chemical Corp.***

This case (dioxin-contaminated site in Arkansas) exemplifies how courts have applied §7003 (in conjunction with CERCLA) to compel cleanup at contaminated industrial sites based on contamination risk rather than proven present injury to a specific individual.

#### Relationship to CERCLA §106

RCRA §7003 is the structural precursor to CERCLA §106, which uses nearly identical "imminent and substantial endangerment" language. The two statutes are often invoked together, with:

| Feature | RCRA §7003 | CERCLA §106 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Waste scope | Solid & hazardous waste (RCRA-defined) | "Hazardous substances" (broader, CERCLA-defined) |
| Petroleum exclusion | Generally excluded (RCRA petroleum exclusion applies) | Also excluded, but CERCLA's substance list is broader otherwise |
| Primary remedy sought | Injunctive relief | Injunctive relief; also underlies unilateral administrative orders |
| Liability standard reached | Contributors to waste handling | PRPs (owners, operators, arrangers, transporters) |

#### Procedural Mechanics

1. EPA (via DOJ) files a civil action in federal district court, or issues an administrative order under §7003(a).
2. Because §7003 endangerment orders are prospective and equitable, courts apply traditional injunction standards loosely — likelihood of endangerment substitutes for the ordinary "irreparable harm" showing.
3. Non-compliance with an EPA administrative order issued under §7003 can trigger penalties under §7003(b) and separately under §3008(h) for corrective action violations.

### Citizen Suits (§7002)

#### **Key Points**

RCRA §7002 authorizes "any person" to commence a civil action in two distinct categories:

**§7002(a)(1)(A) — Against Violators**

- Against any person (including the U.S. and other governmental instrumentalities) alleged to be in violation of any RCRA permit, standard, regulation, condition, requirement, prohibition, or order that has become effective.
- This is a **compliance-forcing** suit — it requires an actual violation of an ascertainable legal requirement, not merely a risk.

**§7002(a)(1)(B) — "Endangerment" Citizen Suits**

- Against any person, including past and present generators, transporters, and owners/operators of treatment, storage, or disposal facilities, who has contributed or is contributing to handling of solid or hazardous waste that **may present an imminent and substantial endangerment to health or the environment.**
- This closely mirrors §7003's imminent hazard standard, effectively giving citizens a private right of action parallel to EPA's own authority. This provision was central to *Meghrig v. KFC Western, Inc.*, 516 U.S. 479 (1996).

**§7002(a)(2) — Against EPA**

- Against the EPA Administrator for failure to perform any non-discretionary duty under RCRA (e.g., failure to promulgate required regulations by statutory deadline).

#### Notice Requirements (Jurisdictional Prerequisites)

Citizen suits cannot proceed to court immediately — RCRA imposes mandatory pre-suit notice periods, and courts treat these as jurisdictional (not merely procedural), meaning failure to comply results in dismissal:

- **§7002(a)(1)(A) violator suits**: 60-day notice to EPA, the state, and the alleged violator before filing.
- **§7002(a)(1)(B) endangerment suits**: **90-day** notice to EPA, the state, and the alleged endangerer.
- **§7002(a)(2) suits against EPA**: 60-day notice.

**Diligent Prosecution Bar**: A citizen suit under (a)(1)(A) is barred if EPA or the state is already diligently prosecuting a civil or criminal action to require compliance. No equivalent bar generally applies to (a)(1)(B) endangerment suits in the same way, though courts assess overlap carefully.

#### **Example**

A community group discovers that a shuttered manufacturing facility's soil and groundwater show elevated solvent concentrations migrating toward a residential well field. No RCRA permit violation is documented because the facility ceased regulated activity years ago and is not currently subject to an active permit condition.

- The group **cannot** proceed under §7002(a)(1)(A) because there is no current "violation" of an operative permit/standard to point to.
- The group **can** proceed under §7002(a)(1)(B), alleging the former owner "contributed to" handling of hazardous waste that "may present an imminent and substantial endangerment," provided it serves the mandatory 90-day notice on EPA, the state, and the responsible party, and no government enforcement action addressing the same relief is underway.

#### Remedies Available

Courts may:

- Order compliance with the applicable requirement (§7002(a)(1)(A))
- Order the responsible party to take **action** — cleanup, monitoring, or other response measures — mirroring §7003 relief (§7002(a)(1)(B))
- Impose civil penalties under §3008 (payable to the U.S. Treasury, not the plaintiff)
- Award **litigation costs, including reasonable attorney and expert witness fees**, to a "substantially prevailing" party (§7002(e))

**Critical limitation — *Meghrig v. KFC Western, Inc.* (1996):** The Supreme Court held that §7002(a)(1)(B) does **not** authorize recovery of past cleanup costs already incurred by a private party. The remedy is **prospective injunctive relief** compelling future abatement action, not cost recovery or restitution for remediation already completed. This sharply distinguishes RCRA citizen suits from CERCLA's cost-recovery framework under §107, and plaintiffs seeking reimbursement for completed cleanup must look to CERCLA instead.

#### **[Inference]** Practical Strategic Implications

Because *Meghrig* forecloses cost recovery, litigants commonly plead RCRA §7002(a)(1)(B) and CERCLA §107/§113 claims together: RCRA to compel any *ongoing or future* corrective action, and CERCLA to recover costs already spent. This pairing is standard practice among environmental litigators, though the appropriateness of combining claims depends on case-specific facts.

| Diligent prosecution bar | N/A | Generally does not bar this claim even if EPA acts, though overlapping relief may affect standing/mootness |
| Remedy | Injunctive; administrative order | Injunctive (compel action); no cost recovery (*Meghrig*) |
| Penalties | Yes, for noncompliance with orders | Civil penalties possible, paid to U.S. Treasury |

### Standing Requirements for Citizen Suits

Beyond the RCRA-specific notice rules, plaintiffs must satisfy Article III standing:

1. **Injury in fact** — concrete and particularized, actual or imminent (courts have accepted aesthetic, recreational, and health-risk-based injuries, e.g., reduced use/enjoyment of property near a contaminated site).
2. **Causation** — the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's waste handling.
3. **Redressability** — a favorable judicial decision (compelling cleanup or compliance) would redress the injury.

*Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), though a Clean Water Act case, is the controlling framework courts apply to environmental citizen-suit standing generally, including RCRA suits — establishing that reasonable concern about environmental effects on a plaintiff's use and enjoyment of an area, even absent proven scientific harm, can satisfy injury-in-fact.

### Interaction with State-Authorized Programs

Most states operate EPA-authorized RCRA programs (§3006). Citizen suits and imminent hazard actions remain available even in authorized states — federal §7002 and §7003 are not displaced by state program authorization, since RCRA preserves federal enforcement and citizen-suit rights as a backstop (§7002(a) applies "notwithstanding" the existence of a state program, though a diligent state prosecution can still bar the compliance-suit branch under (a)(1)(A)).
